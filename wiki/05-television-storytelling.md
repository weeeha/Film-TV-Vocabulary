# 05 · Television storytelling

[Wiki home](../README.md) · [Alphabetical index](../INDEX.md)

Television stories operate on several scales at once. An episode can solve an immediate problem, change a relationship, and complicate a season-long question. A series needs a reason to continue, while each installment needs a reason to exist in its particular form.

The terms here describe creative organization rather than mandatory broadcast lengths, act counts, or platform rules. A streaming episode may use act breaks; a broadcast episode may remain highly serialized. Document the requirements of the actual production separately.

## Continuity models

### Episodic storytelling
<!-- term-id: television-storytelling.episodic-storytelling -->

**Definition.** Storytelling in which individual installments provide substantial local completeness, often introducing and resolving their own principal problems. Returning relationships and remembered events can still develop across episodes; episodic does not necessarily mean that everything resets or that viewing order never matters.

**Use.** Offer satisfying entry points while varying a familiar situation.

**Example.** Each episode follows a repair crew solving one household emergency, with enough context to understand the problem without watching earlier installments.

### Serialized storytelling
<!-- term-id: television-storytelling.serialized-storytelling -->

**Definition.** Storytelling in which significant action and unresolved questions continue across installments, making prior developments important to understanding later ones. Serialization can concern plot, relationships, or both; it does not require every episode to end on immediate physical danger.

**Use.** Build cumulative consequences and sustained change beyond one episode's boundaries.

**Example.** The crew's investigation of forged inspection reports unfolds over eight episodes, with each discovery changing the meaning of the last.

### Hybrid episodic-serialized storytelling
<!-- term-id: television-storytelling.hybrid-episodic-serialized-storytelling -->

**Definition.** An arrangement that combines meaningful local episode stories with continuing developments across the series. The balance may vary by episode or strand; the important question is which problems close, which relationships change, and what knowledge the next installment assumes.

**Use.** Combine accessibility with cumulative investment.

**Example.** A weekly repair case is resolved, but the parts used expose another connection in the season's corruption inquiry and deepen a rivalry within the crew.

## Scales of development

### Series engine
<!-- term-id: television-storytelling.series-engine -->

**Definition.** The repeatable arrangement of characters, circumstances, and conflicts that can generate further installments with meaningful variation. A setting or profession alone is not a complete engine; the design must explain why new problems arise and why these people confront them.

**Use.** Test whether the premise sustains more than its initial episode.

**Example.** A city repair team must solve dangerous failures while negotiating between residents' needs, political promises, and a parts supply controlled by competing districts.

### Season arc
<!-- term-id: television-storytelling.season-arc -->

**Definition.** A substantial progression developed across a designated season, such as an investigation, campaign, relationship change, or institutional crisis. It may reach closure in the finale while leaving series-level possibilities open; season boundaries can be practical as well as dramatic.

**Use.** Give accumulating episodes a coherent developing question and destination.

**Example.** Over ten episodes, the crew works toward exposing the source of counterfeit safety equipment before the annual city inspection.

### Episode arc
<!-- term-id: television-storytelling.episode-arc -->

**Definition.** The local development of an installment from its initial conditions through complications to an altered situation. An episode arc can close a problem or produce a consequential next stage; being serialized does not eliminate the value of a shaped installment.

**Use.** Identify what specifically changes during the audience's current viewing unit.

**Example.** The crew arrives to restore a stalled elevator and ends by persuading the building's tenants to organize their own inspection committee.

### Recurring character arc
<!-- term-id: television-storytelling.recurring-character-arc -->

**Definition.** A character's sustained or periodically revisited development across multiple appearances. The progression can be gradual, discontinuous, or steadfast under changing pressure; recurrence does not require equal screen time in every episode or a complete transformation each season.

**Use.** Track how accumulated experience affects choices beyond a single case.

**Example.** A cautious dispatcher begins by enforcing every rule and gradually learns when taking responsibility requires authorizing an exception she can publicly defend.

## Story strands within an episode

### A-story
<!-- term-id: television-storytelling.a-story -->

**Definition.** The principal story strand within a given episode or planning context, usually carrying its main problem and greatest dramatic emphasis. The label expresses local priority rather than moral importance, and different episodes may assign the A-story to different characters.

**Use.** Establish the installment's central progression when balancing several strands.

**Example.** The episode's A-story follows a crew trying to restore power to a building before its backup system fails.

### B-story
<!-- term-id: television-storytelling.b-story -->

**Definition.** A secondary developed strand that complements, complicates, contrasts with, or intersects the principal story. It may concern relationships or external action; conventions that always identify the B-story with a romance or theme belong to particular frameworks, not all television.

**Use.** Create another source of movement and a meaningful relationship to the A-story.

**Example.** While the crew repairs the generator, the dispatcher negotiates with a supplier whose missing paperwork could explain why it failed.

### C-story
<!-- term-id: television-storytelling.c-story -->

**Definition.** A third strand with less emphasis or space than the episode's principal two, when that distinction is useful to the production. A C-story can still develop a clear local change; it is not automatically expendable or restricted to jokes.

**Use.** Maintain a smaller relationship, question, or setup without overcrowding the main action.

**Example.** An apprentice tries to return a borrowed tool and discovers that its owner quietly left the team weeks ago.

### Runner
<!-- term-id: television-storytelling.runner -->

**Definition.** A brief recurring element or miniature thread distributed through an episode or series, often comic but not necessarily so. A runner usually receives less development than a full subplot and gains effect through repetition, variation, or a final payoff.

**Use.** Create connective rhythm or a small accumulating observation between larger scenes.

**Example.** Throughout the episode, different residents hand the crew a mysterious replacement knob; the final scene reveals an entire room of matching doors.

## Beginnings, interruptions, and endings

### Pilot
<!-- term-id: television-storytelling.pilot -->

**Definition.** An episode or production made to introduce and demonstrate a proposed series, often establishing its world, characters, tone, and continuing possibilities. A pilot may be revised, remain unaired, or differ from the eventual premiere; not every series begins with a separately produced pilot.

**Use.** Show both an engaging installment and the promise of future stories.

**Example.** A new dispatcher joins the repair crew during a blackout, learning the team's methods while uncovering the first sign of systemic equipment fraud.

### Cold open
<!-- term-id: television-storytelling.cold-open -->

**Definition.** Opening dramatic material presented before the usual title sequence or introductory material. It can be a self-contained scene, an immediate problem, or a temporally displaced event; it need not explain the main cast before attracting attention.

**Use.** Establish a question, rhythm, perspective, or tonal promise quickly.

**Example.** An empty office receives a call from its own disconnected emergency phone; the title sequence begins before anyone answers.

### Teaser
<!-- term-id: television-storytelling.teaser -->

**Definition.** An opening segment designed to create interest in the episode's coming action. In many television scripts teaser and cold open overlap or name the same section; teaser also has a separate promotional meaning, which should not be confused with this script usage.

**Use.** Establish an unresolved hook and identify its later relationship to the episode.

**Example.** A worker finds a perfectly new component inside a machine that has been sealed for thirty years.

### Act break
<!-- term-id: television-storytelling.act-break -->

**Definition.** A boundary between labeled acts, often positioned to support a broadcast interruption or a significant dramatic transition. A script may use act breaks without actual commercials, and production-specific act divisions should be distinguished from analytical three-act or five-act models.

**Use.** Shape an interruption around a changed question or situation.

**Example.** An act ends when the repaired elevator starts moving downward despite the crew having physically disconnected its power supply.

### Cliffhanger
<!-- term-id: television-storytelling.cliffhanger -->

**Definition.** An ending or interruption that leaves a pressing outcome unresolved at a moment of significant uncertainty. It can concern a revelation, relationship, or choice as well as danger; withholding an arbitrary final sentence does not necessarily create a meaningful cliffhanger.

**Use.** Carry a specific unresolved concern across a break or installment boundary.

**Example.** The dispatcher opens the incriminating report and recognizes the signature as her own, though she has no memory of writing it.

### Tag
<!-- term-id: television-storytelling.tag -->

**Definition.** A brief concluding scene or segment following the episode's main resolution or final principal act. A tag may deliver a comic payoff, emotional afterthought, or small continuing development; its position relative to credits depends on the show's convention.

**Use.** Leave a precise final impression without reopening every resolved problem.

**Example.** After a tense repair succeeds, the crew returns to its office and finds that the coffee machine has finally stopped working.

### Finale
<!-- term-id: television-storytelling.finale -->

**Definition.** An installment positioned as the conclusion of a season, series, or other defined run. A finale can resolve major arcs, transform the ongoing premise, or leave chosen questions open; a season finale and a series finale carry different expectations of continuation.

**Use.** Decide which scales of story receive closure and which remain active.

**Example.** The season finale exposes the equipment fraud but leaves the crew responsible for rebuilding the agency that previously employed them.

## Episode designs

### Bottle episode
<!-- term-id: television-storytelling.bottle-episode -->

**Definition.** An episode substantially concentrated in a limited set of locations, often using a small portion of the cast and existing resources. The term has production associations with economy, but a contained episode is not automatically cheap, simple, or made only to save money.

**Use.** Intensify relationships by limiting escape routes and outside intervention.

**Example.** The crew spends an entire episode locked in its dispatch room, deciding who altered a report while calls for help continue arriving.

### Standalone episode
<!-- term-id: television-storytelling.standalone-episode -->

**Definition.** An installment whose principal story can be understood and experienced with relatively little dependence on surrounding episodes. It may still acknowledge continuity or develop a recurring character; standalone does not necessarily mean noncanonical or disconnected from the series' concerns.

**Use.** Create an accessible entry point or explore a focused situation.

**Example.** A visiting night worker helps the crew locate a trapped pet, and the story reaches a complete emotional outcome within that episode.

### Two-part episode
<!-- term-id: television-storytelling.two-part-episode -->

**Definition.** A story explicitly distributed across two linked installments, usually with a meaningful interruption and continuation. It differs from ordinary serialization when the production treats the pair as a particular unit, though the first part still benefits from its own dramatic progression.

**Use.** Give a larger event additional space while shaping the transition between viewings.

**Example.** Part one follows a failed evacuation; part two begins with the crew adapting its rescue plan to the neighborhood now cut off by water.

### Anthology episode
<!-- term-id: television-storytelling.anthology-episode -->

**Definition.** An installment presenting a substantially distinct story within an anthology's shared umbrella. Characters, setting, period, or style may change, while a theme or premise supplies connection; this episode-level meaning differs from anthologies that change stories only between seasons.

**Use.** Explore the umbrella concept through a self-contained variation.

**Example.** In a series about impossible messages, one episode follows a lighthouse keeper receiving tomorrow's weather reports in a stranger's handwriting.

## Continuity behavior

### Procedural reset
<!-- term-id: television-storytelling.procedural-reset -->

**Definition.** A return toward the series' familiar operational starting conditions after a case or problem closes. Reset can preserve the workplace, methods, and recurring roles while allowing some emotional or historical continuity; it need not erase everything that happened.

**Use.** Sustain a repeatable process without requiring each installment to rebuild its premise.

**Example.** The inspection is completed and the crew returns to dispatch, ready for a new case, although one member now distrusts the supervisor.

### Accumulating consequences
<!-- term-id: television-storytelling.accumulating-consequences -->

**Definition.** Effects of earlier events that persist and alter later situations, resources, relationships, or choices. Accumulation can be subtle or extensive and can exist within episodic storytelling; mentioning a past event is less significant than letting it change present possibilities.

**Use.** Make continuity consequential rather than merely referential.

**Example.** An unauthorized repair saves residents in episode two but leaves the crew uninsured during a more dangerous operation in episode six.

### Recurring conflict
<!-- term-id: television-storytelling.recurring-conflict -->

**Definition.** A tension that repeatedly generates new situations because its underlying incompatibility remains active. It may evolve in form and stakes; replaying identical arguments without changed context is repetition, but not necessarily useful development of the conflict.

**Use.** Give the series durable friction while varying tactics and consequences.

**Example.** A supervisor prioritizes documented safety while a field worker prioritizes immediate rescue, and each emergency changes which position appears more defensible.

## Practical distinctions

Track closure separately at episode, season, and series levels. An episode can resolve its A-story, leave a B-story open, and complete one step of a character arc. “Serialized” is therefore most useful when attached to a particular strand and its dependencies.

Cold open describes position; teaser emphasizes an opening hook, though many productions use the labels interchangeably. Scriptnotes discusses cold opens as flexible tools involving perspective and time, reinforcing the need to describe their specific function. [The Power of the Cold Open](https://johnaugust.com/2024/the-power-of-the-cold-open)

A pilot must demonstrate a series' potential, but its particular production status varies. BBC Maestro's pilot guidance discusses introducing premise, character, and tone through an initial episode; it is a teaching approach, not a universal commissioning rule. [How to write a TV pilot](https://www.bbcmaestro.com/blog/how-to-write-a-tv-pilot)
