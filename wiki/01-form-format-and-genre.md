# 01 · Form, format, and genre

[Wiki home](../README.md) · [Alphabetical index](../INDEX.md)

These terms answer different questions: what kind of work is this, how is it made, how is it released, what conventions does it use, and what attitude does it take? The categories are editable working distinctions, not a universal classification system. The BFI's own discussion of cataloguing genres describes the difficulty of imposing clean boundaries on overlapping practices. [BFI: Genres—where to draw the line?](https://www.bfi.org.uk/features/genres-where-draw-line)

Every example below is an invented project or scene. A work can use several genres and change tone without changing its medium or release format.

## The classification axes

### Form
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.form -->

**Definition.** The broad organizing approach a work takes toward representation, such as invented storytelling, nonfiction inquiry, or experimentation with images and sound. Here, form is a practical classification axis; elsewhere the word may mean a work's entire artistic organization.

**Use.** Establish the relationship between the work and its material before assigning genre labels.

**Example.** A film about a vanished factory could be a fictional mystery, a documentary investigation, or an abstract study of its machinery.

### Medium
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.medium -->

**Definition.** The means through which moving images are created and presented. This wiki uses live action, animation, and mixed media as useful production distinctions, while acknowledging that film, television, and digital video are also called media in other contexts.

**Use.** Describe the image-making approach independently of subject matter or intended audience.

**Example.** An animated courtroom drama remains a drama; drawing its characters does not automatically make it a children's comedy.

### Release format
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.release-format -->

**Definition.** The unit and continuing arrangement in which audiences receive a work: a short, feature, episode, or particular type of series. Format shapes duration, repetition, and closure, but does not determine genre, artistic ambition, or distribution platform by itself.

**Use.** Decide how much story belongs in one viewing unit and what must continue beyond it.

**Example.** A harbor investigation becomes either one feature or six episodes, each revealing another institution's involvement.

### Genre
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.genre -->

**Definition.** A recognizable grouping of conventions involving situations, imagery, conflicts, narrative expectations, and audience traditions. Genres develop historically and overlap; they are neither ingredient checklists nor promises that every viewer will respond identically.

**Use.** Identify expectations to satisfy, reshape, combine, or deliberately frustrate.

**Example.** A missing-person mystery encourages viewers to examine clues, while its horror elements make discovering the truth itself threatening.

### Genre combinations
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.genre-combinations -->

**Definition.** Deliberate mixtures of conventions associated with two or more genres. A combination can be balanced or dominated by one genre; merely including a joke or a romantic scene does not necessarily establish a second governing genre.

**Use.** Specify which genre drives the main conflict and which changes its texture or expectations.

**Example.** A crime thriller may organize its plot around a robbery while a family drama supplies the characters' deepest consequences.

### Tone
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.tone -->

**Definition.** The work's felt attitude toward its subjects and events, expressed through performance, framing, rhythm, dialogue, and sound. Tone can vary between scenes and coexist with conflicting emotions; it is distinct from genre and from any guaranteed audience reaction.

**Use.** Align creative choices around how events should be treated.

**Example.** A failed wedding can be presented with tender sincerity, biting satire, or quiet melancholy while retaining the same basic plot.

## Forms

### Fiction
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.fiction -->

**Definition.** Storytelling that presents invented or substantially dramatized people, events, and situations within a constructed story world. Fiction may incorporate historical facts or personal experience, but its scenes are not offered simply as direct records of actuality.

**Use.** Explore possibilities, consequences, and perspectives through designed characters and events.

**Example.** An invented ferry captain hides a passenger whose arrival could expose the island council's secret agreement.

### Documentary
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.documentary -->

**Definition.** A nonfiction work that makes claims about the historical or contemporary world through recorded material and editorial construction. Documentary can use observation, interviews, archives, animation, and disclosed reconstruction; selection and framing remain part of its authorship.

**Use.** Investigate, witness, explain, or interpret actual subjects with clear evidence and attribution.

**Example.** A film follows real ferry workers through a service closure and distinguishes their recollections from recorded council proceedings.

### Experimental film
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.experimental-film -->

**Definition.** A practice that investigates or challenges conventions of moving images, sound, narrative, perception, or exhibition. Experimentation may involve abstraction, unusual duration, material processes, or alternative viewing arrangements; absence of a conventional plot alone is not its complete definition.

**Use.** Make the organization and experience of audiovisual material central to the work.

**Example.** Repeated images of a harbor light gradually lose synchronization with recordings of the tide.

### Hybrid documentary-fiction
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.hybrid-documentary-fiction -->

**Definition.** A work combining nonfiction claims or real participants with invented, staged, or fictionalized elements. The mixture can take many forms; its ethical and interpretive meaning depends on what is constructed and how that construction is communicated.

**Use.** Explore lived experience through methods that a purely observational record would not supply.

**Example.** Former factory workers perform imagined conversations in the abandoned building, with the staged status explicitly identified.

## Image-making media

### Live action
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.live-action -->

**Definition.** Moving images principally based on photographing physical performers, objects, or environments in front of a camera. Live action can contain extensive visual effects or animation, and the term does not mean events were broadcast live or recorded without manipulation.

**Use.** Describe a production's photographic basis and its dependence on physical staging.

**Example.** Actors perform a kitchen argument on a built set while a digital storm replaces the view outside.

### Animation
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.animation -->

**Definition.** Moving images created by designing successive visual states, including drawings, computer-generated imagery, and photographed arrangements of objects. Animation encompasses abstract, documentary, comic, and dramatic work; it describes an image-making practice rather than a single genre or audience.

**Use.** Control movement, transformation, and visual worlds beyond straightforward photographic recording.

**Example.** A painted character's apartment expands whenever she exaggerates a memory, making her storytelling visibly reshape space.

### Mixed media
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.mixed-media -->

**Definition.** An approach that combines distinct image-making materials or processes within one work, such as photography, drawing, collage, stop motion, and computer animation. The combination may be seamless or visibly discontinuous; ordinary effects work does not always merit this label.

**Use.** Give different experiences, worlds, or sources their own visual language.

**Example.** A live-action archivist enters paper-collage versions of the documents she is cataloguing.

## Release formats

### Feature
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.feature -->

**Definition.** A film treated as a substantial, usually self-contained viewing unit rather than a short or series episode. Exact runtime boundaries depend on the institution or context; feature status does not require theatrical release or a specific dramatic structure.

**Use.** Plan a sustained experience with enough development and closure for one principal presentation.

**Example.** A ferry captain's single disastrous crossing develops into a complete ninety-minute story.

### Short
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.short -->

**Definition.** A film designed as a comparatively brief viewing unit. Runtime thresholds vary: the Academy's Student Academy Awards currently permit no more than forty minutes including credits, an institutional rule rather than a universal artistic boundary. [Academy application guidance](https://www.oscars.org/saa/apply)

**Use.** Concentrate on a bounded event, formal experiment, relationship shift, or compact argument.

**Example.** An eight-minute film follows two strangers deciding who should receive the last seat on a departing boat.

### Television episode
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.television-episode -->

**Definition.** An individual installment of a television or streaming program, identified as part of a larger series organization. An episode may tell a complete story, advance an unfinished one, or do both; its duration need not match a fixed broadcast slot.

**Use.** Define the immediate viewing unit and its relationship to adjacent installments.

**Example.** One harbor-series episode resolves a missing cargo case while revealing a clue to the season's disappearance.

### Limited series
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.limited-series -->

**Definition.** A series planned around a bounded run and a substantially complete story. Marketing usage, commissioning arrangements, and awards eligibility may differ, so a project should state its intended episode count and closure rather than relying on the label alone.

**Use.** Develop complexity across installments while planning a defined destination.

**Example.** Five episodes reconstruct one evacuation from different participants' perspectives and conclude with the final inquiry.

### Continuing series
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.continuing-series -->

**Definition.** A series designed to sustain further installments through returning characters, situations, institutions, or conflicts. It may contain completed episodes and seasons while retaining a broader premise capable of generating new stories; continuation is an intention, not a guarantee of renewal.

**Use.** Identify what can repeatedly generate meaningful variation.

**Example.** A coastal repair crew encounters a different emergency each week while its members' loyalties develop across seasons.

### Anthology series
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.anthology-series -->

**Definition.** A series organized around separate stories connected by an umbrella concept, tone, creator, setting, or presentation. The story and principal characters may change each episode or each season; returning actors do not necessarily imply returning fictional characters.

**Use.** Explore a shared concern through contrasting casts and situations.

**Example.** Each season follows a different town facing the consequences of one impossible weather event.

## Genres

### Drama
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.drama -->

**Definition.** In genre usage, storytelling emphasizing consequential human situations, relationships, and emotional or social conflict. The word also means enacted storytelling more broadly, so calling a work a drama does not establish that it lacks humor, suspense, or stylization.

**Use.** Signal sustained attention to choices and their human consequences.

**Example.** Three siblings disagree over selling their mother's workshop while attempting to keep its final commission alive.

### Comedy
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.comedy -->

**Definition.** Storytelling organized substantially around humor, comic situations, incongruity, or playful disruption. Comedy can contain pain, criticism, and serious consequences; comic intention does not guarantee laughter, and a happy ending alone does not define the modern screen genre.

**Use.** Explore tensions through timing, reversal, embarrassment, exaggeration, and conflicting perspectives.

**Example.** Two rival inspectors repeatedly certify each other's office as unsafe and must share the only compliant desk.

### Thriller
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.thriller -->

**Definition.** A genre that makes sustained danger, pressure, uncertainty, and urgent action central to its narrative experience. Threat may be physical, political, psychological, or technological; thrills depend on arrangement and perspective rather than simply on a high body count.

**Use.** Organize information and choices around narrowing options and escalating exposure.

**Example.** A translator discovers that correcting one word in a live negotiation will expose the person threatening her family.

### Horror
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.horror -->

**Definition.** A genre that engages with threatening, disturbing, uncanny, or repellent experiences, often confronting vulnerability and violated boundaries. Its threats may be supernatural or ordinary, visible or suggested; gore and sudden scares are optional techniques rather than defining requirements.

**Use.** Explore fear and taboo through the rules, proximity, and implications of a threat.

**Example.** A tenant hears her own voice pleading from behind a wall that was sealed decades earlier.

### Science fiction
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.science-fiction -->

**Definition.** Speculative storytelling whose imagined conditions are framed through science, technology, or materially reasoned changes to the world. Scientific plausibility varies widely, but the narrative treats its speculation as part of an intelligible system rather than simply unexplained enchantment.

**Use.** Explore the consequences of a changed capability, discovery, environment, or social arrangement.

**Example.** A public memory archive lets citizens retrieve experiences, but every retrieval permanently changes another person's record.

### Fantasy
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.fantasy -->

**Definition.** Speculative storytelling in which impossible beings, powers, or worlds are accepted through magical, mythical, or otherwise extraordinary premises. Fantasy can use rigorous internal rules and contemporary settings; castles, quests, and medieval imagery are common possibilities rather than requirements.

**Use.** Externalize desires, obligations, and conflicts through a world with extraordinary conditions.

**Example.** An apartment building chooses a new caretaker by making every broken object speak its owner's secret.

### Romance
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.romance -->

**Definition.** A genre in which the formation, testing, or transformation of a romantic relationship provides the principal narrative concern. Screen usage is broad, and conventions about optimistic endings differ across markets and adjacent literary traditions; a romantic subplot alone does not establish the genre.

**Use.** Make mutual attraction and compatibility consequential organizing questions.

**Example.** Two translators fall in love while disagreeing about whether their shared language should ever be standardized.

### Mystery
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.mystery -->

**Definition.** A genre organized around an unresolved question and the process of interpreting clues, testimony, or concealed relationships. Mysteries often concern crimes but can investigate identity, history, or unexplained phenomena; the crucial engine is discovering what is hidden.

**Use.** Shape what audiences know, suspect, reconsider, and eventually understand.

**Example.** Every house on an island receives a letter from someone listed as having died before the settlement existed.

### Crime
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.crime -->

**Definition.** A genre centered on criminal acts, illicit systems, enforcement, or their social consequences. It can follow offenders, investigators, victims, or communities and need not endorse the law's legitimacy or organize itself around solving a puzzle.

**Use.** Examine risk, loyalty, power, and responsibility through conflicts around prohibited conduct.

**Example.** A warehouse crew begins diverting medicine and discovers that its customers include the officials investigating the thefts.

### Action
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.action -->

**Definition.** A genre in which physical confrontation, movement, danger, and kinetic problem-solving carry substantial narrative weight. Action sequences work through goals, space, changing advantage, and consequences; speed or spectacle alone does not explain their dramatic function.

**Use.** Make choices legible through bodily action and material obstacles.

**Example.** A courier must carry a fragile generator across a collapsing pedestrian bridge while others try to seize it.

### Adventure
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.adventure -->

**Definition.** A genre emphasizing exploration, risk, unfamiliar environments, and consequential journeys. Adventure can include action, discovery, wonder, and companionship without requiring combat; the changing encounters of the journey often matter as much as reaching its destination.

**Use.** Organize varied situations around movement into the unknown.

**Example.** A cartographer and a runaway apprentice follow a river that appears on no map and changes course every dawn.

### Western
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.western -->

**Definition.** A genre associated with contested frontiers, settlement, mobility, violence, land, and competing forms of authority, historically linked to representations of the American West. Its conventions can be adapted elsewhere, but historical representation and whose perspective defines the frontier matter.

**Use.** Explore conflicts over territory, belonging, law, and imposed order.

**Example.** A surveyor hired to mark a new railway route encounters residents whose claims the official map deliberately omits.

### Musical
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.musical -->

**Definition.** A genre in which musical numbers substantially organize storytelling, characterization, or spectacle. Characters may sing within realistic performance settings or enter expressive conventions where song replaces ordinary speech; a prominent instrumental score alone does not make a musical.

**Use.** Let rhythm, lyrics, movement, and musical repetition carry dramatic information.

**Example.** During a town meeting, competing proposals become interlocking songs until one person's silence changes the balance.

### Historical film
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.historical-film -->

**Definition.** A film whose significant setting or subject belongs to an earlier period and whose dramatic conditions depend on that history. It can depict documented people or invented ones, and it can combine with other genres; period décor alone does not establish historical accuracy.

**Use.** Connect individual choices to specific institutions, technologies, and events.

**Example.** An invented typesetter navigates a documented printing strike, with factual context distinguished from the fictional plot.

## Common genre combinations

### Romantic comedy
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.romantic-comedy -->

**Definition.** A combination that places a romantic relationship at the center while using comic conflict, timing, and incongruity as substantial storytelling principles. The comedy should affect how the relationship develops rather than merely interrupting a separate love story with jokes.

**Use.** Explore attraction through mistaken assumptions, incompatible habits, and changing self-understanding.

**Example.** Two anonymous advice columnists fall for each other while publicly attacking each other's solutions to the same reader's problem.

### Science-fiction horror
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.science-fiction-horror -->

**Definition.** A combination in which speculative science, technology, or environments generate or reshape a horror threat. The science-fiction premise changes what can happen, while horror organizes the danger's disturbing implications; scientific explanation need not make the threat feel controllable.

**Use.** Connect fear to the consequences of a discovery or designed system.

**Example.** A habitat's repair organisms begin treating the sleeping residents as structural damage that must be removed.

### Crime thriller
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.crime-thriller -->

**Definition.** A combination that uses criminal activity or investigation as its subject while organizing events around urgent danger, pressure, and unstable advantage. Its central pleasure may be anticipating what happens next rather than solving an initially unknown culprit's identity.

**Use.** Turn criminal decisions into escalating exposure and time-sensitive choices.

**Example.** An accountant must replace stolen ledgers before an audit, unaware that the buyer intends to auction them live that evening.

### Tragicomedy
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.tragicomedy -->

**Definition.** A work in which comic and tragic elements meaningfully coexist, often making humor inseparable from loss, failure, or contradiction. It need not alternate mechanically between funny and sad scenes, and the ending may resist a clean emotional classification.

**Use.** Preserve the awkwardness and mixed feelings within serious circumstances.

**Example.** Estranged siblings rehearse a dignified memorial while their father's elaborate mechanical inventions repeatedly disrupt every solemn gesture.

## Tonal vocabulary

### Sincere
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.sincere -->

**Definition.** A tone that treats expressed feelings, commitments, and concerns with apparent directness and emotional investment. Sincerity does not require simplicity or an absence of humor; it describes how the work presents a feeling rather than proving a character is truthful.

**Use.** Allow vulnerability or conviction to stand without immediately undercutting it.

**Example.** After a clumsy apology, the camera stays with the listener instead of cutting to a joke about the speaker.

### Ironic
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.ironic -->

**Definition.** A tone shaped by a perceivable gap between appearance and reality, stated meaning and implied meaning, or expectation and outcome. Ironic treatment can be affectionate, bitter, or detached, and should be distinguished from dramatic irony as an information arrangement.

**Use.** Encourage audiences to notice a second meaning beneath the apparent presentation.

**Example.** A manager celebrates the company's new listening policy while an assistant silently removes every employee suggestion box.

### Satirical
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.satirical -->

**Definition.** A tone using humor, exaggeration, incongruity, or ridicule to expose and criticize a target such as an institution, ideology, or social practice. Satire requires an intelligible critical relationship to its target; absurd events alone do not establish that relationship.

**Use.** Make contradictions in systems visible through dramatic exaggeration.

**Example.** A city creates an emergency committee whose first binding rule prohibits acknowledging emergencies without a six-week review.

### Tragic
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.tragic -->

**Definition.** A tone that treats suffering, irreversible loss, or destructive conflict with weight and gravity. It can appear within many genres and does not automatically imply a classical tragedy, a morally flawed protagonist, or an ending in death.

**Use.** Emphasize the cost of incompatible commitments or consequences that cannot be repaired.

**Example.** A successful rescue leaves a captain knowing that the person who trusted her most will never return.

### Playful
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.playful -->

**Definition.** A tone of curiosity, experimentation, teasing, or lightness toward events and presentation. Playfulness can coexist with serious stakes; it often emerges from inventive rules, performance, or audiovisual patterns rather than from conventional jokes alone.

**Use.** Invite viewers to enjoy discovery and variation in how the work behaves.

**Example.** A detective's imagined explanations briefly rearrange the furniture before the room snaps back to its actual, inconvenient layout.

### Contemplative
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.contemplative -->

**Definition.** A tone that invites sustained attention, reflection, and awareness of duration or experience. It may use quiet, repetition, observation, or open questions, but long shots and sparse dialogue do not automatically produce contemplation for every audience.

**Use.** Give perceptions and unresolved feelings room to develop without immediate explanation.

**Example.** After receiving an offer to leave, a gardener completes the evening watering while distant trains pass beyond the fence.

### Absurd
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.absurd -->

**Definition.** A tone that foregrounds the irrationality, incompatibility, or instability of apparently meaningful rules and actions. Absurdity can be comic or unsettling; it differs from random events when its contradictions accumulate into a recognizable experience or argument.

**Use.** Make ordinary assumptions strange enough to examine.

**Example.** A traveler must prove that she has arrived before the station will allow her to leave the train.

### Ominous
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.ominous -->

**Definition.** A tone suggesting that harm or disruption is approaching, even when the immediate situation appears calm. It depends on context and implication; low music or dark lighting may contribute, but neither guarantees that viewers anticipate danger.

**Use.** Give ordinary details the pressure of a possible future threat.

**Example.** During a cheerful breakfast, every radio in the apartment quietly changes to the same unfamiliar station.

### Hopeful
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.hopeful -->

**Definition.** A tone that makes improvement, connection, or meaningful action feel possible despite uncertainty. Hopefulness does not require a resolved happy ending or the denial of loss; it can rest on a small credible possibility within difficult circumstances.

**Use.** Support forward movement by showing an attainable opening or renewed commitment.

**Example.** The workshop remains closed, but its former apprentices arrive carrying tools and ask where they can begin.

### Melancholic
<!-- term-id: form-format-and-genre.melancholic -->

**Definition.** A tone of lingering sadness, longing, or awareness of passing time, often quieter and less immediately catastrophic than tragic treatment. Melancholy can include affection, beauty, and pleasure; it describes an atmosphere rather than a clinical state.

**Use.** Hold loss and attachment together without forcing immediate resolution.

**Example.** A retired projectionist tests the theater's lamp one last time, watching dust move through the empty beam.

## Practical distinctions

An animated, melancholic science-fiction short has four compatible properties: medium, tone, genre, and release format. Documentary and fiction concern the work's relationship to actuality; live action and animation concern how images are made. An anthology describes how separate installments relate, while an episodic structure describes narrative organization.

Use genre labels as starting expectations and attach concrete evidence: a scene pattern, a conflict, a visual convention, or an intended experience. Record tone at scene level when the work changes attitude. Awards definitions are institution-specific; verify the actual year's rules before making eligibility decisions.
