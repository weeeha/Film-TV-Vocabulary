# 15 · Camera movement and support

[Wiki home](../README.md) · [Alphabetical index](../INDEX.md)

Describe camera movement through three separate decisions: where the camera travels, how it rotates or aims, and what supports it. Then describe timing and purpose. A lateral tracking shot might use a dolly, a handheld camera, a gimbal, or a vehicle mount; the path does not determine the support.

The examples below are hypothetical. A movement can guide attention or change spatial relationships, but it cannot guarantee an emotional response. Physical production plans also need feasibility checks; this glossary is not a rigging or vehicle-operation manual.

## Rotation and translation

### Pan
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.pan -->

**Definition.** Rotation of the camera left or right about a generally vertical axis. The camera's orientation changes; a pure pan does not translate the viewpoint sideways through the scene.

**Use.** Follow lateral action, compare adjacent areas, or reveal something outside the initial frame. A pan can be combined with translation and other rotations.

**Example.** From one tripod position, the camera pans from a departing bus to the passenger left behind.

### Tilt
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.tilt -->

**Definition.** Rotation of the camera upward or downward about a generally horizontal axis. Tilting changes viewing direction, whereas raising or lowering the whole camera changes its position.

**Use.** Follow vertical attention, describe height, or connect details at different elevations. Specify whether the support also moves during the shot.

**Example.** The camera tilts from a child's muddy boots to the immaculate portrait she is carrying overhead.

### Roll
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.roll -->

**Definition.** Rotation around the camera's viewing axis, making the image turn clockwise or counterclockwise. Roll changes the horizon's orientation without necessarily changing where the center of the frame points.

**Use.** Describe physical rotation, an unusual viewpoint, or a deliberate graphic transition. A fixed rolled position produces a canted framing.

**Example.** As a mechanic turns inside a circular access tunnel, the camera rolls to maintain alignment with her body.

### Dolly in
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.dolly-in -->

**Definition.** Physical camera movement toward a subject or chosen scene point. Push-in is a common alias. The name often describes the movement even when a wheeled dolly is not the support.

**Use.** Change viewpoint and progressively alter a subject's prominence or surrounding relationships. It differs optically from changing focal length at a fixed position.

**Example.** The camera advances toward a seated witness as she notices a familiar mark on the evidence bag.

### Dolly out
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.dolly-out -->

**Definition.** Physical camera movement away from a subject or chosen scene point. Pull-back or pull-out are common aliases, although local terminology should be confirmed when another support is used.

**Use.** Reveal surrounding space, redistribute attention, or change the audience's spatial relation to an action. A backward move can also maintain framing on an approaching subject.

**Example.** The camera withdraws from a carefully set table until the empty warehouse around it becomes visible.

### Lateral tracking
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.lateral-tracking -->

**Definition.** Camera translation sideways relative to a defined scene axis or subject relationship. Trucking and side tracking are common related names; the camera can aim straight ahead or rotate while traveling.

**Use.** Follow parallel action, compare adjacent spaces, or create depth changes through parallax. Identify the reference direction because “left” can mean screen-left or world-left.

**Example.** The camera travels beside a row of train windows, revealing different passengers in successive compartments.

### Pedestal up
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.pedestal-up -->

**Definition.** Raising the camera vertically while its viewing direction may remain fixed. Pedestal describes position change, not the upward rotation of a tilt; boom up is sometimes used for related support motion.

**Use.** See over an obstruction or change the relationship between foreground and background. Confirm whether the actual support produces a vertical line or a slight arc.

**Example.** The camera rises above stacked crates to reveal a person hiding behind them.

### Pedestal down
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.pedestal-down -->

**Definition.** Lowering the camera vertically, independently of whether it also changes orientation. It differs from tilting down because the viewpoint itself descends through space.

**Use.** Move from one height relationship to another, pass beneath an obstruction, or meet a seated subject's level. Record any simultaneous tilt separately.

**Example.** As an adult kneels to speak to a child, the camera descends and keeps both faces at a similar image height.

### Crane movement
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.crane-movement -->

**Definition.** Camera travel produced by a crane or jib, potentially combining elevation, lateral reach, and sweeping arcs. A crane is a support mechanism, so its movement still requires a described path.

**Use.** Connect levels or reveal geography beyond ordinary standing camera positions. Different crane designs allow different trajectories; the label alone does not specify the result.

**Example.** A shot begins beside a ticket queue and rises over the booth to reveal the empty stadium beyond.

### Orbit
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.orbit -->

**Definition.** A path traveling around a subject or selected center, commonly while keeping the camera aimed toward it. An orbit may cover part of a circle or continue through a full revolution.

**Use.** Reveal changing background relationships while retaining a central subject. State radius, angular coverage, and orientation behavior where useful.

**Example.** The camera circles a stalled car until the hidden damage on its far side becomes visible.

### Arc
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.arc -->

**Definition.** A curved segment of camera travel. An arc may bend around a subject, but it does not require constant radius, a fixed center, or continuous inward-facing orientation.

**Use.** Move between viewpoints smoothly while controlling what passes in front of the lens. “Arc” and “orbit” overlap in everyday shot descriptions.

**Example.** The camera curves from behind a receptionist to a three-quarter view of the visitor approaching the desk.

### Follow
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.follow -->

**Definition.** Camera movement that accompanies a subject, commonly from behind or beside them. It describes the camera-subject relationship rather than a particular support, lens, or path shape.

**Use.** Let movement through an environment structure the audience's discovery. Decide how closely the camera maintains distance and how independently it looks around.

**Example.** The camera follows a night porter through service corridors, discovering each locked door when he reaches it.

### Lead
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.lead -->

**Definition.** Camera movement ahead of a moving subject, commonly traveling backward while looking toward them. Lead describes relative placement and accompaniment; it does not prescribe how the camera is supported.

**Use.** Preserve access to a moving performer's face while allowing the environment to unfold behind. The path and subject distance need coordination.

**Example.** The camera retreats ahead of two sisters as they walk toward an appointment neither wants to discuss.

## Support and stabilization

### Locked-off camera
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.locked-off-camera -->

**Definition.** A camera held at a fixed position and orientation for the intended duration of a shot. Lens focus or focal length could still change, so “locked-off” does not automatically freeze every image property.

**Use.** Let action enter, leave, and reorganize a stable frame, or support aligned repeated takes. Specify any permitted lens adjustments.

**Example.** One fixed view records a café opening, filling with customers, and emptying after an unexpected announcement.

### Handheld camera
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.handheld-camera -->

**Definition.** A camera substantially carried and guided by the operator's hands and body, without a support intended to isolate all bodily movement. Handheld operation can range from restrained to visibly unsettled.

**Use.** Respond flexibly to performers or accommodate constrained spaces. Handheld texture does not by itself prove documentary authenticity or create urgency.

**Example.** An operator adjusts position among cooks working in a narrow kitchen during a single busy service.

### Shoulder rig
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.shoulder-rig -->

**Definition.** A support configuration resting part of a camera's weight on the operator's shoulder, often with handles and other contact points. It changes handling and load distribution without eliminating bodily movement.

**Use.** Sustain mobile operating with a particular camera height and physical response. Shoulder-mounted operation remains compatible with handheld aesthetics and varied movement paths.

**Example.** A shoulder-mounted camera accompanies a supervisor inspecting a production line while the operator makes small reframing adjustments.

### Stabilized camera
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.stabilized-camera -->

**Definition.** A camera whose unwanted movement is reduced by a stabilization system or process. Stabilization may be mechanical, electronic, optical, sensor-based, or applied in post-production; these produce different constraints and artifacts.

**Use.** Specify the desired motion character independently from the system used. Stabilization does not mean the camera cannot intentionally move or shake.

**Example.** A walking shot maintains a relatively steady horizon while still advancing through a crowded entrance hall.

### Gimbal
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.gimbal -->

**Definition.** A pivoted support allowing rotation around one or more axes. In contemporary production shorthand, it often means a motorized stabilizer that controls camera orientation; not every gimbal is electronic.

**Use.** Separate some unwanted angular movement from intentional aiming. Translational bobbing, operating skill, balance, and payload still affect the result.

**Example.** A motorized three-axis gimbal keeps the camera aimed toward a courier while the operator walks an adjacent path.

### Steadicam
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.steadicam -->

**Definition.** A trademarked family of camera stabilization systems associated with Tiffen, including body-supported configurations. It is a specific brand, not a generic name for every gimbal or smooth moving shot. [Tiffen, Steadicam](https://tiffen.com/pages/steadicam).

**Use.** Name the intended system when its operating characteristics matter; otherwise describe stabilization and path separately.

**Example.** A Steadicam operator follows a performer from a courtyard through a doorway into a continuous interior scene.

### Vehicle mount
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.vehicle-mount -->

**Definition.** A support attaching a camera to a vehicle or vehicle-associated rig. The view may face passengers, the vehicle's body, the road, or action traveling alongside.

**Use.** Establish a consistent camera-vehicle relationship while distinguishing it from a camera carried by an occupant. Describe the desired frame separately from the engineered mounting solution.

**Example.** A fixed exterior view keeps the bus driver's reflection aligned with a changing streetscape.

### Aerial camera
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.aerial-camera -->

**Definition.** A camera operating from an airborne platform, such as an aircraft or drone. It is a platform description; an elevated crane shot is not automatically an aerial shot.

**Use.** Show large geographical relationships or travel through viewpoints inaccessible from the ground. Specify path, height reference, orientation, and subject relation independently.

**Example.** An aerial view follows a flood channel until it reaches the first houses of a settlement.

## Purpose and attention

### Motivated movement
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.motivated-movement -->

**Definition.** Camera movement with an apparent trigger or reason in the unfolding action, attention, or information of the scene. Motivation may be a glance, a sound, a person's movement, or a developing thought.

**Use.** Link camera behavior to what matters at a particular moment. Motivation can be subtle and need not be literal imitation of a character's eyes.

**Example.** A faint knock prompts a slow pan toward a door previously left outside the frame.

### Unmotivated movement
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.unmotivated-movement -->

**Definition.** Movement without an immediately evident trigger in the depicted action or a character's attention. The label describes its relationship to scene events, not an absence of artistic purpose.

**Use.** Explore an independent narrator-like camera, sustained formal pattern, or atmosphere. Record the intended reason even when characters provide no visible cue.

**Example.** The camera drifts away from an ordinary conversation and studies the unused end of the room.

### Reveal
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.reveal -->

**Definition.** A movement that makes previously unseen visual information available through a change in framing or viewpoint. The information can have been outside the frame, hidden behind an object, or obscured by an angle.

**Use.** Control when a spatial fact becomes knowable. A reveal is a function, compatible with pans, tracking, rises, and other moves.

**Example.** A lateral move clears a pillar and exposes a second suitcase beside the missing traveler's bag.

### Reframing
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.reframing -->

**Definition.** Adjusting the image's boundaries or subject placement during a shot. Reframing may use rotation, translation, focal-length change, or a planned post-production crop, which should be identified separately.

**Use.** Maintain readable action or intentionally shift emphasis as circumstances change. A small correction and a conspicuous compositional change are both forms of reframing.

**Example.** When one person stands, a subtle tilt preserves both her face and the seated listener's response.

### Whip pan
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.whip-pan -->

**Definition.** A very rapid pan, often producing a brief interval of pronounced motion blur. Swish pan is a common alias; the term still describes angular movement rather than lateral translation.

**Use.** Redirect attention abruptly or connect shots through similar blurred movement. Blur amount depends on motion, shutter timing, and scene detail.

**Example.** The camera snaps from a dropped tray to the doorway where everyone suddenly looks.

## Movement parameters

### Path
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.path -->

**Definition.** The trajectory followed by the camera's position through space. A path describes where it travels, while orientation describes where it points; either may change independently.

**Use.** Plan spatial reveals and coordinate repeatability with performers and support. A drawn path is schematic unless its coordinate system and measurements are defined.

**Example.** An L-shaped route passes along a shelving aisle before turning into the room behind it.

### Direction
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.direction -->

**Definition.** The orientation of travel or rotation relative to a stated reference, such as world axes, screen space, the camera, or a subject. “Forward” is ambiguous without that reference.

**Use.** Make movement notes interpretable across diagrams, shot lists, and operators. Distinguish clockwise travel around a subject from clockwise camera roll.

**Example.** A note specifies movement toward the doorway in world space while the camera continues looking backward at the speaker.

### Travel distance
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.travel-distance -->

**Definition.** The length covered along a camera's translation path, normally measured in meters or feet. It differs from straight-line displacement between endpoints when the route bends.

**Use.** Relate path geometry to duration, speed, framing, and available space. Pure rotation has angular travel but no intended translational distance.

**Example.** A curved route covers four meters even though its end position is only two meters from its start.

### Duration
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.duration -->

**Definition.** The time occupied by a defined camera movement or interval, normally measured in seconds. Movement duration and final shot duration differ when the shot holds before or after the move.

**Use.** Coordinate a reveal with dialogue, gesture, or editing. Identify whether timing refers to capture time or playback time when footage is retimed.

**Example.** A six-second shot holds for two seconds, moves for three, and settles for one.

### Speed profile
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.speed-profile -->

**Definition.** How travel or rotation speed changes over time. A profile can remain constant, ease in and out, pause, surge, or respond to a moving subject.

**Use.** Describe the feel and timing of a move beyond its total distance and duration. Linear speed and angular speed need different units.

**Example.** A camera begins almost imperceptibly, travels faster across an empty wall, then slows as a hidden portrait enters view.

### Acceleration
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.acceleration -->

**Definition.** Change in velocity over time, including change of direction as well as speed. Linear acceleration uses units such as meters per second squared; angular acceleration requires an angular measure.

**Use.** Discuss starts, stops, turns, and physically plausible transitions. A constant-speed orbit still changes velocity direction and therefore has acceleration.

**Example.** A programmed move gradually increases from rest so the first shift in viewpoint does not arrive as a visible jolt.

### Subject tracking
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.subject-tracking -->

**Definition.** Maintaining a chosen relationship to a subject as it or the camera moves. It may mean following position, keeping the subject framed, or using a tracking system; it does not automatically mean continuous focus.

**Use.** Specify what stays controlled: aim, screen position, distance, or focus. Avoid one checkbox silently promising all four.

**Example.** A pan keeps a cyclist near frame center while focus is adjusted independently as the cyclist approaches.

## Lens changes combined with movement

### Zoom in
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.zoom-in -->

**Definition.** Increasing a zoom lens's focal length during a shot, narrowing the field of view for a fixed active image area. The camera can remain stationary while the subject becomes larger in the frame.

**Use.** Change image scale without the viewpoint change of a dolly move. Lens behavior may introduce additional optical changes.

**Example.** From a fixed observation window, a zoom isolates one numbered crate on the loading dock.

### Zoom out
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.zoom-out -->

**Definition.** Decreasing a zoom lens's focal length during a shot, widening the field of view for a fixed active image area. It reveals additional surrounding image area without requiring backward camera travel.

**Use.** Expand visual context while maintaining approximately the same viewpoint. Distinguish optical zoom from a changing digital crop.

**Example.** A close view of a handwritten sign widens to show that dozens of identical signs fill the room.

### Dolly zoom
<!-- term-id: camera-movement-and-support.dolly-zoom -->

**Definition.** Coordinated camera translation and an opposing focal-length change that keep a chosen subject approximately the same image size while perspective relationships change. Moving away while zooming in is one version. [DJI, introduction to dolly zoom](https://repair.dji.com/help/content?customId=01700006815&documentType=&lang=en&paperDocType=ARTICLE&re=US&spaceId=17).

**Use.** Make changing depth relationships conspicuous; sufficient subject-background separation helps the effect read.

**Example.** The camera retreats from a seated passenger while zooming in, making the platform beyond appear to expand around her.

## Practical distinctions

| Change | Camera position | Camera orientation | Focal length |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pure pan | Fixed | Rotates left/right | Fixed unless separately specified |
| Pure tilt | Fixed | Rotates up/down | Fixed unless separately specified |
| Dolly in/out | Translates | May remain fixed or track | Fixed unless separately specified |
| Optical zoom | May remain fixed | May remain fixed | Changes |
| Dolly zoom | Translates | Usually tracks the subject | Changes to compensate subject scale |

Camera position determines ordinary perspective relationships; a focal-length change at a fixed viewpoint changes the portion and magnification of the scene captured. This is why a dolly and a zoom can produce different results even when their final subject sizes match. [Nikon, choosing lenses: perspective](https://www.nikonimgsupport.com/na/NSG_article?articleNo=000047395&configured=1&lang=en_SG).

“Smooth” describes a result; “gimbal,” “Steadicam,” and “dolly” identify possible equipment. “Follow” identifies a relationship; “arc” identifies a path; “reveal” identifies a function. A useful movement description combines compatible values from these dimensions.

## Visual interaction and structured fields

These proposals do not operate equipment or simulate a verified camera. Keep measured, estimated, and unknown values distinct.

| Decision | Visual interaction | Proposed data fields |
|---|---|---|
| Translation path | Top and side views with a playhead | `path_points_m`, `coordinate_space`, `path_type`, `distance_m`, `measurement_status` |
| Camera orientation | Aim cone and rotation preview | `pan_deg`, `tilt_deg`, `roll_deg`, `look_at_target_id`, `orientation_keyframes` |
| Timing | Position and speed curves on a time ruler | `duration_s`, `speed_m_per_s`, `angular_speed_deg_per_s`, `acceleration_m_per_s2`, `time_basis` |
| Support | Equipment-independent look comparison plus optional support record | `support_type`, `stabilization_method`, `equipment_model`, `motion_character_note` |
| Subject relation | Camera and subject paths shown together | `subject_id`, `tracking_mode`, `target_distance_m`, `target_frame_position_normalized` |
| Zoom coordination | Paired viewpoint and framing previews | `focal_length_keyframes_mm`, `active_sensor_width_mm`, `active_sensor_height_mm`, `subject_scale_target` |
| Dramatic purpose | Trigger marker linked to a scene beat | `movement_function`, `trigger_event`, `intended_effect`, `interpretation_note` |
