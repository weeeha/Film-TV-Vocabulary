# 03 · Story patterns and dramatic engines

[Wiki home](../README.md) · [Alphabetical index](../INDEX.md)

A story pattern describes a recognizable kind of action or change. A dramatic engine is the arrangement that keeps generating consequential problems and choices. A rescue can begin as an investigation, become a pursuit, and force an internal conflict without needing a single exclusive category.

The inventory below is editorial and expandable. It is not a claim that all stories reduce to twelve plots. Joseph Campbell's monomyth is one influential interpretive framework, and the Joseph Campbell Foundation explicitly cautions against treating its variations as rigid, sequential requirements. [Joseph Campbell and the Hero's Journey](https://www.jcf.org/learn/joseph-campbell-heros-journey)

## Patterns of action and change

### Quest
<!-- term-id: story-patterns-and-dramatic-engines.quest -->

**Definition.** A sustained undertaking to reach, obtain, discover, or accomplish something significant, typically through a chain of encounters and tests. A quest need not involve a magical object or geographical travel; what matters is the organizing pursuit and its developing demands.

**Use.** Connect varied episodes through a destination whose meaning can change.

**Example.** A translator searches scattered settlements for the last person who can read a treaty everyone claims to own.

### Pursuit
<!-- term-id: story-patterns-and-dramatic-engines.pursuit -->

**Definition.** A pattern organized around one party trying to catch, reach, expose, or overtake another who can resist or move away. It can be physical, informational, or social; unlike a generic goal, pursuit depends on a changing relationship between pursuer and pursued.

**Use.** Generate pressure through distance, concealment, changing routes, and reversals of advantage.

**Example.** A reporter follows a witness through successive safe houses while the witness deliberately leaves contradictory trails.

### Escape
<!-- term-id: story-patterns-and-dramatic-engines.escape -->

**Definition.** A pattern organized around getting free of confinement, coercion, surveillance, or an untenable situation. Escape requires a meaningful boundary and forces maintaining it; departure alone is not an escape unless something makes leaving difficult or consequential.

**Use.** Turn routes, resources, trust, and timing into connected dramatic problems.

**Example.** Residents of a sealed research station must convince its security system that an evacuation is actually an authorized maintenance exercise.

### Rescue
<!-- term-id: story-patterns-and-dramatic-engines.rescue -->

**Definition.** A pattern in which someone attempts to bring another person or group out of danger or captivity. Rescue includes questions of access, consent, trust, and aftermath; the person being rescued can have substantial agency and conflicting priorities.

**Use.** Connect urgency to responsibility for someone beyond the rescuer's immediate self-interest.

**Example.** A medic reaches an isolated settlement, but its residents refuse evacuation unless their detained neighbors can leave too.

### Investigation
<!-- term-id: story-patterns-and-dramatic-engines.investigation -->

**Definition.** A pattern in which characters gather, compare, and interpret information to understand something unresolved. An investigation may expose a crime, a history, or a scientific anomaly; collecting facts becomes dramatic when interpretations affect choices and provoke resistance.

**Use.** Make each discovery change the next question or the cost of continuing.

**Example.** A maintenance worker tracing a water leak discovers that supposedly abandoned apartments are still receiving official supplies.

### Revenge
<!-- term-id: story-patterns-and-dramatic-engines.revenge -->

**Definition.** A pattern organized around inflicting a perceived answering harm on someone held responsible for an earlier wrong. Revenge can intersect with justice but is not synonymous with it; uncertainty, mistaken attribution, and collateral consequences can reshape the pursuit.

**Use.** Examine how grievance governs choices and transforms relationships.

**Example.** A dismissed musician plans to ruin a conductor's premiere, then discovers the replacement orchestra includes her former students.

### Survival
<!-- term-id: story-patterns-and-dramatic-engines.survival -->

**Definition.** A pattern organized around maintaining life or continued existence under sustained threat. Its obstacles often concern resources, exposure, injury, cooperation, and adaptation, but survival becomes a story through changing decisions rather than an uninterrupted list of hardships.

**Use.** Make immediate needs repeatedly test practical and ethical priorities.

**Example.** Three stranded surveyors must ration their last battery between a distress transmitter and the pump keeping their shelter breathable.

### Transformation
<!-- term-id: story-patterns-and-dramatic-engines.transformation -->

**Definition.** A pattern centered on a substantial change in a person's condition, identity, capacity, or way of living. Transformation may be chosen or imposed, physical or social, beneficial or destructive; visible alteration alone does not establish an inner change.

**Use.** Compare the possibilities and costs before, during, and after a change.

**Example.** A clerk suddenly gains the authority to reverse court decisions and gradually loses the ability to trust any judgment, including her own.

### Coming of age
<!-- term-id: story-patterns-and-dramatic-engines.coming-of-age -->

**Definition.** A pattern concerning movement into a new understanding of adulthood, independence, social responsibility, or identity. It often involves young people, but age alone is not its dramatic mechanism; the meaningful change lies in how the character understands and acts within the world.

**Use.** Connect new freedom with the discovery of limits and obligations.

**Example.** An apprentice earns his first solo crossing and must decide whether to report the captain who falsified his qualification.

### Rise and fall
<!-- term-id: story-patterns-and-dramatic-engines.rise-and-fall -->

**Definition.** A pattern tracing an ascent in power, recognition, wealth, or influence followed by its erosion or collapse. The fall can expose costs embedded in the rise, but success need not always cause failure and the pattern need not imply a simple moral punishment.

**Use.** Compare the methods that win status with the pressures that make it unsustainable.

**Example.** A neighborhood organizer becomes mayor through personal favors and loses every ally when those favors become incompatible obligations.

### Homecoming
<!-- term-id: story-patterns-and-dramatic-engines.homecoming -->

**Definition.** A pattern organized around returning to a place, community, or relationship understood as home and discovering what return now means. The traveler, the home, or both may have changed; reaching the location does not necessarily restore belonging.

**Use.** Put remembered identity into contact with present reality.

**Example.** A sailor returns to inherit the family boat and learns that the crew has turned it into a community school.

## Sources of conflict

### Internal conflict
<!-- term-id: story-patterns-and-dramatic-engines.internal-conflict -->

**Definition.** A tension within a character between incompatible desires, beliefs, fears, loyalties, or self-understandings. It becomes dramatically visible through behavior and choice; describing someone as conflicted is less useful than specifying the alternatives pulling them in different directions.

**Use.** Give external action an additional personal cost or contradiction.

**Example.** A witness wants to expose a fraud but also wants to preserve her daughter's faith in the accused parent.

### Interpersonal conflict
<!-- term-id: story-patterns-and-dramatic-engines.interpersonal-conflict -->

**Definition.** A clash between people whose goals, interpretations, obligations, or methods cannot easily coexist. Conflict can be quiet, affectionate, or indirect; hostility and shouting are possible expressions rather than requirements, and both participants may have understandable aims.

**Use.** Make relationships active by forcing negotiation, resistance, or incompatible decisions.

**Example.** Two siblings agree to care for their father but disagree over whether respecting his wishes means letting him remain alone.

### Institutional conflict
<!-- term-id: story-patterns-and-dramatic-engines.institutional-conflict -->

**Definition.** A clash involving the rules, incentives, procedures, or authority of an organization or social system. Individuals may enforce, resist, or be trapped by the institution; replacing one unpleasant official may not remove the mechanism producing the conflict.

**Use.** Show how systems constrain even well-intentioned choices.

**Example.** A hospital worker can secure emergency supplies only by reporting lower demand, which will reduce the hospital's allocation next month.

### Environmental conflict
<!-- term-id: story-patterns-and-dramatic-engines.environmental-conflict -->

**Definition.** A struggle arising from material surroundings and conditions, including weather, terrain, isolation, infrastructure failure, or ecological change. The environment need not act with intention; social decisions may determine who is exposed and what resources are available to respond.

**Use.** Make physical conditions consequential to tactics, timing, and cooperation.

**Example.** A thaw opens the river route the travelers need while destabilizing the ice beneath their only shelter.

## Engine components

### Goal
<!-- term-id: story-patterns-and-dramatic-engines.goal -->

**Definition.** An outcome a character or group is actively trying to bring about, preserve, or prevent. A goal becomes usable in a scene when progress and failure can be recognized; broad desires such as happiness often need more concrete expression.

**Use.** Give actions a direction and allow tactics to change without losing coherence.

**Example.** The archivist wants the council to postpone demolition until she can retrieve one sealed box from the building.

### Obstacle
<!-- term-id: story-patterns-and-dramatic-engines.obstacle -->

**Definition.** Anything that materially obstructs a goal, including another person's action, missing information, a rule, a physical condition, or the pursuer's own limitation. An obstacle should affect possible choices; inconvenience without consequence may add duration without meaningful dramatic movement.

**Use.** Force adaptation and expose the character's priorities through new tactics.

**Example.** The archivist has access to the building, but its inventory lists the box under a name that no longer exists.

### Stakes
<!-- term-id: story-patterns-and-dramatic-engines.stakes -->

**Definition.** What may be gained, lost, protected, or irreversibly changed through a situation's outcome. Stakes can be intimate or collective, immediate or delayed; larger quantities of danger do not automatically matter more than a clearly established personal consequence.

**Use.** Explain why a character cannot treat failure as a trivial inconvenience.

**Example.** If the box is destroyed, the last evidence of the residents' ownership disappears and the entire block can be legally displaced.

### Deadline
<!-- term-id: story-patterns-and-dramatic-engines.deadline -->

**Definition.** A time boundary after which an option closes, a condition changes, or a consequence occurs. It may be explicit or gradually discovered; a useful deadline has a credible cause and changes how characters allocate time, risk, and attention.

**Use.** Make delay itself a consequential choice.

**Example.** The building will be demolished at sunrise, but the only official who can postpone the work leaves town at midnight.

### Dilemma
<!-- term-id: story-patterns-and-dramatic-engines.dilemma -->

**Definition.** A choice between alternatives that each carry meaningful costs, competing obligations, or unacceptable consequences. A difficult task is not necessarily a dilemma; the tension comes from what choosing one course sacrifices in another valued direction.

**Use.** Reveal priorities when no available tactic preserves everything the character wants.

**Example.** The archivist can save the box by surrendering the witness who revealed its location, or protect the witness and risk losing the evidence.

### Escalation
<!-- term-id: story-patterns-and-dramatic-engines.escalation -->

**Definition.** A development that increases pressure by changing danger, exposure, cost, complexity, or the difficulty of retreat. Escalation can be emotional or informational rather than physically larger; repeating the same problem more loudly may not create a meaningful increase.

**Use.** Ensure that successful tactics generate new demands or narrowing options.

**Example.** The archivist finds the evidence, but using it would reveal that her own department authorized the illegal clearances.

### Reversal
<!-- term-id: story-patterns-and-dramatic-engines.reversal -->

**Definition.** A consequential change that turns an apparent direction, advantage, or understanding into its opposite or a sharply different condition. A reversal is stronger when it follows from prior action or evidence rather than arriving as an unrelated interruption.

**Use.** Reorganize what characters and viewers believe is possible.

**Example.** The official expected to block the retrieval gives the archivist a key, while her trusted ally orders the demolition brought forward.

### Consequence
<!-- term-id: story-patterns-and-dramatic-engines.consequence -->

**Definition.** An effect produced by an action, omission, decision, or changed circumstance. Consequences can be intended or unforeseen, immediate or delayed, and need not function as moral rewards or punishments; they connect events into a developing causal situation.

**Use.** Let choices alter what the next scene can contain.

**Example.** Breaking the archive seal saves the records but makes every document inadmissible until another witness verifies their provenance.

## Practical distinctions

A premise provides an initial situation; an engine explains how it continues generating story. “A team repairs coastal equipment” becomes an engine when every repair forces it to negotiate with residents, officials, and competing uses of scarce parts. Describe the repeatable conflict and its capacity for variation.

Stakes answer what matters. A deadline answers when an option changes. An obstacle blocks movement. A dilemma asks the character to choose between costly alternatives. Escalation increases or transforms pressure; a reversal changes its direction. One event can perform several of these functions.

The useful unit is often a causal sentence: because the character does this, another condition changes, forcing the next choice. John August's discussion of opposing characters stresses the value of both sides actively influencing the plot; that advice is one practical engine model, not a requirement for a personified antagonist. [Writing better bad guys](https://johnaugust.com/2012/writing-better-bad-guys)

## Visual interaction and structured fields

These are proposals for exploring story decisions, not implemented simulation or prediction tools.

| Decision | Visual interaction | Proposed data fields |
|---|---|---|
| Combine story patterns | Stackable pattern cards linked to sequences | `pattern_ids`, `sequence_ids`, `pattern_transition_notes` |
| Explain the engine | Causal graph of action, resistance, result, and renewed problem | `engine_statement`, `actor_id`, `action`, `opposition`, `consequence_ids` |

| Compare a dilemma's alternatives | Branching choice cards with costs and uncertainty | `choice_options`, `costs_by_option`, `values_in_conflict`, `unknowns` |
