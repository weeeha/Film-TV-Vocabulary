# 21 · Documentary and nonfiction approaches

[Wiki home](../README.md) · [Alphabetical index](../INDEX.md)

Nonfiction filmmaking combines records, encounters, accounts, and interpretations of the shared world. Its images and sounds still result from choices about access, framing, recording, selection, and presentation. Describing those choices makes a work's evidence and point of view easier to examine.

The first six approaches below refer to **Bill Nichols's six documentary modes**: observational, participatory, expository, reflexive, poetic, and performative. They are overlapping analytical approaches, not mutually exclusive genres or an exhaustive classification of nonfiction practice. A single film or sequence may use several. Nichols's own book and its publisher establish the framework and its attribution. [Bill Nichols: Introduction to Documentary](https://billnichols.net/books/introduction-to-documentary-2/), [Indiana University Press: Third edition contents](https://iupress.org/9780253026903/introduction-to-documentary-third-edition/)

All examples here are invented teaching situations, not claims about identifiable films or real events.

## Documentary modes

### Observational approach
<!-- term-id: documentary-and-nonfiction.observational-approach -->

**Definition.** An approach emphasizing people and events unfolding before the camera, with comparatively little overt prompting or explanatory intervention in the presented scene. This is Nichols's observational mode.

**Use.** Plan patient coverage that preserves actions and relationships. Document the recording circumstances separately, since an unobtrusive presentation does not mean the filmmaker had no influence.

**Example.** A sequence follows a volunteer kitchen preparing a meal, allowing coordination and disagreement to emerge through the work itself.

### Participatory approach
<!-- term-id: documentary-and-nonfiction.participatory-approach -->

**Definition.** An approach in which encounters between filmmaker and participants help generate the material and knowledge presented. This is Nichols's participatory mode; conversation, inquiry, and intervention become part of the account.

**Use.** Clarify the filmmaker's role and the conditions of the encounter. Decide which questions, responses, and power differences viewers need to understand.

**Example.** A filmmaker accompanies a resident on a walk, asking how each changed building relates to the resident's memories.

### Expository approach
<!-- term-id: documentary-and-nonfiction.expository-approach -->

**Definition.** An approach organized around an explanation or argument, commonly using commentary, testimony, and images in support. This is Nichols's expository mode; a single authoritative voice is common but not required.

**Use.** Map each assertion to relevant support and identify where interpretation enters. Let images provide meaningful evidence instead of merely decorating an already complete spoken explanation.

**Example.** A short film explains a river's changing course using dated maps, observations, and clearly attributed expert interpretation.

### Reflexive approach
<!-- term-id: documentary-and-nonfiction.reflexive-approach -->

**Definition.** An approach that draws attention to how documentary representation is constructed and interpreted. Nichols's reflexive mode examines the making of an account, rather than simply making the filmmaker visible.

**Use.** Reveal a specific representational problem that affects the audience's understanding. Showing a camera or editing room matters when it exposes a consequential choice.

**Example.** The film presents two edits of one exchange, then examines how each changes the apparent relationship between the speakers.

### Poetic approach
<!-- term-id: documentary-and-nonfiction.poetic-approach -->

**Definition.** An approach emphasizing sensory relationships, association, pattern, and rhythm over a continuously explained argument. This is Nichols's poetic mode, which organizes nonfiction material through expressive form.

**Use.** Develop a coherent pattern while retaining enough context for viewers to understand what the images represent. A suggestive arrangement should not quietly turn unrelated material into evidence of causation.

**Example.** Dawn recordings of shutters, train doors, and opening flowers create a study of a city's repeated beginnings.

### Performative approach
<!-- term-id: documentary-and-nonfiction.performative-approach -->

**Definition.** An approach foregrounding situated, embodied, or subjective experience as a way of knowing a subject. This is Nichols's performative mode, not simply a label for documentaries containing staged performances.

**Use.** Explain the relationship between personal experience and broader claims. First-person expression can be revealing without being representative of everyone affected by the subject.

**Example.** A maker combines spoken recollections and expressive movement to explore how a childhood home is remembered through the body.

## Material and encounter forms

### Interview
<!-- term-id: documentary-and-nonfiction.interview -->

**Definition.** A recorded exchange in which questions or prompts elicit a person's account, knowledge, opinion, or reflection. Interviews can be formal or conversational and may show the interviewer, omit them, or present only selected answers.

**Use.** Preserve the context needed to understand each response, especially when editing out the prompt. Distinguish recollection, firsthand observation, expertise, and interpretation.

**Example.** An engineer describes a machine failure, while the edit retains the question that limits her answer to one shift.

### Direct address
<!-- term-id: documentary-and-nonfiction.direct-address -->

**Definition.** A person speaking or otherwise communicating explicitly toward the viewer, often by looking into the camera. Direct address describes the relationship to the audience rather than whether the speaker's statement is verified.

**Use.** Decide why this encounter should feel personal, confrontational, explanatory, or intimate. Separate eye contact from any assumption of spontaneous or unmediated testimony.

**Example.** A neighborhood organizer looks into the lens to explain which parts of the redevelopment process remain unresolved.

### Archival footage
<!-- term-id: documentary-and-nonfiction.archival-footage -->

**Definition.** Previously recorded moving-image material brought into a new work, usually with a meaningful relationship to an earlier time or context. It may come from institutions, broadcasters, personal collections, or other identified sources.

**Use.** Record origin, date, version, and what the footage actually depicts. Distinguish exact event documentation from illustrative footage of a similar place or period.

**Example.** A dated home-movie excerpt shows the building before renovation, with its family source identified alongside the image.

### Observational scene
<!-- term-id: documentary-and-nonfiction.observational-scene -->

**Definition.** A scene built from recorded activity as it unfolds, allowing viewers to follow behavior, relations, and developments. It is a unit of material that can appear within many documentary approaches.

**Use.** Preserve the context of significant actions and make consequential time compression legible. One observational scene does not determine the mode of an entire film.

**Example.** A repair team tests three possible causes of a fault before deciding which component to examine next.

### Reconstruction
<!-- term-id: documentary-and-nonfiction.reconstruction -->

**Definition.** A constructed representation of an event, place, process, or condition that cannot be presented through direct recording alone. It may use models, diagrams, animation, staged images, or other explicit methods.

**Use.** Separate supported details from interpretive additions and label the representation where confusion is likely. Reconstruction is broader here than reenacting an event through performed action.

**Example.** An animated plan reconstructs a demolished room from measurements, with uncertain wall positions drawn differently from documented ones.

### Reenactment
<!-- term-id: documentary-and-nonfiction.reenactment -->

**Definition.** A performed restaging of an earlier event or reported action, using original participants, actors, or other stand-ins. It is one possible reconstruction method and is not a recording of the original occurrence.

**Use.** Identify the basis of the restaging and how its constructed nature will be communicated. Distinguish reenacted detail from information directly supported by surviving sources.

**Example.** Hands demonstrate a remembered sorting procedure on a recreated desk while a caption identifies the scene as a reenactment.

### Actuality
<!-- term-id: documentary-and-nonfiction.actuality -->

**Definition.** Recorded material presenting an actual event or activity, often used for immediate location sound or footage; historically, “actualities” also names an early nonfiction film form. The exact production usage depends on context.

**Use.** Specify whether the term means an audio excerpt, event footage, or the historical form. Actual recording still requires questions about selection and circumstances.

**Example.** A brief recording of a public meeting is identified as actuality from that meeting, distinct from a later explanatory interview.

## Perspective, evidence, and responsibility

### Narrator position
<!-- term-id: documentary-and-nonfiction.narrator-position -->

**Definition.** The narrator's relationship to the subject, including participation, knowledge, perspective, time of telling, and stated limitations. Position affects what the voice can reasonably claim and how its account relates to other material.

**Use.** Describe whether the narrator witnessed events, learned about them later, or speaks from specialist or personal experience. Make shifts in position clear.

**Example.** A former employee narrates remembered routines while identifying which later developments were learned from records rather than witnessed.

### Subject participation
<!-- term-id: documentary-and-nonfiction.subject-participation -->

**Definition.** The ways people represented in a documentary contribute to, negotiate, or influence its making and presentation. Participation can include access, conversation, self-recording, consultation, or shared creative decisions; these are different levels of involvement.

**Use.** Record what was actually agreed and how expectations evolve. Appearing on camera does not by itself establish editorial authority or shared authorship.

**Example.** Residents contribute recordings and discuss context with the maker, while the project clearly describes who makes the final editing decisions.

### Evidence
<!-- term-id: documentary-and-nonfiction.evidence -->

**Definition.** Material used to support or challenge a particular claim, such as records, observations, measurements, testimony, or images. Its relevance and strength depend on the claim, source, context, and possible alternative explanations.

**Use.** Connect each material item to the statement it supports and note its limits. An evocative image can be meaningful without proving the adjacent spoken assertion.

**Example.** Dated maintenance logs support when repairs occurred, while an interview supplies a participant's interpretation of why they were delayed.

### Attribution
<!-- term-id: documentary-and-nonfiction.attribution -->

**Definition.** Identifying who created a record, made a statement, supplied material, or advanced an interpretation. Attribution allows viewers and collaborators to distinguish the filmmaker's claims from the claims of other sources.

**Use.** Preserve source identity, context, date, and any necessary description of limits. Naming a source does not automatically verify everything that source says.

**Example.** A caption identifies a historical estimate as the municipality's published figure rather than presenting it as a new independent measurement.

### Uncertainty
<!-- term-id: documentary-and-nonfiction.uncertainty -->

**Definition.** The recognized limits of what is known, supported, or inferable about a subject. Uncertainty can concern missing records, incompatible accounts, imprecise dates, incomplete measurements, or an interpretation that remains open.

**Use.** State what is uncertain and why, without implying that all available accounts are equally supported. Update the claim when better evidence becomes available.

**Example.** The film establishes that a closure occurred during winter but leaves the exact week unresolved because surviving records disagree.

### Editorial framing
<!-- term-id: documentary-and-nonfiction.editorial-framing -->

**Definition.** The perspective created through selection, ordering, omission, emphasis, and audiovisual presentation. Framing includes questions asked, statements retained, music added, and the relationships implied between adjacent pieces of material.

**Use.** Examine what a reasonable viewer is likely to infer, especially about cause, chronology, and agreement. Compare those inferences with what the recorded evidence supports.

**Example.** Moving a reaction shot after a different statement changes its apparent meaning, so the editor checks the original exchange before using it.

## Practical distinctions

| Question | Useful distinction |
| --- | --- |
| How is the work organized? | A documentary mode describes an approach; an interview or archive excerpt describes a material form. |
| What does the image show? | Recorded occurrence, performed reenactment, constructed reconstruction, and illustration make different evidentiary claims. |
| Is the filmmaker visible? | Visibility can support participation; reflexivity specifically examines how representation works. |
| What kind of truth is claimed? | Personal experience, observed events, corroborated facts, and interpretations need distinct descriptions. |
| Who shaped the work? | Participation, consultation, approval of particular material, and final editorial control are separate arrangements. |

The six modes are a useful framework, not a ranking of truthfulness. A voice-led film can be carefully evidenced; an observational scene can imply unsupported chronology through editing. This wiki's proposed fields therefore record the status of the material independently from the mode label.

## Visual interaction and structured fields

These proposed controls should make source relationships inspectable without converting complex evidence into a misleading single confidence score.

| Decision | Visual interaction | Proposed fields |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Documentary approach | Multiple mode tags attached to a sequence | Modes; dominant approach; purpose; overlapping approaches; rationale |
| Material status | Source strip linked to the edited excerpt | Material type; capture date; original context; observed/reconstructed/inferred; source ID |
| Claim support | Claim card connected to supporting and conflicting material | Claim; evidence; relevance; limitations; contradiction; verification status |
| Participation | Role map and agreement history | Participant role; contributions; agreed review scope; decision owner; dated changes |
| Reconstruction clarity | Switch between represented scene and supporting source annotations | Supported details; inferred details; invented connective material; disclosure method |
| Editorial inference | Compare adjacent edits with a recorded implication note | Edit order; implied chronology; implied cause; source sequence; unresolved concern |

“Reconstruction” and “reenactment” overlap in actual practice. Their distinction here is explicit and useful for annotation: reconstruction is the broader category; reenactment specifies restaged action. The proposed evidence and decision fields are this wiki's application design, not Nichols's taxonomy or an industry certification.
