# 08 · Character arcs and relationships

[Wiki home](../README.md) · [Alphabetical index](../INDEX.md)

A character arc tracks meaningful continuity or change across a chosen span. A relationship arc tracks how people relate to one another: what they know, expect, permit, withhold, and do together. Neither requires a smooth progression or a permanently positive outcome.

Describe changes through observable choices. In discussing endings, John August and Craig Mazin connect an ending's significance to what a character can finally do and to relationships carried through the story. That is a useful craft lens, not a requirement that every character undergo a transformation. [Scriptnotes: Crafting Your Ending](https://johnaugust.com/2024/scriptnotes-episode-647-crafting-your-ending-transcript)

## Arc patterns

### Growth arc
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.growth-arc -->

**Definition.** A progression in which a character develops a capacity, understanding, or way of acting that the work presents as constructive. Growth need not make the person perfect or remove grief, conflict, and limitation; identify the specific dimension that changes.

**Use.** Connect new behavior to experiences and choices that make it credible.

**Example.** A dispatcher begins by hiding uncertainty and ends by asking the field crew to challenge her plan before they put it into action.

### Decline arc
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.decline-arc -->

**Definition.** A progression toward diminished capacity, connection, judgment, integrity, or possibility, depending on the story's chosen focus. Decline is broader than corruption: worsening circumstances or isolation need not mean moral deterioration, and the work should clarify what is actually being lost.

**Use.** Trace how successive choices and conditions narrow a character's world.

**Example.** An admired captain refuses every offer of help until maintaining the boat consumes the friendships and resources that once made her independence possible.

### Steadfast arc
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.steadfast-arc -->

**Definition.** A progression in which a character's central commitment remains substantially intact while events test, clarify, or alter its expression. Sometimes called a flat arc in particular frameworks, it does not mean nothing happens or that the character learns no practical lessons.

**Use.** Make continuity active by showing its cost and effect on others.

**Example.** A clerk continues defending accurate public records through successive threats, eventually persuading colleagues to join her without abandoning her original principle.

### Redemption
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.redemption -->

**Definition.** A progression in which a character confronts prior wrongdoing and undertakes meaningful accountability, change, or repair. Redemption is an ethical interpretation, not a transaction that guarantees forgiveness; one dramatic sacrifice need not erase an established history of harm.

**Use.** Connect changed intention to sustained action and consequences for those affected.

**Example.** A former official testifies against the scheme he administered, returns what he gained, and accepts that the displaced residents may still refuse his friendship.

### Corruption
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.corruption -->

**Definition.** A progression in which a character increasingly accepts, rationalizes, or commits conduct that violates the ethical standards relevant to the story. Corruption can occur through small compromises rather than a single conversion, and external success can accompany it.

**Use.** Track how exceptions become habits and how language changes to excuse them.

**Example.** A reformer first hides one donor's misconduct to win an election, then builds an office dedicated to hiding every donor's misconduct.

### Disillusionment
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.disillusionment -->

**Definition.** A progression in which confidence in a person, institution, ideal, or interpretation breaks down through experience. Losing an illusion may produce clarity, bitterness, renewed commitment, or paralysis; disillusionment is not automatically decline or the end of all belief.

**Use.** Explore what remains actionable after a valued explanation no longer holds.

**Example.** An apprentice discovers that her mentor's celebrated rescue depended on an uncredited crew, then chooses to learn from that crew instead.

### Reconciliation
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.reconciliation -->

**Definition.** A progression toward a renewed or newly workable relationship after conflict, estrangement, or incompatible accounts of the past. Reconciliation may involve acknowledgment, boundaries, and partial agreement; it does not require forgetting harm, complete forgiveness, or returning to the former arrangement.

**Use.** Make renewed connection depend on specific changes both participants can recognize.

**Example.** Two siblings agree to manage their inherited workshop together only after naming the debt that previously made every conversation impossible.

## Relationship configurations

### Mentor–student
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.mentorstudent -->

**Definition.** A relationship organized around guidance and learning, often involving unequal experience or recognized expertise. Influence can run both ways, and authority should have a defined domain; teaching skill does not confer unlimited control over the student's other choices.

**Use.** Dramatize knowledge transfer, dependence, disagreement, and eventual renegotiation of authority.

**Example.** A navigator teaches an apprentice to read storms, then must accept the apprentice's safer route when the old charts prove unreliable.

### Rivals
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.rivals -->

**Definition.** People whose pursuit of a valued outcome places them in continuing comparison or competition. Rivalry can coexist with friendship, affection, and shared standards; the relationship depends on what is contested and how each person's success affects the other's possibilities.

**Use.** Reveal character through responses to someone whose competence or recognition matters personally.

**Example.** Two local pilots compete for one contract but insist that the winning offer must include adequate maintenance funding for either of them to fly safely.

### Uneasy allies
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.uneasy-allies -->

**Definition.** People cooperating toward an overlapping goal while retaining mistrust, incompatible priorities, or the expectation of future conflict. Their alliance has conditions; tension becomes useful when those conditions affect tactics rather than remaining only sarcastic dialogue.

**Use.** Make necessary cooperation vulnerable to information and shifting incentives.

**Example.** A smuggler and an inspector join forces to reach a stranded crew, each knowing the cargo they recover may become evidence against the other.

### Adversaries
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.adversaries -->

**Definition.** People in a relationship of sustained opposition over goals, values, resources, or power. Adversaries need not hate each other or possess unequal moral worth, and they may recognize each other's legitimacy while finding their immediate aims incompatible.

**Use.** Give conflict continuity through recognizable motives and changing methods.

**Example.** A tenant organizer and a building manager both seek to prevent a collapse but fight over whether closing the building will protect residents or permanently displace them.

### Family
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.family -->

**Definition.** A relationship configuration understood through kinship, adoption, partnership, caregiving, or culturally recognized family bonds. Families vary in membership and obligation; the label does not establish affection, safety, shared values, or a universal hierarchy.

**Use.** Explore histories and expectations that precede the current conflict and are difficult to treat as disposable.

**Example.** Three cousins who barely speak inherit responsibility for an aunt's care and discover that each believes a different promise was made.

### Found family
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.found-family -->

**Definition.** A relationship configuration in which people develop durable bonds of care, belonging, and obligation understood as familial beyond or alongside inherited kinship. The term does not require rejecting an existing family or pretending chosen bonds contain no conflict.

**Use.** Show belonging formed through repeated commitments rather than a single declaration of closeness.

**Example.** A night-shift crew begins sharing meals, then rearranges work and housing when one member loses the support that previously made the job possible.

### Partners
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.partners -->

**Definition.** People connected through a shared undertaking, commitment, or continuing arrangement, which may be professional, creative, romantic, domestic, or several at once. Partnership does not guarantee equality or identical goals; specify the domain, terms, and expectations involved.

**Use.** Make coordination, division of labor, and negotiated responsibility part of the relationship.

**Example.** Two boat owners share repair costs but discover that one sees the business as permanent while the other sees it as a route out of town.

## Relationship dynamics

### Trust
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.trust -->

**Definition.** A willingness to rely on another person in a particular domain despite some uncertainty or vulnerability. Trust can be uneven and specific: someone may trust a colleague's technical skill while doubting their discretion, and trust need not be mutually held.

**Use.** Show what risk a character will allow another person to influence.

**Example.** The captain hands her rival the wheel during a storm but still refuses to disclose the passenger hidden below deck.

### Dependency
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.dependency -->

**Definition.** Reliance on another person for a resource, capacity, condition, or form of support. Dependency may be reciprocal, chosen, temporary, or imposed; it is not inherently weakness, but its consequences depend on alternatives and how the relied-upon person uses their position.

**Use.** Make practical reliance visible alongside affection, conflict, or resentment.

**Example.** A skilled engineer can repair the town's pumps only with the interpreter whose unpaid labor the council has repeatedly dismissed.

### Loyalty
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.loyalty -->

**Definition.** A continuing commitment to a person, group, or principle that influences behavior when competing pressures arise. Loyalty can support care or sustain harm; the crucial questions are what is owed, to whom, under which conditions, and at whose expense.

**Use.** Force choices between commitments that ordinarily coexist without being tested.

**Example.** A deputy loyal to her captain refuses his order to destroy a report because she believes protecting him now requires ending his deception.

### Jealousy
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.jealousy -->

**Definition.** Concern or distress about losing a valued bond, position, attention, or exclusive place to a perceived rival. Jealousy differs from simply wanting what another possesses, often called envy, though the experiences can overlap and be misinterpreted by characters.

**Use.** Reveal insecurity through the relationship or status the character fears losing.

**Example.** An apprentice resents the mentor's new assistant less for her skill than for receiving the private jokes that once signaled their own special connection.

### Intimacy
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.intimacy -->

**Definition.** A degree of personal closeness involving access, attention, shared knowledge, vulnerability, or physical connection. Intimacy is not necessarily sexual or romantic and can be mutual or uneven; a private confession alone does not establish that it is safe or reciprocated.

**Use.** Show what characters permit each other to see or do that others cannot.

**Example.** Two coworkers quietly exchange hearing batteries and packed lunches every morning, revealing an attentive closeness neither expresses through declarations.

### Alienation
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.alienation -->

**Definition.** A felt or enacted separation from a person, group, place, activity, or former sense of belonging. Alienation can persist amid physical proximity and polite cooperation; it should be grounded in specific changes rather than treated as a permanent personality trait.

**Use.** Show disconnection through altered attention, participation, language, and shared routines.

**Example.** A captain still attends the crew's meals but no longer recognizes the stories they tell about the voyage they all survived.

### Betrayal
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.betrayal -->

**Definition.** An action experienced as violating a significant trust, commitment, or understood obligation. Betrayal depends on the relationship's terms and perspectives; characters may disagree over whether an action broke a promise or honored a more important one.

**Use.** Make a violated expectation produce consequences beyond a momentary shock.

**Example.** A confidant gives the protagonist's hidden documents to investigators, believing disclosure will protect the people the protagonist promised to help.

### Power imbalance
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.power-imbalance -->

**Definition.** An unequal ability to shape decisions, access resources, impose consequences, or leave a relationship. Imbalance can involve authority, money, knowledge, dependence, or social standing; identify the specific mechanism rather than assuming every unequal relationship behaves alike.

**Use.** Clarify what agreement, refusal, and negotiation actually cost each participant.

**Example.** A landlord offers to forgive a tenant's rent if she testifies in his favor, making a seemingly friendly request difficult to refuse freely.

## Relationship changes

### Relationship formation
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.relationship-formation -->

**Definition.** The process through which previously unconnected or loosely connected people establish recognizable expectations, commitments, or patterns of interaction. Formation can involve attraction, collaboration, conflict, or need; the important change is what they now mean or owe to one another.

**Use.** Show connection becoming consequential through repeated action and negotiated terms.

**Example.** Two strangers share emergency shelter, then agree that neither will leave for help without first telling the other where they are going.

### Rupture
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.rupture -->

**Definition.** A significant break in a relationship's trust, continuity, expectations, or agreed arrangement. Rupture can be sudden or become undeniable after accumulated damage; a loud argument is not necessarily a rupture if the underlying relationship remains unchanged.

**Use.** Identify the action or recognition after which the old terms no longer hold.

**Example.** The apprentice discovers her mentor claimed credit for her design and removes her tools from their shared workshop without joining the planned launch.

### Repair
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.repair -->

**Definition.** Actions intended to address damage to a relationship through acknowledgment, restitution, changed behavior, boundaries, or renewed communication. Repair is a process with uncertain acceptance; an apology alone does not prove restoration, and repair can support respectful separation rather than reunion.

**Use.** Make attempted improvement observable and responsive to the harmed person's terms.

**Example.** The mentor corrects the public credit, gives the apprentice control of her design, and continues honoring that decision after she declines to return.

### Reversal
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.reversal -->

**Definition.** A development that changes the relationship's direction or distribution of roles, dependence, knowledge, or authority. A reversal needs a meaningful before-and-after relationship, not merely a surprising fact; it may expose a concealed arrangement or create a genuinely new one.

**Use.** Reconsider who guides, protects, needs, or can refuse whom.

**Example.** A navigator loses access to the route she memorized and must rely on the apprentice whose new mapping method she had repeatedly dismissed.

### Separation
<!-- term-id: character-arcs-and-relationships.separation -->

**Definition.** A movement apart that changes contact, shared activity, commitment, or relationship status. Separation can be physical, emotional, temporary, chosen, or imposed; it may end a relationship, preserve it under new terms, or leave its meaning unsettled.

**Use.** Show which bonds survive distance and which depended on a shared situation.

**Example.** The two boat owners sell their vessel and take different jobs, but continue setting aside one evening each month to repair equipment together.

## Practical distinctions

A plot victory does not prove growth, and a painful ending does not prove decline. Compare specific choices across similar pressures. A character may become more honest while losing status, or become more powerful while increasingly exploiting others. Name the dimension before drawing the arc.

Relationships are directional and contextual. A trusts B to navigate; B trusts A with money; neither trusts the other to keep a secret. That description is more useful than one permanent trust score. Public status, private feeling, and practical dependence may move independently.

Differentiate intention from observable behavior and interpretation. Scriptnotes' discussion of character emotions emphasizes playable actions and the particulars of a scene; this supports recording what someone does rather than relying only on an emotional adjective. ## Visual interaction and structured fields

These proposals describe a future relationship editor. They are not implemented emotional measurements, personality scores, or predictions of audience response.

| Decision | Visual interaction | Proposed data fields |
|---|---|---|
| Define the arc's dimension | Timeline of before-and-after choices under comparable pressure | `arc_id`, `arc_scope`, `change_dimension`, `initial_behavior`, `later_behavior`, `evidence_scene_ids` |
| Map a relationship | Directed, labeled links that change at selected scenes | `relationship_id`, `from_character_id`, `to_character_id`, `relationship_type`, `scene_id` |
| Make trust specific | Domain cards for skill, confidentiality, care, and resources | `trust_domain`, `reliance_action`, `vulnerability`, `trust_evidence` |
| Track damage and repair | Event chain linking commitment, rupture, response, and outcome | `commitment`, `rupture_event_id`, `repair_actions`, `response`, `current_terms` |
| Describe unequal power | Resource and exit-option map for each participant | `controlled_resources`, `decision_authority`, `exit_options`, `constraints`, `power_shift_event_id` |
