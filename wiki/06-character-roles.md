# 06 · Character roles

[Wiki home](../README.md) · [Alphabetical index](../INDEX.md)

A role describes what a character does in relation to a particular story or strand. It does not determine personality, moral worth, screen time, or casting credit. One person can be a protagonist in one strand, an antagonist in another, and a confidant within a third relationship.

Keep function separate from judgment: a protagonist need not be heroic, and an antagonist need not be a villain. John August's discussion of opposing characters offers a useful practical emphasis on both sides having reasons and influencing events, though this chapter uses protagonist and antagonist more broadly than his informal hero-and-villain language. [Writing better bad guys](https://johnaugust.com/2012/writing-better-bad-guys)

## Principal and supporting roles

### Protagonist
<!-- term-id: character-roles.protagonist -->

**Definition.** The principal character or collective whose pursuit, predicament, or developing choices organize a story or substantial strand. Protagonist names narrative centrality, not goodness; the protagonist may be selfish, mistaken, destructive, or different from the character through whose eyes events are presented.

**Use.** Identify whose situation provides the main through-line and what changes for them.

**Example.** A corrupt port official trying to hide a falsified manifest is the protagonist of a story that follows the consequences of his concealment.

### Antagonist
<!-- term-id: character-roles.antagonist -->

**Definition.** A character whose goals or actions substantially oppose the protagonist's pursuit within the relevant story. The term is also used more broadly for an antagonistic force, such as an institution or environment; opposition does not by itself imply moral wrongdoing.

**Use.** Specify the source of sustained resistance and what it seeks to preserve or achieve.

**Example.** An honest inspector is the antagonist to the corrupt official because her investigation prevents him from destroying the manifest.

### Deuteragonist
<!-- term-id: character-roles.deuteragonist -->

**Definition.** A second principal character with sustained importance beside the protagonist. The label describes a narrative hierarchy when one is useful; it does not prescribe friendship, morality, billing order, or a fixed share of screen time, and an ensemble may have no clear second lead.

**Use.** Recognize a major accompanying perspective or line of action without treating it as incidental support.

**Example.** The official's deputy pursues a separate attempt to protect the workers implicated by his decisions, carrying the story's second major arc.

### Ensemble lead
<!-- term-id: character-roles.ensemble-lead -->

**Definition.** One of several characters sharing substantial narrative emphasis in a work organized around a group or multiple central strands. Ensemble balance can change across scenes or episodes; it does not require equal dialogue, identical arcs, or the absence of local protagonists.

**Use.** Design meaningful interdependence among several centers of action.

**Example.** An evacuation drama follows a dispatcher, a ferry captain, and a stranded teacher, each making choices that determine the others' options.

### Supporting character
<!-- term-id: character-roles.supporting-character -->

**Definition.** A character with less overall narrative emphasis than the principal figures whose actions still contribute to the work's situations, relationships, or meaning. Supporting describes placement, not a requirement to serve unquestioningly or remain without goals and contradictions.

**Use.** Give the story a socially and causally credible world beyond its central pursuit.

**Example.** A harbor cook refuses to feed the crew on credit, forcing a plan to change while revealing the protagonist's history of unpaid promises.

## Roles defined through relationships and perspective

### Foil
<!-- term-id: character-roles.foil -->

**Definition.** A character whose similarities and differences make another character's qualities, methods, or values more visible. A foil need not be an enemy or exact opposite; carefully shared circumstances can make a single important difference especially revealing.

**Use.** Develop characterization through comparison in action rather than explanatory description.

**Example.** Two inspectors discover the same violation: one protects the workers by delaying the report, while the other protects them by making it public immediately.

### Confidant
<!-- term-id: character-roles.confidant -->

**Definition.** A character trusted with another person's private information, uncertainties, or feelings. This relationship can make hidden concerns dramatizable, but a confidant should not automatically become an exposition device with no independent interests or capacity to respond.

**Use.** Let disclosure create a relationship consequence as well as communicate information.

**Example.** The deputy admits her plan to the harbor cook, who listens sympathetically but refuses to hide the documents in his kitchen.

### Ally
<!-- term-id: character-roles.ally -->

**Definition.** A character who supports another's goal or action in a particular situation. Alliance does not require affection, shared values, equal commitment, or permanent loyalty; its scope and conditions may be narrower than either participant initially assumes.

**Use.** Expand possible action while introducing dependence, negotiation, and conflicting priorities.

**Example.** A rival captain lends the protagonist a boat on the condition that no passengers are taken beyond the marked safe channel.

### Rival
<!-- term-id: character-roles.rival -->

**Definition.** A character competing with another for a valued outcome, position, recognition, or relationship. Rivals may respect, resemble, or cooperate with each other; rivalry becomes antagonism when it substantially obstructs the central pursuit, but the roles are not identical.

**Use.** Make comparable ambitions reveal different methods and priorities.

**Example.** Two dispatchers seek the same promotion, yet one risks losing it by publicly defending the other's necessary breach of protocol.

### Love interest
<!-- term-id: character-roles.love-interest -->

**Definition.** A character positioned as the focus of another's romantic attraction or as a participant in a developing romantic relationship. The label describes one relationship function, not the character's whole identity, availability, or obligation to reciprocate affection.

**Use.** Track how attraction affects choices while preserving both participants' agency.

**Example.** A cartographer falls for a pilot whose plan to leave the coast conflicts with the cartographer's determination to rebuild the town.

### Narrator
<!-- term-id: character-roles.narrator -->

**Definition.** The voice or figure that tells, comments on, or organizes an account for an audience or listener. A narrator may participate in the events or stand outside them; narrator, protagonist, and viewpoint character can be different roles.

**Use.** Define who presents information, from what position, and with what limitations.

**Example.** An elderly cook narrates the captain's final voyage while acknowledging that the last exchange aboard the boat was told to him secondhand.

### Audience surrogate
<!-- term-id: character-roles.audience-surrogate -->

**Definition.** A character whose position allows viewers to encounter unfamiliar information, customs, or situations alongside them. The surrogate may ask useful questions or share limited knowledge, but cannot represent every audience member's identity, values, or emotional response.

**Use.** Give exposition a situated reason and make learning part of the action.

**Example.** A new apprentice's first dispatch introduces the crew's procedures as she discovers which rules are followed, bent, and quietly ignored.

## Roles that change access and action

### Catalyst
<!-- term-id: character-roles.catalyst -->

**Definition.** A character whose arrival, action, demand, or example initiates significant change in another person's situation. The catalyst may remain central or leave after triggering events; being a catalyst does not mean having no agency or no change of their own.

**Use.** Identify who disrupts an established pattern and why that disruption matters now.

**Example.** A child returns a lost key to the official, revealing that someone has entered the archive he believed permanently sealed.

### Gatekeeper
<!-- term-id: character-roles.gatekeeper -->

**Definition.** A character who controls access to a place, resource, permission, knowledge, or social position. Gatekeeping can be protective, obstructive, procedural, or exploitative; the role is defined by control over a threshold rather than by villainy or bureaucratic manner.

**Use.** Make access depend on negotiation, qualification, persuasion, or a consequential choice.

**Example.** A librarian will release the damaged shipping records only if the investigator agrees to document how they were previously mishandled.

### Witness
<!-- term-id: character-roles.witness -->

**Definition.** A character who observes, survives, remembers, or can testify to relevant events. Witnessing is not equivalent to complete knowledge or unquestionable accuracy; vantage point, attention, memory, and willingness to speak shape what the character can establish.

**Use.** Connect evidence to a situated person with their own risks and interpretations.

**Example.** A night cleaner saw the official enter the archive but could not see whether the person leaving later wore the same face or only the same coat.

## Practical distinctions

Ask three different questions: who organizes the story, who experiences or presents it, and whom the work invites us to admire? The answers may name three different characters. A protagonist can cause harm; a morally admirable inspector can oppose them; a witness can narrate both without becoming the central actor.

Roles are scoped. Record “ally to Mara during the evacuation,” not simply “ally.” A rival can become a confidant, a gatekeeper can act as a catalyst, and the same character can support one goal while obstructing another. A character's current behavior provides better evidence than a permanent label.

Character planning should connect roles to observable activity. John August's account of planning characters and events together is useful here as a practitioner method, not a demand that every story begin from plot. [Story first, then characters](https://johnaugust.com/2003/story-first-then-characters)

## Visual interaction and structured fields

These are proposed ways to inspect roles in context, not implemented casting or characterization tools.

| Decision | Visual interaction | Proposed data fields |
|---|---|---|
| Scope a role | Character card linked to a story strand and time interval | `character_id`, `role_id`, `story_strand_id`, `valid_from_scene`, `valid_to_scene` |
| Distinguish centrality and perspective | Separate narrative-focus and viewpoint lanes | `protagonist_ids`, `viewpoint_character_ids`, `narrator_id`, `perspective_notes` |
| Describe opposition | Directed link labeled with the incompatible goals | `opposes_character_id`, `opposed_goal_id`, `own_goal_id`, `opposition_reason` |
| Track role changes | Relationship graph that changes by scene | `relationship_id`, `role_transition`, `trigger_scene_id`, `evidence_note` |
| Preserve independent agency | Goal and choice cards beside each role label | `current_goal`, `available_choices`, `constraints`, `consequences` |
