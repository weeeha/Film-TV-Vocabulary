# 13 · Production design and character appearance

[Wiki home](../README.md) · [Alphabetical index](../INDEX.md)

Production design and appearance make story circumstances visible through spaces, objects, clothing, bodies, and surfaces. This chapter groups related creative decisions without implying that one department owns all of them. Costume, hair, makeup, props, set decoration, construction, and cinematography collaborate, with responsibilities varying by production.

The examples are original, hypothetical situations. A repaired sleeve or expensive chair is evidence within a designed fiction, not a reliable real-world test of someone's identity, wealth, or moral character.

## Spaces, objects, and fixtures

### Set design
<!-- term-id: production-design-and-appearance.set-design -->

**Definition.** The conception and specification of spaces built or adapted for filming, including layout, architecture, surfaces, and functional requirements. A set may be physical, virtual, or a planned combination.

**Use.** Shape the possibilities for action and camera placement while expressing the story's world. Production designers coordinate design with construction, decoration, props, and other departments. [ScreenSkills, production designer skills](https://www.screenskills.com/skills-checklists/scripted-film-and-tv/art-department/production-designer-skills/).

**Example.** A records office is designed with narrow public counters and a vast archive visible behind them.

### Location dressing
<!-- term-id: production-design-and-appearance.location-dressing -->

**Definition.** Adding, removing, covering, or rearranging visible elements at an existing filming location to suit the story. It adapts a found environment rather than necessarily building its underlying architecture.

**Use.** Transform a suitable place into a specific fictional one while working with existing geometry and access. The term overlaps with set dressing but identifies the location context.

**Example.** A contemporary café receives older signs, period tableware, and coverings over electronic payment terminals.

### Props
<!-- term-id: production-design-and-appearance.props -->

**Definition.** Objects selected or made for use within the action, especially objects handled by performers. “Properties” is the longer form; boundaries with dressing and costume vary across departments and productions.

**Use.** Give characters practical actions and make plot information tangible. ScreenSkills distinguishes handled objects from the environmental focus of set decoration. [ScreenSkills, set decorator](https://www.screenskills.com/job-profiles/browse/film-and-tv-drama/craft/set-decorator/).

**Example.** A dented lunchbox carries the note that changes a factory worker's decision.

### Hero prop
<!-- term-id: production-design-and-appearance.hero-prop -->

**Definition.** A featured object, or a specially detailed version of an object, intended for prominent scrutiny or important action. “Hero” describes production emphasis and does not mean the object belongs to a heroic character.

**Use.** Identify items needing readable detail, dependable interaction, close-up finish, or matching duplicates. Distinguish a display-quality version from stunt or background versions where relevant.

**Example.** A brass compass has a working engraved dial for the scene in which its hidden inscription is discovered.

### Set dressing
<!-- term-id: production-design-and-appearance.set-dressing -->

**Definition.** The furnishings, decoration, and environmental objects arranged to give a set its specific character and history. Dressing may include furniture, textiles, wall objects, and background clutter.

**Use.** Communicate how a place is occupied and maintained without requiring dialogue. A dressing item may need coordination with props if it becomes part of performed action. [ScreenSkills, set decorator](https://www.screenskills.com/job-profiles/browse/film-and-tv-drama/craft/set-decorator/).

**Example.** Repeated replacement chair legs suggest that a boarding house repairs possessions instead of replacing them.

### Practical fixture
<!-- term-id: production-design-and-appearance.practical-fixture -->

**Definition.** A functioning fixture within the designed environment, most commonly a visible lamp or other light source. In broader art-department usage, “practical” can also indicate that a door, tap, or device actually operates.

**Use.** Let performers interact with the environment and provide visible motivation for illumination. A practical lamp may be supplemented by lights outside the frame.

**Example.** A desk lamp switches on during a confession while concealed lighting helps its apparent beam illuminate the listener.

## Character appearance and surfaces

### Costume
<!-- term-id: production-design-and-appearance.costume -->

**Definition.** Clothing and wearable accessories chosen or constructed for a character, including their fit, condition, and changes through the story. Contemporary everyday clothing is costume when deliberately selected for the production.

**Use.** Express circumstances, self-presentation, occupation, or transformation while supporting movement and continuity. Clothing should work with the performer and scene rather than function only as a symbolic label.

**Example.** A character wears an interview jacket with the temporary sleeve stitching still attached.

### Makeup
<!-- term-id: production-design-and-appearance.makeup -->

**Definition.** Cosmetic and corrective or transformative work applied to a performer's visible appearance. It may create an intentionally ordinary result, a stylized design, or a changing physical condition.

**Use.** Support characterization and camera rendering while maintaining a documented progression across scenes. Makeup is distinct from the full wardrobe, hairstyle, and any separate prosthetic appliances.

**Example.** Carefully tracked fading bruises help show that several story days have passed between scenes shot out of order.

### Hair
<!-- term-id: production-design-and-appearance.hair -->

**Definition.** The design and maintenance of a character's hairstyle, facial hair, wigs, and related hairpieces. Decisions include shape, grooming, texture, movement, and condition rather than only color or length.

**Use.** Establish routine, period, chosen presentation, and continuity across a story. Coordinate movement and silhouette with costume and camera tests.

**Example.** A meticulously pinned hairstyle loosens gradually during a long overnight shift, with each stage recorded for later coverage.

### Prosthetics
<!-- term-id: production-design-and-appearance.prosthetics -->

**Definition.** Applied pieces that alter a performer's apparent anatomy or surface, such as sculpted appliances for age, injury, creatures, or other transformations. In this context the term concerns performance makeup effects.

**Use.** Create repeatable three-dimensional changes that interact with light and movement. Design must consider expression, application, removal, and the needs of the performer.

**Example.** A subtle silicone cheek appliance gives a fictional survivor a consistent healed injury across several time periods.

### Silhouette
<!-- term-id: production-design-and-appearance.silhouette -->

**Definition.** The recognizable outer contour of a figure or object when internal detail is reduced or ignored. Appearance silhouette is a shape property; a lighting silhouette is an image in which the subject appears relatively dark.

**Use.** Make characters and objects distinguishable at distance or in brief views. Costume, hair, posture, and carried items all affect the contour.

**Example.** A courier's asymmetric satchel and short cape identify her before the camera reaches her face.

### Material
<!-- term-id: production-design-and-appearance.material -->

**Definition.** The substance an object is made from, or is designed to appear made from, such as wood, wool, glass, or steel. Actual fabrication material and fictional represented material may differ.

**Use.** Connect surfaces to resources, manufacturing, function, and sound. Record substitutions when a lightweight or flexible object must read as another substance.

**Example.** A lightweight scenic panel is finished to resemble heavy cast iron in an abandoned pumping station.

### Texture
<!-- term-id: production-design-and-appearance.texture -->

**Definition.** The visual or tactile character of a surface, including roughness, weave, grain, pattern, and small-scale variation. Texture is not identical to material: the same material can receive very different finishes.

**Use.** Add specificity and distinguish objects under the chosen lighting. Test whether fine patterns remain readable and stable at the intended camera distance.

**Example.** The director compares a polished wooden desk with a splintered version to determine which supports the room's history.

### Patina
<!-- term-id: production-design-and-appearance.patina -->

**Definition.** A surface character associated with age, exposure, repeated handling, or chemical change, whether naturally acquired or artificially created. Patina can enrich a surface without implying that the object is broken.

**Use.** Suggest a history of environment and care rather than applying uniform dirt everywhere. Place changes where use and exposure plausibly produce them.

**Example.** A brass door handle remains bright where hands touch it but darkens in its recessed ornament.

### Wear
<!-- term-id: production-design-and-appearance.wear -->

**Definition.** Deterioration or alteration caused by repeated use, contact, stress, or weathering. Wear includes fraying, scratches, fading, dents, and worn-through areas; it overlaps with patina but emphasizes use and damage.

**Use.** Reveal routines and changing conditions through specific contact points. Track new damage when it becomes a continuity fact.

**Example.** A delivery worker's right coat cuff is more worn because it repeatedly rubs against a bicycle grip.

## Visual organization

### Palette
<!-- term-id: production-design-and-appearance.palette -->

**Definition.** A selected family of colors and their relationships within an object, character, environment, or production. A palette includes relative prominence and placement, not merely a list of swatches.

**Use.** Coordinate departments and make meaningful departures visible. Separate colors built into costumes and sets from lighting color and later grading adjustments.

**Example.** A muted green workshop contains one orange safety cabinet whose color becomes associated with a concealed document.

### Shape language
<!-- term-id: production-design-and-appearance.shape-language -->

**Definition.** A recurring vocabulary of contours and forms, such as rounded enclosures, narrow verticals, broad wedges, or repeated arches. It is an art-direction description rather than a universal psychological code.

**Use.** Create family resemblance across architecture, props, and costume, or deliberately distinguish competing cultures and organizations.

**Example.** A municipal service uses circular windows, rounded badges, and curved desks, while a private contractor introduces severe rectangular replacements.

### Scale
<!-- term-id: production-design-and-appearance.scale -->

**Definition.** Size understood relative to people, neighboring objects, a reference dimension, or a model's represented world. Scale can describe actual construction and apparent size in the image, which need separate records.

**Use.** Shape bodily access, spatial relationships, and perceived institutional importance. Perspective tricks or miniatures may alter apparent scale without corresponding full-size construction.

**Example.** An oversized public counter forces visitors to look upward when asking the seated clerk for help.

### Density
<!-- term-id: production-design-and-appearance.density -->

**Definition.** The concentration of visible objects, detail, or occupied space within an area or frame. Density can be uneven, with a crowded corner beside a deliberately empty surface.

**Use.** Describe accumulation, activity, scarcity of space, or controlled order. Distinguish an overall busy environment from the density actually visible in a chosen shot.

**Example.** A collector's packed shelves stop abruptly around a blank rectangle where one important object was removed.

### Repetition
<!-- term-id: production-design-and-appearance.repetition -->

**Definition.** The recurrence of a form, object, color, or arrangement within a design. Repetition may be regular or approximate; its pattern can make an exception more noticeable.

**Use.** Suggest production systems, routines, institutional order, or shared ownership. Clarify whether the repeated element is simply practical or carries intended narrative emphasis.

**Example.** Identical numbered lockers line a corridor except for one handmade wooden replacement.

### Visual hierarchy
<!-- term-id: production-design-and-appearance.visual-hierarchy -->

**Definition.** The relative prominence of visible elements and the order in which a composition encourages attention. Size, contrast, placement, grouping, detail, and illumination can contribute together.

**Use.** Support the intended reading of a set or character without assuming that every viewer will look in the same order. Check hierarchy through the actual lens and frame.

**Example.** A bright white envelope on a nearly empty dark desk becomes easier to notice than the richly decorated walls.

## Coherence, history, and evidence

### Period authenticity
<!-- term-id: production-design-and-appearance.period-authenticity -->

**Definition.** The extent to which design choices are supported by evidence about a specified historical time, place, community, and use. It is contextual accuracy, not simply the presence of old-looking objects.

**Use.** Ground decisions in documented construction, clothing, availability, and habits. Identify deliberate exceptions and gaps in evidence instead of presenting guesses as verified facts.

**Example.** A shop interior uses researched regional packaging while marking an invented brand as a fictional substitution.

### Stylization
<!-- term-id: production-design-and-appearance.stylization -->

**Definition.** Deliberate selection, simplification, exaggeration, or transformation of visible reality according to a formal design approach. Stylization can coexist with accurate historical research and functional objects.

**Use.** Create a coherent visual world by deciding which qualities to amplify and which to omit. State the rule concretely rather than calling everything “cinematic.”

**Example.** A period train station retains plausible construction but simplifies all advertising to two colors and unusually large lettering.

### Lived-in detail
<!-- term-id: production-design-and-appearance.lived-in-detail -->

**Definition.** Specific evidence that a space or object has been used by particular people over time. It includes organization, repairs, adaptations, and omissions, rather than being synonymous with mess.

**Use.** Tie decoration to routines and biography. A carefully maintained room can feel inhabited when its arrangements reveal consistent purposes.

**Example.** A kettle handle has a cloth wrap, and the nearest cupboard contains matching replacement strips cut to size.

### Visual continuity
<!-- term-id: production-design-and-appearance.visual-continuity -->

**Definition.** Consistency, or deliberately tracked change, in visible details across shots and story time. It includes placement, condition, costume, hair, makeup, and the progressive effects of action.

**Use.** Let scenes shot separately describe a coherent event. Continuity records should follow fictional chronology as well as production order.

**Example.** A coat acquires a torn pocket during a chase, and every subsequent scene preserves that damage until a repair occurs.

## Practical distinctions

Set design determines an environment's organization; dressing specifies how that environment is furnished and occupied. Location dressing applies similar decisions to an existing place. A chair can be part of the dressing and also require prop coordination when an actor breaks it: production responsibilities need explicit agreement, not a rigid glossary boundary.

Material describes substance, texture describes surface character, patina describes accumulated appearance, and wear describes changes associated with use or deterioration. They can all be recorded for the same object.

Character appearance is collaborative. This chapter's grouping does not make costume or hair and makeup subordinate categories of the art department. The [British Film Designers Guild's role guide](https://britishfilmdesigners.com/resources/job-roles-explained/) is a useful primary professional reference for design and set-decoration responsibilities.

## Visual interaction and structured fields

These proposed interactions describe a future interface. They are not implemented tools, material simulations, or verified production specifications.

|---|---|---|
| Set or location treatment | Annotated plan with before/after reference images | `environment_id`, `design_intent`, `existing_features`, `planned_changes`, `reference_ids` |

| Surface history | Layered material and wear reference board | `actual_material`, `represented_material`, `texture_note`, `patina_note`, `wear_locations` |

| Dimensions | Optional dimension overlays | `width_m`, `height_m`, `depth_m`, `scale_ratio`, `measurement_status`; unknown values remain unset |
