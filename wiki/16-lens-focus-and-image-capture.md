# 16 · Lens, focus, and image capture

[Wiki home](../README.md) · [Alphabetical index](../INDEX.md)

Lens and capture choices work as a system. A focal length needs the active sensor or film area to describe field of view. A depth-of-field claim needs aperture, focus distance, framing conditions, and an acceptable-sharpness criterion. A frame rate needs a stated capture or playback role. Keep these dependencies visible instead of treating a number as a complete visual instruction.

The teaching examples are hypothetical. Formula examples use simplified, stated conditions rather than promising exact results for a particular lens or camera. Camera tests remain the practical check for distortion, focus breathing, flare, noise, and filter behavior.

## Lenses and framing

### Focal length
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.focal-length -->

**Definition.** An optical property conventionally stated in millimeters, not the physical barrel length. For a fixed active image area, a longer focal length generally yields a narrower field of view. [Nikon, understanding focal length](https://www.nikonusa.com/learn-and-explore/c/tips-and-techniques/understanding-focal-length).

**Use.** Specify lens choice together with format and camera position; focal length alone does not specify perspective or shot size.

**Example.** A 50 mm lens frames different amounts of the same room when the camera switches between full-width and cropped recording modes.

### Field of view
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.field-of-view -->

**Definition.** The extent of the scene captured, expressed as an angular span or a visible width and height at a stated distance. FOV depends on focal length and active image area, with lens projection and focus also relevant.

**Use.** Compare framing consistently by specifying horizontal, vertical, or diagonal angle. Do not present “35 mm” as a complete viewing-angle specification.

**Example.** Two camera modes use the same lens but show different horizontal spans because one crops the sensor.

### Wide-angle lens
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.wide-angle-lens -->

**Definition.** A lens providing a relatively broad angle of view for its capture format, generally with focal length shorter than that format's normal range. “Wide” is therefore format-dependent.

**Use.** Include more surrounding space or work from a nearby viewpoint. Apparent exaggeration of near-far size relationships comes from camera position, not focal length alone.

**Example.** In a cramped lift, a wide lens includes both passengers and the control panel from the available corner.

### Normal lens
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.normal-lens -->

**Definition.** A lens in the conventional middle range for a format, often near the image diagonal in focal length. Standard lens is a common alias; there is no single lens that reproduces the full human visual experience.

**Use.** Establish a reference point between wider and narrower framing options. Confirm format and desired camera distance rather than assuming “normal” means universally neutral.

**Example.** A filmmaker tests a standard-range lens for a conversation where modest environmental context remains useful.

### Telephoto lens
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.telephoto-lens -->

**Definition.** In common filmmaking usage, a relatively long lens giving a narrow field of view for the format. Strict optical usage also describes a design whose physical length is shorter than its effective focal length.

**Use.** Frame distant details or work from a farther viewpoint. So-called compression reflects camera distance when framing is matched.

**Example.** From across a courtyard, a long lens isolates a person at a window without moving the camera closer.

### Prime lens
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.prime-lens -->

**Definition.** A lens with one nominal focal length rather than an adjustable zoom range. Changing focus may still slightly change framing through focus breathing, so fixed focal length does not promise a perfectly fixed image.

**Use.** Choose a particular optical character and working setup. Primes vary greatly; the label alone guarantees neither superior sharpness nor a larger maximum aperture.

**Example.** A sequence uses a 40 mm prime, with camera position changed deliberately between setups.

### Zoom lens
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.zoom-lens -->

**Definition.** A lens offering an adjustable range of focal lengths. It permits reframing between takes or a visible focal-length change during a take; focus retention and aperture behavior depend on the design.

**Use.** Adjust framing without swapping lenses or create a deliberate zoom. Check whether the lens maintains focus while zooming rather than assuming it does.

**Example.** A locked camera gradually changes from 35 mm to 70 mm to isolate a figure across the street.

### Anamorphic lens
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.anamorphic-lens -->

**Definition.** A lens system that scales the image differently along horizontal and vertical axes, typically squeezing horizontal information for capture and requiring corresponding desqueezing for display. Squeeze factor and capture area must be recorded. [Canon, EOS C500 Mark II white paper, anamorphic shooting](https://downloads.canon.com/nw/learn/white-papers/cinema-eos/EOS_C500_MarkII_White_Paper.pdf).

**Use.** Explore its framing and optical character; oval blur or streak flares vary by design.

**Example.** A 2× anamorphic image is monitored desqueezed so performers' proportions appear correct.

## Aperture and focus

### Aperture
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.aperture -->

**Definition.** The lens opening controlling the cone of light admitted. F-number relates focal length to entrance-pupil diameter; a smaller f-number means a larger relative opening. Cinema T-stops account for light transmission. [Nikon, understanding aperture](https://www.nikonusa.com/learn-and-explore/c/tips-and-techniques/understanding-maximum-aperture).

**Use.** Balance exposure and depth of field with other settings. Smaller openings can increase depth of field but eventually increase diffraction effects.

**Example.** The crew stops down from f/2.8 to f/4 and compensates the reduced light separately.

### Depth of field
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.depth-of-field -->

**Definition.** The range of scene distances judged acceptably sharp around the focus distance. It depends on aperture, focal length, focus distance, and the sharpness criterion, which relates to format and viewing conditions. [Canon, EOS-1Ds manual, basic terminology](https://files.canon-europe.com/files/soft24318/manual/EOS1Ds_CUG_EN.pdf).

**Use.** Plan what must remain legible together. Comparisons must state which conditions stay fixed.

**Example.** A camera test checks whether a foreground letter and its reader remain sufficiently sharp at the chosen aperture.

### Shallow focus
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.shallow-focus -->

**Definition.** An image treatment in which only a limited depth range appears acceptably sharp, leaving other distances visibly out of focus. It is the resulting selective-focus condition, not a synonym for close-up.

**Use.** Concentrate detail or withhold background information while considering movement and focus tolerance. Background blur also depends on subject-background separation and magnification.

**Example.** A ring on the table is sharp while the person deciding whether to take it remains blurred behind.

### Deep focus
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.deep-focus -->

**Definition.** An image treatment that keeps substantially separated depth planes acceptably sharp at the same time. It describes a visual result, which may be achieved optically or through combined production techniques.

**Use.** Let foreground and background actions remain simultaneously readable. Deep focus does not mean every distance is perfectly sharp or that audience attention becomes uncontrolled.

**Example.** A child hides a letter near the lens while adults argue clearly in the far doorway.

### Rack focus
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.rack-focus -->

**Definition.** An intentional shift of focus distance during a shot, commonly transferring sharpness between subjects at different depths. Focus pull is a broader related term that also includes maintaining focus on a moving subject.

**Use.** Change which information is optically available without necessarily moving or cutting the camera. Lens breathing may change framing during the adjustment.

**Example.** Focus leaves the listener's face and settles on the visitor reflected in the mirror behind her.

### Focus distance
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.focus-distance -->

**Definition.** The distance from the camera's sensor or film plane to the plane of best focus for an ordinary flat-field setup. It is normally expressed in meters or feet and differs from distance measured from the lens front.

**Use.** Coordinate focus marks, moving subjects, and depth-of-field estimates. Confirm equipment conventions when close distances or unusual optics matter.

**Example.** A focus mark identifies the actor's final position two meters from the sensor-plane reference.

## Exposure and time

### Exposure
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.exposure -->

**Definition.** The light delivered to the recording surface over the exposure interval, and by extension the resulting brightness placement. Illumination, aperture, exposure time, and filtration affect collected light; digital ISO does not itself add photons.

**Use.** Protect the information needed for the intended image while coordinating motion blur and depth of field. Brightness in the final grade is a separate adjustment.

**Example.** Neutral-density filtration reduces incoming light while preserving the chosen aperture and shutter timing.

### Frame rate
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.frame-rate -->

**Definition.** A count of images per second, expressed in fps. Capture frame rate describes acquisition; playback or project frame rate describes presentation. Matching or differing rates produce different timing outcomes.

**Use.** Specify both values when motion speed matters. A high capture rate does not automatically create slow playback. [Sony, slow and quick motion](https://www.sony.com/electronics/support/articles/00286854).

**Example.** A clip captured at 48 fps plays in real time at 48 fps or half speed when conformed frame-for-frame to 24 fps.

### Shutter angle
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.shutter-angle -->

**Definition.** Exposure duration expressed as a fraction of a frame cycle using degrees. Under ordinary constant-rate operation, exposure time in seconds equals `shutter_angle_deg / (360 × capture_fps)`. [ARRI, electronic and mirror shutter white paper](https://www.arri.com/resource/blob/186770/ad23969317dafff402f0902d47bb4ab7/alexa-studio-electronic-and-mirror-shutter-white-paper-data.pdf).

**Use.** Relate exposure time to capture rate and motion rendition. It is not a rotation of the camera.

**Example.** At 24 fps, a 180° shutter exposes each frame for approximately 1/48 second.

### Motion blur
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.motion-blur -->

**Definition.** Image smearing produced when scene detail moves across the recording surface during an exposure. It depends on exposure duration, relative motion, magnification, and direction, not merely on playback frame rate.

**Use.** Shape motion rendition while retaining necessary detail. Shorter exposure intervals generally reduce blur for the same motion. [ARRI, shutter white paper](https://www.arri.com/resource/blob/186770/ad23969317dafff402f0902d47bb4ab7/alexa-studio-electronic-and-mirror-shutter-white-paper-data.pdf).

**Example.** A spinning wheel produces longer streaks when the exposure interval increases at the same capture rate.

### Slow motion
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.slow-motion -->

**Definition.** Presentation in which an event occupies more screen time than its original duration. Capturing more frames per second than are played frame-for-frame is one method; post-production interpolation or duplication are others.

**Use.** Reveal brief actions or reshape temporal emphasis. Preserve capture and playback rates so the intended speed can be reproduced. [Sony, high-frame-rate shooting](https://helpguide.sony.net/dsc/1630/v1/en/contents/TP0000829893.html).

**Example.** One second captured at 120 fps becomes five seconds when all frames play at 24 fps.

### Time-lapse
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.time-lapse -->

**Definition.** A sequence captured at widely spaced intervals and presented faster, making a long process unfold in a shorter viewing time. Capture interval and exposure duration are separate quantities.

**Use.** Reveal gradual change, such as construction or shifting weather. Plan the desired screen duration from the number of captured images and playback rate.

**Example.** Recording one frame every ten seconds for an hour yields 360 frames, or fifteen seconds at 24 fps.

## Format and image character

### Sensor or film format
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.sensor-or-film-format -->

**Definition.** The recording medium's size and configuration, especially the active image area used for a shot. A named format, film gauge, or sensor model may allow multiple recording gates, crops, and aspect ratios.

**Use.** Interpret lens coverage and field of view using active dimensions, not resolution alone. ARRI distinguishes sensor, recording format, and target frame. [ARRI, frame-line tool](https://www.arri.com/en/learn-help/learn-help-camera-system/tools/frame-line-lens-illumination-tool).

**Example.** A camera's cropped high-speed mode changes framing even though the mounted lens remains the same.

### Grain
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.grain -->

**Definition.** The visible granular texture associated with photographic film's image structure, or a deliberately added simulation of it. Apparent grain varies with stock, exposure, processing, enlargement, scanning, and scene content. [Kodak, VISION3 250D technical information](https://www.kodak.com/content/products-brochures/motion-picture/KODAK-VISION3-250D-5207-7207-technical-information.pdf).

**Use.** Discuss texture separately from sharpness and compression artifacts. Record whether grain originated in capture or was added later.

**Example.** A digital sequence receives a tested grain treatment matched across bright walls and darker clothing.

### Digital noise
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.digital-noise -->

**Definition.** Unwanted variation in recorded pixel values arising from photon statistics, sensor electronics, and processing. It may appear as luminance variation, colored speckles, or patterns; it is not the same physical process as film grain.

**Use.** Assess exposure and processing through representative footage. Low signal and shadow lifting can make noise more visible. [ARRI, dynamic-range white paper](https://www.arri.com/resource/blob/295460/e10ff8a5b3abf26c33f8754379b57442/2022-09-28-arri-dynamic-range-whitepaper-data.pdf).

**Example.** A dim corridor test reveals colored fluctuations in the shadows when the image is brightened for the intended grade.

### Lens distortion
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.lens-distortion -->

**Definition.** Optical deviation from the intended geometric mapping, commonly visible as barrel, pincushion, or more complex bending of straight lines in a rectilinear image. It differs from viewpoint-dependent perspective. [Canon, correcting distortion](https://global.canon/en/c-museum/special/exhibition1.html).

**Use.** Evaluate architecture, faces near frame edges, and matching across lenses. Correction may alter framing and require cropping.

**Example.** A grid filmed during a lens test reveals that straight door frames bow outward near the image edges.

### Diffusion
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.diffusion -->

**Definition.** In lens and capture discussion, optical filtration that redistributes some light to alter fine detail, highlight glow, or contrast. Effects vary substantially among filters; diffusion does not necessarily mean deliberate defocus. [Tiffen, diffusion guide](https://tiffen.com/pages/diffusion-guide).

**Use.** Test the intended image character under actual highlights, lens settings, and subject distances. Lighting diffusion is a different intervention.

**Example.** A filter gently spreads bright practical lamps while the actor's eye remains accurately focused.

### Flare
<!-- term-id: lens-focus-and-image-capture.flare -->

**Definition.** Unwanted or deliberately welcomed light spread, reflection, or scattering within an optical system, producing veiling brightness, ghosts, streaks, or other artifacts. A bright source can cause flare from within or outside the frame.

**Use.** Consider flare as part of lens character while monitoring contrast and essential detail. Its appearance depends on lens, coating, filtration, angle, and light level.

**Example.** As a car turns, a streetlamp briefly creates a translucent reflection across the windshield view.

## Practical distinctions and simple calculations

For an ideal rectilinear lens focused sufficiently far away, horizontal angular field of view can be approximated by `2 × atan(active_image_width_mm / (2 × focal_length_mm))`. Convert radians to degrees if needed. This simplified geometry is not a calibration for anamorphic projection, distortion, close-focus behavior, or digital correction. [Zeiss-hosted paper, *Optical design of camera optics for mobile phones*](https://lenspire.zeiss.com/photo/app/uploads/2018/02/Advanced-Optical-Technologies-Optical-design-of-camera-optics-for-mobile-phones.pdf).

Perspective is governed by viewpoint. Matching subject size with a wide lens nearby and a long lens farther away changes perspective because the camera moved. Cropping a fixed view does not reproduce that move. [Nikon, choosing lenses: perspective](https://www.nikonimgsupport.com/na/NSG_article?articleNo=000047395&configured=1&lang=en_SG).

Depth-of-field comparisons need a fixed basis. At the same focal length, focus distance, and sharpness criterion, stopping down generally increases depth of field. If camera position, focal length, or output enlargement also changes, a one-variable slogan no longer describes the whole comparison. [Canon, EOS-1Ds manual](https://files.canon-europe.com/files/soft24318/manual/EOS1Ds_CUG_EN.pdf).

For frame-for-frame conforming, `playback_duration_s = capture_duration_s × capture_fps / playback_fps`. This describes retiming, not a rule that all high-frame-rate footage must be slowed. Sound and motion-processing choices require separate treatment.

## Visual interaction and structured fields

These are proposals for a future interface. Label approximate simulations and require the missing inputs for calculations; unknown technical values should remain unknown.

| Decision | Visual interaction | Proposed data fields |
|---|---|---|
| Lens and field of view | Paired frame preview with a fixed camera-position option | `focal_length_mm`, `active_image_width_mm`, `active_image_height_mm`, `projection_type`, `anamorphic_squeeze_ratio` |
| Perspective | Top view showing camera and subject distances | `camera_position_m`, `subject_positions_m`, `coordinate_space`, `subject_distance_m` |
| Focus | Depth planes and time-based focus marks | `focus_distance_m`, `focus_keyframes`, `f_number`, `circle_of_confusion_mm`, `sharpness_criterion_note` |
| Exposure | Reference frame with waveform or exposure notes | `f_number`, `t_stop`, `exposure_time_s`, `iso_setting`, `nd_stops`, `exposure_intent` |
| Capture and playback | Two time rulers connected by a speed ratio | `capture_fps`, `playback_fps`, `retime_method`, `capture_duration_s`, `playback_duration_s` |
| Time-lapse | Interval schedule and duration calculator | `capture_interval_s`, `exposure_time_s`, `frame_count`, `playback_fps` |
| Image character | Controlled comparison frames with processing provenance | `lens_model`, `filter_type`, `filter_strength_label`, `grain_source`, `noise_processing_note`, `test_reference_id` |
