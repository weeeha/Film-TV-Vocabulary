# 07 · Character archetypes and construction

[Wiki home](../README.md) · [Alphabetical index](../INDEX.md)

Archetypes offer recognizable patterns of motivation or behavior. Character construction gives a particular person specific desires, habits, contradictions, circumstances, and choices. Use the pattern to ask better questions, then preserve the details that make the person exceed it.

This chapter does not reproduce a single archetype system or claim that these labels are scientifically established personality types. Carol S. Pearson explicitly identifies her twelve-archetype model as her own developed framework and discusses different names across its applications. Store the named framework when using one; do not relabel every popular twelve-part list as Jung's. [Pearson's account of her system](https://carolspearson.com/about/the-pearson-12-archetype-system-human-development-and-evolution)

## Archetypal patterns

### Mentor
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.mentor -->

**Definition.** A pattern organized around guiding another person's learning, development, or passage into a new situation. A mentor can offer practical skill, perspective, challenge, or a cautionary example; wisdom, kindness, advanced age, and infallibility are not necessary features.

**Use.** Make learning relational and give guidance its own motives and limits.

**Example.** A retired navigator teaches an apprentice to read currents but refuses to discuss the voyage on which she abandoned her own crew.

### Trickster
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.trickster -->

**Definition.** A pattern that disrupts expectations, rules, status, or categories through deception, play, ingenuity, or transgression. Tricksters can expose hypocrisy or create harm, and their cultural meanings vary; the label should not reduce distinct traditions to a single comic personality.

**Use.** Test the stability of a social order by letting someone manipulate its assumptions.

**Example.** A street performer gains entry to a restricted council meeting by staging an official-looking ceremony that no official wants to admit misunderstanding.

### Guardian
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.guardian -->

**Definition.** A pattern centered on protecting a person, place, value, boundary, or entrusted object. Protection can be generous, controlling, necessary, or outdated; a guardian differs from a gatekeeper when the emphasis falls on sustained care or defense rather than access alone.

**Use.** Create conflict over what preservation requires and who decides what is worth protecting.

**Example.** A keeper prevents anyone entering a damaged observatory, including the astronomer whose research might explain why it is still moving.

### Rebel
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.rebel -->

**Definition.** A pattern organized around resisting an established authority, norm, hierarchy, or expectation. Rebellion can seek reform, independence, replacement, or destruction; opposition alone does not establish that the rebel's aims are just or that their methods are liberating.

**Use.** Clarify what rule is challenged and what the character proposes or risks in its place.

**Example.** An apprentice refuses the guild's hereditary licenses but discovers that abolishing them would also remove workers' strongest protection against unsafe commissions.

### Caregiver
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.caregiver -->

**Definition.** A pattern organized around sustaining others through practical assistance, protection, attention, or emotional support. Caregiving can express love and competence while also producing exhaustion, control, resentment, or unequal obligation; it is not confined to a gender or family role.

**Use.** Make the labor and boundaries of care visible.

**Example.** A cook feeds every stranded traveler but finally refuses to continue when the rescue committee treats his work as an unlimited free resource.

### Explorer
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.explorer -->

**Definition.** A pattern driven by encountering unfamiliar places, possibilities, knowledge, or ways of living. Exploration can concern inner or social territory as well as travel; curiosity does not automatically justify intrusion, possession, or ignoring people already living in the explored place.

**Use.** Put discovery in tension with attachment, obligation, and the consequences of entering new territory.

**Example.** A cartographer finds an unrecorded settlement whose residents ask her to leave it off the map.

### Ruler
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.ruler -->

**Definition.** A pattern centered on organizing, directing, or maintaining a collective order. Rulers may seek stewardship, stability, recognition, or control; holding authority does not establish competence, and ruling can take place within a household, business, or informal group.

**Use.** Test how a character handles delegation, legitimacy, dissent, and accountability.

**Example.** A volunteer coordinator keeps an evacuation orderly until she must decide whether another person's better plan is worth surrendering command.

### Outsider
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.outsider -->

**Definition.** A pattern involving distance from or exclusion by a relevant group, place, institution, or norm. Outsider status is contextual and can be imposed, chosen, temporary, or uneven; someone excluded in one setting may hold considerable authority in another.

**Use.** Reveal assumptions that insiders no longer notice and the costs of limited belonging.

**Example.** A newly arrived engineer sees why the town's repair custom fails, but her accurate explanation cannot overcome distrust until she learns its history.

### Innocent
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.innocent -->

**Definition.** A pattern associated with trust, openness, idealism, or limited experience of a particular world's dangers and compromises. Innocence can be a stance rather than an age, and it does not require stupidity, helplessness, moral perfection, or ignorance of every difficulty.

**Use.** Test what happens when generous expectations encounter contradictory evidence.

**Example.** A new clerk assumes that correcting a documented mistake will be welcomed and gradually discovers why several departments depend on keeping it wrong.

### Sage
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.sage -->

**Definition.** A pattern organized around seeking, preserving, interpreting, or sharing knowledge and judgment. A sage may be insightful in one field and mistaken in another; the label should not substitute for showing evidence, uncertainty, or the limits of expertise.

**Use.** Put understanding into tension with action and responsibility.

**Example.** An archivist can explain how the flood-control system failed but must decide whether preserving the records matters more than helping residents escape immediately.

## Other character patterns

### Antihero
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.antihero -->

**Definition.** A central character who lacks, resists, or complicates qualities conventionally associated with heroic figures, such as courage, nobility, selflessness, or moral clarity. Usage is broad; an antihero can be timid or compromised without being equivalent to a villainous protagonist.

**Use.** Explore action through someone whose methods or limitations trouble straightforward admiration.

**Example.** A smuggler exposes a corrupt inspector only to cancel his own debt, then finds that protecting witnesses costs more than remaining silent.

### Tragic hero
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.tragic-hero -->

**Definition.** A central figure whose consequential action participates in a tragic outcome involving grave loss, suffering, or downfall. Traditions differ; avoid treating a single fatal personality flaw as a universal requirement, since error, limited knowledge, conflicting duties, and circumstance can also matter.

**Use.** Connect a person's understandable commitments to consequences they cannot fully control.

**Example.** A captain refuses to abandon passengers during a storm and loses the boat that was the island's only route to medical care.

### Reluctant hero
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.reluctant-hero -->

**Definition.** A character who resists taking on a consequential protective or corrective role before acting within it. Reluctance can arise from fear, competing duties, mistrust, exhaustion, or principled objections; initial refusal should not automatically be dismissed as cowardice.

**Use.** Give commitment a meaningful cost and show what changes the character's decision.

**Example.** A former rescuer refuses another mission until she learns that the organizers have finally accepted the safety conditions she demanded after her last loss.

### Unreliable narrator
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.unreliable-narrator -->

**Definition.** A narrator whose account gives the audience reason to question its factual accuracy, interpretation, omissions, or judgments. Unreliability may be deliberate or unknowing and does not require mental illness; limited knowledge alone becomes unreliability only in relation to what the telling claims.

**Use.** Build a productive gap between the account offered and evidence available elsewhere.

**Example.** A retired captain insists she never left the harbor, while her story repeatedly includes details only visible from the departing vessel.

## Inner construction

### Want
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.want -->

**Definition.** An outcome, experience, possession, or condition a character desires, often consciously enough to pursue. Wants can conflict, remain partly unspoken, or change by scene; they are not restricted to external objects and need not always oppose a deeper need.

**Use.** Connect motivation to a specific action the character attempts now.

**Example.** The apprentice wants a solo assignment because she seeks both professional recognition and a day beyond her mentor's supervision.

### Need
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.need -->

**Definition.** Something the character requires or that a particular interpretation identifies as necessary for their well-being, effectiveness, or development. In some arc models, need names an unrecognized inner change; this is an optional lens, not an objective diagnosis or universal story requirement.

**Use.** Test whether achieving the stated want addresses the underlying difficulty.

**Example.** An apprentice wins independence yet discovers she still needs a way to ask for help without experiencing it as humiliation.

### Fear
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.fear -->

**Definition.** An anticipated harm, loss, exposure, or experience that influences a character's attention and choices. Fear can be reasonable, exaggerated, concealed, or contradictory; it should be specified through what the person expects will happen rather than used as a generic weakness.

**Use.** Explain avoidance, overpreparation, aggression, hesitation, or costly acts of courage.

**Example.** A dispatcher checks every address three times because she fears sending rescuers to the wrong building again.

### Belief
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.belief -->

**Definition.** A proposition a character treats as true about themselves, others, or the world. A belief may be explicit or revealed through behavior, and it can be accurate in one context yet harmful when generalized; it need not be a falsehood awaiting correction.

**Use.** Make interpretations and decisions consistent enough to be tested by events.

**Example.** A captain believes asking passengers for help destroys their confidence, so she hides a repair problem until it becomes impossible to manage alone.

### Value
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.value -->

**Definition.** Something a character considers important or worthy of protection, such as loyalty, independence, fairness, competence, or comfort. Values differ from factual beliefs and can conflict within one person; declared priorities may diverge from what choices reveal.

**Use.** Design decisions that force the character to rank commitments under pressure.

**Example.** A clerk who values honesty and family loyalty must decide whether to correct a report that would expose her brother's misconduct.

### Contradiction
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.contradiction -->

**Definition.** A meaningful tension between aspects of a character, such as stated values and behavior, public manner and private action, or different commitments. Contradiction can produce depth when its context is understandable; accidental inconsistency is not automatically complex characterization.

**Use.** Identify circumstances that reveal different but connected sides of the person.

**Example.** A supervisor dismisses sentiment in every meeting but keeps repairing the office chair used by the colleague she refused to promote.

### Flaw
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.flaw -->

**Definition.** A trait, habit, limitation, or pattern of judgment that creates difficulty in the character's circumstances. What counts as a flaw depends on context and interpretation; disability, difference, vulnerability, or victimization should not automatically be treated as moral failure.

**Use.** Connect a specific repeated behavior to concrete consequences.

**Example.** A skilled negotiator always postpones giving bad news, turning manageable disagreements into crises that others must resolve without preparation.

### Strength
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.strength -->

**Definition.** A capacity, habit, relationship, or quality that helps a character act effectively in relevant circumstances. Strength is contextual rather than an unlimited advantage; the same persistence that enables a rescue can become damaging when it prevents accepting new evidence.

**Use.** Give the character credible resources and test their limits.

**Example.** A mechanic's patience lets her repair an obsolete pump, but spending too long on it leaves less time to evacuate the workshop.

### Secret
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.secret -->

**Definition.** Information a character conceals from specified people, whether through silence, deception, or controlled access. A secret is relational: someone may know it while others do not, and its dramatic importance depends on why disclosure would change choices or relationships.

**Use.** Track knowledge, concealment tactics, and the cost of maintaining or revealing it.

**Example.** The dispatcher knows the emergency route crosses private land because she helped remove that fact from the public map.

### Formative experience
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.formative-experience -->

**Definition.** A past event or sustained condition that significantly shaped a character's habits, assumptions, attachments, or skills. It can be ordinary, joyful, difficult, or traumatic; a single backstory incident should not mechanically explain every present behavior.

**Use.** Give current patterns a particular history without reducing the person to an origin story.

**Example.** Years repairing radios with his grandmother taught a technician both patient listening and the expectation that every object deserves another chance.

## Agency

### Initiative
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.initiative -->

**Definition.** The act of beginning, proposing, or redirecting an action rather than only responding within another person's plan. Initiative is one expression of agency, but constrained or reactive characters can still make meaningful choices about how to respond.

**Use.** Show what the character chooses to set in motion and accepts responsibility for starting.

**Example.** Before receiving orders, an apprentice recruits residents to mark which apartments still contain people who need help.

### Choice
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.choice -->

**Definition.** A selection among perceived possibilities, including refusal, delay, or accepting a constraint. A meaningful story choice changes action or reveals priorities; available options can be narrow, unequal, or coercively shaped without becoming equivalent to complete freedom.

**Use.** Make the character's participation in events visible within their actual circumstances.

**Example.** A detained witness cannot leave, but decides to name the official who ordered her arrest instead of repeating the statement prepared for her.

### Resistance
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.resistance -->

**Definition.** An action or sustained stance that opposes pressure, control, change, or another person's demand. Resistance can be open, indirect, collective, or quiet; it is not inherently virtuous, since characters may resist accountability as readily as oppression.

**Use.** Show how a character preserves a boundary or contests the terms offered.

**Example.** A clerk follows every instruction exactly, forcing a corrupt supervisor to put an unlawful demand in writing rather than relying on implication.

### Complicity
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.complicity -->

**Definition.** Participation in, assistance to, or enabling of an action or system that causes harm or wrongdoing. Degrees of knowledge, power, coercion, benefit, and alternatives matter; a story should establish those conditions before treating every participant as equally accountable.

**Use.** Explore the difference between private objection and behavior that sustains a system.

**Example.** An engineer objects to unsafe repairs but continues signing completion forms so her department will retain its funding.

### Responsibility
<!-- term-id: character-archetypes-and-construction.responsibility -->

**Definition.** A character's connection to obligations and consequences arising from their choices, role, knowledge, or capacity to act. Causal involvement, assigned duty, and moral accountability can differ; the character's own account may not match what others believe they owe.

**Use.** Make agency include what happens after an action, not merely the moment of decision.

**Example.** After authorizing an improvised rescue, the dispatcher publicly accepts the inquiry instead of letting the apprentice who carried it out take all the blame.

## Practical distinctions

An archetype is a pattern, a role is a story function, and a trait is a particular tendency. A mentor can be a protagonist, rival, or antagonist; a caregiver can behave selfishly; a rebel can defend a deeply conservative value. Attach labels to evidence and allow exceptions.

Want and need are optional interpretive tools. John August explicitly critiques their usefulness as a universal planning method and favors asking what explains the character's current action. This wiki retains both terms because they are common, while allowing a project to omit the distinction. [Rethinking motivation](https://johnaugust.com/2008/rethinking-motivation)

Agency is not omnipotence or constant initiative. A person with limited power can choose what to reveal, resist, preserve, or accept. Record real constraints and alternatives before judging a choice. Backstory supports present action but does not replace the need to dramatize it.

## Visual interaction and structured fields

These proposals are for fictional-character development. They are not implemented personality assessments or psychological measurements.

| Decision | Visual interaction | Proposed data fields |
|---|---|---|
| Use a pattern provisionally | Archetype cards with framework and exception notes | `archetype_ids`, `framework_name`, `framework_source_url`, `exceptions` |
| Explain a current action | Linked desire, belief, constraint, and action cards | `want`, `interpreted_need`, `beliefs`, `constraints`, `action_reason` |
| Test a contradiction | Compare stated values with choices in selected scenes | `declared_values`, `observed_choices`, `contradiction_note`, `evidence_scene_ids` |
| Track concealed information | Knowledge map showing who knows what and when | `secret_id`, `known_by`, `concealed_from`, `disclosure_event_id` |
| Describe constrained agency | Branch cards with available options and their costs | `available_options`, `coercion_context`, `chosen_action`, `responsibility_note` |
