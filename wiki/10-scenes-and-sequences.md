# 10 · Scenes and sequences

[Wiki home](../README.md) · [Alphabetical index](../INDEX.md)

A scene can be described along several independent dimensions: what happens, what changes, who wants what, what the audience learns, and where the event sits in a larger sequence. Select several labels when useful. A meal can be a negotiation, an introduction, and the start of a betrayal.

The categories below are an editorial working vocabulary. A contemplative or observational scene does not need a conventional conflict-and-reversal pattern to justify its existence.

## Units of action

### Scene
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.scene -->

**Definition.** A unit of dramatic or observational action, usually organized around a relatively continuous situation in place and time. Screenplay scene headings and analytical scene boundaries do not always coincide exactly.

**Use.** Give an event a manageable identity for writing, staging, revising, and coverage.

**Example.** A visitor's attempt to obtain a permit unfolds in one office, with several changes of tactic before departure.

### Sequence
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.sequence -->

**Definition.** A larger unit made from connected scenes or passages that develop a shared action, question, or pattern. A sequence can move across locations, times, and participants.

**Use.** Organize progression beyond the needs of one local encounter.

**Example.** Researching a route, acquiring supplies, crossing a canal, and reaching a locked terminal form one expedition sequence.

## Dramatic functions

### Introduction
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.introduction -->

**Definition.** A passage that gives the audience an initial relationship to a character, place, object, system, or problem. It establishes selective expectations rather than needing to provide a complete biography.

**Use.** Choose the first behavior or detail that makes later developments meaningful.

**Example.** A mechanic quietly repairs a stranger's broken bag before the story reveals that she refuses paid work.

### Exposition
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.exposition -->

**Definition.** Information supplied so an audience can understand circumstances, relationships, history, or rules. It can emerge through action, dialogue, imagery, sound, or an explicit explanation.

**Use.** Provide the knowledge needed for an upcoming choice without halting the scene's other activity.

**Example.** A customer tries to pay with obsolete currency, revealing a changed economy through an immediate transaction.

### Discovery
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.discovery -->

**Definition.** An event in which a character encounters something previously unknown or unrecognized. Discovery describes the character's experience; the audience may already possess the information.

**Use.** Let new evidence change behavior, goals, or relationships.

**Example.** A courier opens a supposedly empty locker and finds a recent meal, realizing someone still occupies the abandoned station.

### Confrontation
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.confrontation -->

**Definition.** An encounter in which incompatible positions, desires, or accusations become directly engaged. It can be quiet, indirect in language, or physically restrained while remaining dramatically confrontational.

**Use.** Bring an existing conflict into a situation that requires response.

**Example.** During a routine inventory, a worker places the missing equipment receipt in front of the supervisor and waits.

### Negotiation
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.negotiation -->

**Definition.** An exchange in which participants attempt to reach or reshape an agreement through offers, refusals, threats, concessions, or persuasion. Bargaining power may change during the encounter.

**Use.** Make competing needs visible and reveal what each participant is willing to trade.

**Example.** A ferry owner offers passage only if the traveler repairs the engine before departure.

### Confession
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.confession -->

**Definition.** An admission of concealed responsibility, experience, desire, or knowledge to another person or audience. The admission may be truthful, partial, coerced, or self-serving.

**Use.** Shift trust and consequences through the act of disclosure, not just through new information.

**Example.** A guard admits that she let the missing child through the gate, then reveals she has been searching alone.

### Decision
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.decision -->

**Definition.** A moment in which a character commits to a course of action, including refusal or deliberate inaction. A decision becomes legible through behavior and consequences, not necessarily a spoken announcement.

**Use.** Turn deliberation into direction and locate agency in the story.

**Example.** After listening to both proposals, the engineer hands her only spare battery to the rescue team.

### Reversal
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.reversal -->

**Definition.** A change that redirects a situation or reorders advantage, expectation, or interpretation. A reversal requires a meaningful before-and-after relationship; it is more specific than any new event.

**Use.** Force participants to reconsider their plan or understanding.

**Example.** A suspect invited to answer questions produces evidence that puts the investigator under suspicion instead.

### Payoff
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.payoff -->

**Definition.** A later event that makes an earlier detail, promise, pattern, or preparation consequential. A payoff can fulfill an expectation, transform it, or reveal a previously hidden relevance.

**Use.** Give earlier material a satisfying relationship to current action.

**Example.** A casual lesson in repairing a hand pump becomes the skill that lets a frightened apprentice help during a flood.

### Aftermath
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.aftermath -->

**Definition.** A passage attending to the consequences of a significant event: damage, relief, grief, reorganization, responsibility, or changed relationships. It can introduce new problems as well as register completed action.

**Use.** Give consequences time to become emotionally and practically legible.

**Example.** After a successful evacuation, residents discover that their names were never recorded and begin searching for one another.

## Activities and encounter types

### Chase
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.chase -->

**Definition.** An action in which one participant attempts to catch another who is actively evading capture. Its dramatic clarity depends on pursuit, distance, obstacles, and changing opportunities.

**Use.** Turn a goal into immediate spatial pressure while revealing tactics and costs.

**Example.** A messenger escapes across crowded walkways while the pursuer uses a parallel maintenance route to intercept her.

### Interrogation
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.interrogation -->

**Definition.** A sustained attempt to obtain information through questioning under an unequal or coercive power relationship. The questioned person can still influence the exchange through silence, deception, or selective disclosure.

**Use.** Explore control over both knowledge and the conditions of speaking.

**Example.** A detained technician answers every question except the one that would identify a colleague.

### Meal
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.meal -->

**Definition.** A scene organized around preparing, distributing, eating, or clearing food. Shared routine supplies behavior, timing, and social expectations through which another dramatic function can unfold.

**Use.** Reveal care, hierarchy, exclusion, familiarity, or scarcity through concrete actions.

**Example.** A family welcomes a visitor warmly but quietly serves her the portion normally reserved for an absent relative.

### Journey
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.journey -->

**Definition.** Movement between places treated as meaningful action rather than an omitted connection. A journey can emphasize obstacles, encounters, transformation, or observation along the route.

**Use.** Develop geography and character through the process of reaching a destination.

**Example.** Two rivals must carry a fragile instrument across the city and gradually develop a shared rhythm of cooperation.

### Battle
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.battle -->

**Definition.** An organized or sustained physical conflict involving opposing forces. It can be depicted through individual experience, strategic overview, fragmented impressions, or some combination.

**Use.** Clarify the participants' immediate objectives and the consequences of changing control.

**Example.** A defensive group tries to keep a corridor open long enough for residents to leave, giving each advance a specific purpose.

### Escape
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.escape -->

**Definition.** Action directed toward leaving confinement, danger, control, or another restrictive situation. An escape need not include an active pursuer at every moment.

**Use.** Organize obstacles around the difference between apparent freedom and actual safety.

**Example.** Leaving the locked building is only the first step; the characters must then cross an illuminated yard without their access badges.

### Reunion
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.reunion -->

**Definition.** An encounter between people separated by time, distance, estrangement, or presumed loss. Its tension often comes from the gap between remembered relationships and present circumstances.

**Use.** Make changed identities and unresolved expectations visible through recognition and response.

**Example.** A returning brother uses an old nickname, but his sister introduces him formally to her new community.

### Farewell
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.farewell -->

**Definition.** An encounter marking departure or anticipated separation. It may be explicit, disguised as ordinary activity, interrupted, or recognized only afterward.

**Use.** Give an ending emotional specificity through what participants say, withhold, exchange, or leave unfinished.

**Example.** A mentor gives an apprentice the workshop key while pretending it is merely inconvenient to carry.

### Ritual
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.ritual -->

**Definition.** A scene centered on a patterned act with shared meaning, such as a ceremony, initiation, commemoration, or repeated communal practice. Its dramatic function depends on participation and deviation.

**Use.** Place personal conflict inside public expectations.

**Example.** Everyone recites the annual pledge except the person nominated to lead the next ceremony.

## Information and interpretation

### Reveal
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.reveal -->

**Definition.** The presentation of previously withheld information to an audience or character. A reveal describes the information event; the camera, edit, performance, or dialogue supplies its delivery.

**Use.** Decide precisely who learns what and whether the revelation changes interpretation or action.

**Example.** A wide frame shows that the friendly radio operator has been working inside the rival station all along.

### Concealment
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.concealment -->

**Definition.** Keeping relevant information unavailable to a character or audience through circumstances or presentation. Concealment can create curiosity but should remain consistent with the chosen viewpoint and situation.

**Use.** Control the timing of understanding while leaving enough information for engagement.

**Example.** A camera follows a delivered parcel but does not reveal its contents until its recipient opens it.

### Misdirection
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.misdirection -->

**Definition.** Guiding attention or interpretation toward a plausible but incomplete or incorrect explanation. It differs from merely omitting information because it actively encourages another reading.

**Use.** Build an alternative expectation that later evidence can revise.

**Example.** Repeated attention to a damaged lock suggests a break-in, while the crucial clue is that nothing inside was disturbed.

### Dramatic irony
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.dramatic-irony -->

**Definition.** A difference in knowledge through which the audience understands a situation or statement in a way that one or more characters do not. Its effect can be comic, painful, suspenseful, or mixed.

**Use.** Give ordinary actions additional significance without changing their surface behavior.

**Example.** The audience has seen a resignation letter while the supervisor enthusiastically discusses the employee's future promotion.

### Recognition
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.recognition -->

**Definition.** A change from not knowing or misreading something to understanding its identity, meaning, or relationship. Recognition can reorganize the significance of information already present.

**Use.** Let a character's interpretation become an event rather than supplying only another fact.

**Example.** An engineer recognizes a familiar repair pattern in an anonymous machine and realizes her missing teacher built it.

## Movement within a scene

### Objective
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.objective -->

**Definition.** The immediate outcome a participant is trying to achieve in the current encounter. It is often more specific than the character's larger story goal.

**Use.** Give performance and dialogue a direction that can be pursued through changing tactics.

**Example.** A traveler ultimately wants to find her father; in this scene she wants the clerk to reveal a departure record.

### Obstacle
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.obstacle -->

**Definition.** A person, condition, rule, limitation, or internal resistance that interferes with an objective. Obstacles can change without requiring a new location or antagonist.

**Use.** Make effort visible and prevent an objective from resolving without meaningful action.

**Example.** The clerk agrees to help, but the record room has no power and closes in ten minutes.

### Tactic
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.tactic -->

**Definition.** A specific method used to pursue an objective: charm, bargain, intimidate, evade, reassure, challenge, or another playable action. Tactics can fail and be replaced within one exchange.

**Use.** Give a scene internal variation without changing its overall purpose.

**Example.** After an appeal to duty fails, the traveler offers to repair the clerk's broken radio.

### Turning point
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.turning-point -->

**Definition.** A moment after which the encounter proceeds on meaningfully different terms. It may arise from information, a decision, an arrival, a failed tactic, or a change of power.

**Use.** Identify where the scene's direction changes and give that change adequate emphasis.

**Example.** The clerk recognizes the name on the traveler's document and immediately locks the office door.

### Changed situation
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.changed-situation -->

**Definition.** The difference between the conditions at the beginning and end of a passage: knowledge, commitment, access, power, relationships, or practical circumstances. This is a useful analysis field rather than a fixed industry category.

**Use.** Describe what the scene contributes beyond its surface activity.

**Example.** The traveler leaves without the record but with an unexpected ally and a new deadline.

## Sequence patterns

### Preparation
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.preparation -->

**Definition.** A sequence arranging the knowledge, resources, relationships, and commitments required for a later action. Preparation can establish expectations that the execution fulfills or disrupts.

**Use.** Make a complex plan understandable while showing disagreements and vulnerabilities.

**Example.** A crew rehearses a salvage route, discovers a missing tool, and recruits someone whose local knowledge changes the plan.

### Heist
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.heist -->

**Definition.** A sequence or story organized around acquiring a guarded or otherwise inaccessible target through planning and coordinated action. The target may be an object, data, or something less tangible.

**Use.** Connect distinct skills, stages, contingencies, and failures into a legible operation.

**Example.** A crew diverts a maintenance inspection to retrieve an impounded archive without disrupting the hospital it powers.

### Pursuit
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.pursuit -->

**Definition.** Sustained effort to locate, catch, obtain, or reach a moving or elusive target. Pursuit can contain many scenes of searching and planning between immediate chases.

**Use.** Maintain direction across changing places and tactics.

**Example.** An investigator follows a stolen generator through a chain of exchanges, arriving repeatedly after it has moved onward.

### Investigation
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.investigation -->

**Definition.** A process of gathering evidence, testing explanations, and revising an understanding of events. Dramatic progression comes from the relationship between questions and consequential discoveries.

**Use.** Make reasoning visible and distinguish useful clues from information that merely accumulates.

**Example.** Conflicting maintenance logs lead an engineer to test whether a blackout was accidental or deliberately timed.

### Training
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.training -->

**Definition.** A sequence in which someone acquires, tests, or changes a capability through instruction, practice, error, and feedback. Improvement need not be linear or complete.

**Use.** Connect later competence to observable effort while developing relationships and costs.

**Example.** An apprentice learns to navigate by sound, succeeds indoors, then fails when outdoor echoes invalidate the familiar cues.

### Rescue
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.rescue -->

**Definition.** An organized attempt to bring someone or something out of danger or an otherwise intolerable situation. Rescue becomes specific through access, urgency, consent, and the consequences of intervention.

**Use.** Coordinate parallel problems around a clear change in safety or freedom.

**Example.** A team reaches a stranded caretaker who refuses to leave until the community's stored medicine can be moved too.

### Siege
<!-- term-id: scenes-and-sequences.siege -->

**Definition.** A sustained situation in which one group confines or pressures another within a bounded place, often restricting movement and supplies. Duration and resource depletion shape the action.

**Use.** Develop conflict through endurance, negotiation, internal division, and attempts to alter the boundary.

**Example.** A workshop survives a blockade by rationing power while negotiating with residents who need its repairs.

## Practical distinctions

An activity label is not enough to describe a scene. Record both “meal” and “negotiation” when the meal's dramatic work is bargaining. A reveal belongs to information design; a discovery belongs to someone's experience; the two can occur at different times. Reversal and turning point overlap, but not every turn completely reverses advantage.

The [BBC screenplay guide](https://downloads.bbc.co.uk/writersroom/scripts/screenplaytv.pdf) distinguishes scene headings, action passages, and grouped shots. This wiki's dramatic labels are an additional planning layer, not required screenplay notation.

Intended effects remain editable prose. None of these fields should require conflict in every scene or turn a selected dramatic function into a mandatory camera setup.
