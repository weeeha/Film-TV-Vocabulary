# 14 · Shot size, angle, and composition

[Wiki home](../README.md) · [Alphabetical index](../INDEX.md)

A shot can be described along several compatible dimensions: how large its subject appears, where the camera looks from, how subjects are arranged, what the shot does, and how the frame organizes attention. “Low-angle medium close-up reaction shot” combines these dimensions rather than naming competing shot types.

Shot-size boundaries are approximate conventions. Human-body landmarks provide useful shorthand, but objects, animals, architecture, and moving compositions require contextual descriptions. All examples below are original hypothetical shots; suggested audience effects are intentions, not guaranteed responses.

## Shot size

### Extreme wide shot
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.extreme-wide-shot -->

**Definition.** A framing in which the environment occupies most of the image and the principal subject, if visible, appears very small. Also called an extreme long shot or EWS/ELS.

**Use.** Establish large spatial relationships, show collective activity, or relate an individual to an extensive setting. It need not be the first shot of a scene.

**Example.** A single maintenance worker crosses the illuminated floor of an otherwise dark aircraft hangar.

### Wide shot
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.wide-shot -->

**Definition.** A relatively expansive framing that presents the subject together with substantial surroundings. Often called a long shot or WS/LS, although usage overlaps with full shot and differs among crews.

**Use.** Make geography, body movement, and relationships readable within one image. Specify the necessary boundaries when the label alone is ambiguous.

**Example.** The frame includes a shopkeeper, the customer at the counter, and the open back door between them.

### Full shot
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.full-shot -->

**Definition.** A framing that includes a person's whole body from head to feet, usually with less environmental emphasis than a wide shot. Full-length shot is a common alternative name.

**Use.** Preserve posture, gesture, costume silhouette, and footwork. A full shot describes body coverage rather than a fixed lens choice or camera distance.

**Example.** A dancer pauses in a doorway, with both the hesitant hand movement and planted feet visible.

### Medium long shot
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.medium-long-shot -->

**Definition.** A framing commonly extending from around the knees to above the head. MLS is a frequent abbreviation; “three-quarter shot” overlaps, while a cowboy shot often specifies approximately mid-thigh framing.

**Use.** Keep substantial bodily action while making expressions easier to read than in a full shot. Confirm the intended crop rather than assuming exact agreement.

**Example.** A station guard's face, radio hand, and shifting stance remain visible during a disputed entry.

### Medium shot
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.medium-shot -->

**Definition.** A framing commonly showing a person from roughly the waist upward. MS is the usual abbreviation; the boundary is a practical convention rather than a measurement standard.

**Use.** Balance facial expression with hand gestures and nearby action. A medium shot can include multiple people if their scale within the frame is comparable.

**Example.** A baker folds a paper bag while answering a customer's unexpectedly personal question.

### Medium close-up
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.medium-close-up -->

**Definition.** A framing commonly extending from the upper chest or shoulders to above the head. MCU sits between a medium shot and a close-up, with terminology varying slightly across productions.

**Use.** Emphasize facial response while retaining some posture and environmental context. Check how the chosen aspect ratio changes space around the subject.

**Example.** A teacher reads a note, and the small movement of her shoulders remains visible as she understands it.

### Close-up
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.close-up -->

**Definition.** A framing in which a face or another selected subject occupies much of the image. CU describes relative image scale; a close-up of an object does not require human-body landmarks.

**Use.** Make a detail or response available for sustained scrutiny. A close-up can clarify information, withhold surroundings, or create emphasis without necessarily indicating intimacy.

**Example.** The camera holds on a locksmith's face as he recognizes the mechanism's unusual sound.

### Extreme close-up
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.extreme-close-up -->

**Definition.** A very tight framing of a small portion of a subject, such as an eye, a fingertip, or part of a mechanism. ECU and detail shot are common related labels.

**Use.** Isolate information whose significance might be lost in a broader image. Orientation may need support from neighboring shots.

**Example.** The image shows the instant a fine crack spreads through the glass of a pressure gauge.

## Camera angle

### Eye level
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.eye-level -->

**Definition.** A viewpoint positioned approximately at the subject's eye height, commonly with a relatively level viewing direction. Camera height and tilt should still be specified separately when precision matters.

**Use.** Establish a direct spatial relationship without a pronounced view from above or below. Eye level does not guarantee neutrality or equal social power.

**Example.** The camera is lowered to a seated child's eyes while an adult waits outside the frame.

### High angle
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.high-angle -->

**Definition.** A viewpoint looking downward toward the subject. The amount of downward inclination and the camera's distance both affect the resulting view; high angle does not necessarily mean directly overhead.

**Use.** Expose floor relationships, show activity behind a foreground subject, or describe an elevated observer's position. Emotional interpretations depend on context.

**Example.** From a stair landing, the frame reveals where each guest has placed their wet shoes around the entrance.

### Low angle
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.low-angle -->

**Definition.** A viewpoint looking upward toward the subject. It can arise from a low camera position, a raised subject, or both, and varies from subtle inclination to a steep upward view.

**Use.** Reveal ceilings, connect figures to architecture, or adopt a lower observer's vantage. It does not inherently make a subject powerful.

**Example.** A technician peers upward through floor machinery as a colleague lowers a missing component toward her.

### Overhead
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.overhead -->

**Definition.** A viewpoint looking approximately straight down onto the action. Also called a top shot or bird's-eye view, though “bird's-eye” sometimes describes a less strictly vertical elevated view.

**Use.** Present spatial patterns, simultaneous tasks, or relationships difficult to see from the side. Height alone does not make a shot overhead; viewing direction matters.

**Example.** An overhead image reveals two hands moving toward the same unclaimed envelope across a round table.

### Canted angle
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.canted-angle -->

**Definition.** A framing rotated around the camera's viewing axis so normally level or vertical references appear tilted. Dutch angle or Dutch tilt are common aliases; it differs from tilting the camera upward or downward.

**Use.** Establish a graphic imbalance or a particular viewpoint. Disorientation is one possible contextual effect, not an automatic meaning.

**Example.** A camera aligned with a ship's listing deck makes the shore beyond appear sharply diagonal.

## Subject arrangement

### Single
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.single -->

**Definition.** A shot organized primarily around one person. A clean single excludes another participant's foreground body; a dirty single includes part of another person or an intervening element.

**Use.** Give an individual response or action its own emphasis while retaining flexibility about environmental context. “Single” does not specify shot size.

**Example.** During a council vote, each member receives a medium single before the group result is shown.

### Two-shot
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.two-shot -->

**Definition.** A shot organized around two people together. Their relative sizes, orientations, and depth positions can differ; they do not need to stand side by side or face the camera.

**Use.** Let a relationship unfold within one continuous image and show simultaneous responses. Specify whether one person dominates the framing.

**Example.** A father sits in the foreground while his adult daughter remains standing in the open doorway behind him.

### Group shot
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.group-shot -->

**Definition.** A shot organized around several people as a shared arrangement. The group can contain internal clusters or unequal prominence; it is more than a count of incidental background figures.

**Use.** Show alliances, collective reactions, or competing actions without separating everyone into singles. Blocking and depth become important tools for readability.

**Example.** A band hears its first recorded performance together, with each member responding differently in the same frame.

### Over-the-shoulder shot
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.over-the-shoulder-shot -->

**Definition.** A view past part of a foreground person's shoulder, head, or upper body toward another subject. OTS is the common abbreviation; it establishes a spatial relationship rather than literal first-person vision.

**Use.** Connect participants while emphasizing the person or object beyond. Foreground size and focus affect how strongly the nearer person occupies the exchange.

**Example.** The blurred shoulder of a customer frames the clerk quietly sliding a returned photograph across the counter.

## Shot function

### Establishing shot
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.establishing-shot -->

**Definition.** A shot that supplies useful context about location, geography, time, or relationships for an upcoming or ongoing passage. It is a function, not necessarily a wide exterior view.

**Use.** Give viewers enough orientation to interpret later details. Establishing information may be distributed across several shots or deliberately postponed.

**Example.** A close view of an operating-room clock and illuminated status sign establishes a night surgery before faces appear.

### Master shot
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.master-shot -->

**Definition.** A shot that covers the main action of a scene through a substantial continuous span, commonly including its principal participants. It provides a broad account that may be combined with closer coverage.

**Use.** Preserve performance relationships and an editorial foundation. A master need not remain wide or static, and an establishing shot need not cover complete action.

**Example.** One moving take follows the entire argument from the apartment entrance to the kitchen table.

### Insert
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.insert -->

**Definition.** A shot emphasizing a detail within the scene's action or immediate space, often an object or small gesture. Its scale is frequently close, but its informational relationship is what matters.

**Use.** Clarify an action, expose evidence, or make a detail available at the necessary moment. Coordinate object state and hand positions with surrounding coverage.

**Example.** Between conversation shots, an insert reveals the date printed on the visitor's expired access card.

### Cutaway
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.cutaway -->

**Definition.** A shot that shifts attention away from the principal action to something related, nearby, or contextually relevant. Compared with an insert, it more clearly leaves the main action's immediate detail.

**Use.** Broaden context, introduce another response, or bridge an editorial omission. The boundary between insert and cutaway varies by usage.

**Example.** During a platform farewell, the film cuts away to the departure board changing from delayed to boarding.

### Reaction shot
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.reaction-shot -->

**Definition.** A shot whose primary function is to show a response to another event, action, or piece of information. It can show a face, a whole body, or a group.

**Use.** Establish what an event means to a participant and determine whose response guides interpretation. A reaction can precede the audience's view of its cause.

**Example.** The editor shows the assistant's startled look before revealing the empty display case.

### Point-of-view shot
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.point-of-view-shot -->

**Definition.** A shot presented as what a particular character sees from their position. POV is the common abbreviation; its identification usually relies on surrounding shots, gaze, sound, or established context.

**Use.** Restrict visual access to a character's viewpoint or make looking itself significant. An over-the-shoulder shot is not automatically a literal POV.

**Example.** After a child raises binoculars, the next image shows the distant ferry through the binocular view.

## Composition

### Symmetry
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.symmetry -->

**Definition.** An arrangement in which visual elements correspond across an axis or around a center. Symmetry can be exact, approximate, or temporarily disrupted as people and camera positions change.

**Use.** Establish a legible pattern whose stability or interruption matters. Symmetry may support formality, humor, or discomfort depending on the scene.

**Example.** Two identical waiting-room benches balance the frame until one applicant stands and leaves an empty half.

### Asymmetry
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.asymmetry -->

**Definition.** An arrangement without matching distribution across the frame's principal axes. Asymmetry can still feel balanced through differences in size, contrast, depth, or the implied weight of attention.

**Use.** Hold competing points of interest or reserve meaningful space. It is a compositional description, not a synonym for carelessness or instability.

**Example.** A small bright exit sign on one side counterbalances a large dark figure on the other.

### Central framing
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.central-framing -->

**Definition.** Placement of the principal subject near the frame's center. Central framing concerns location; the surrounding image may remain asymmetrical and the subject may face in any direction.

**Use.** Sustain direct attention, preserve space around a moving subject, or work with a setting's geometry. It has no mandatory emotional effect.

**Example.** A courier stays centered while commuters stream unevenly past on both sides.

### Rule of thirds
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.rule-of-thirds -->

**Definition.** A compositional guide that divides the frame into three equal horizontal and vertical bands, suggesting lines and intersections as possible placements for important elements. It is a heuristic, not a rule of correctness.

**Use.** Compare arrangements and discuss displacement from the center. Other proportions may serve the scene equally well.

**Example.** A horizon rests near the lower third so the changing cloud cover dominates the landscape.

### Negative space
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.negative-space -->

**Definition.** Areas around or between principal subjects that contain relatively little competing visual information. Negative space may be textured, dark, bright, or environmentally detailed while remaining subordinate to the subject.

**Use.** Shape isolation, direction, anticipation, or graphic balance through a contextual relationship. Its meaning changes when another subject enters it.

**Example.** An interview frame leaves an empty chair-sized area beside a person discussing someone absent.

### Headroom
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.headroom -->

**Definition.** The visible space between the top of a subject's head and the upper frame boundary. It is a framing relationship and can change with movement, aspect ratio, or intended delivery crop.

**Use.** Control vertical placement and compare shots in a sequence. Very tight framing may deliberately crop the head rather than preserve conventional headroom.

**Example.** A character rises from a chair, and the camera reframes to avoid an unintended crop of the eyes.

### Looking room
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.looking-room -->

**Definition.** Space left in the direction a subject looks. Look room and nose room are common aliases; movement-oriented lead room is related but concerns travel rather than gaze alone.

**Use.** Suggest attention beyond the subject and organize unseen space. Reducing looking room can be deliberate when the frame should feel obstructed or closed.

**Example.** A woman looks toward the right edge, with open space reserved there until her friend enters.

### Frame within a frame
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.frame-within-a-frame -->

**Definition.** Use of an element inside the image, such as a doorway, window, mirror, or arch, to enclose or bracket another subject. The internal frame can be partial or complete.

**Use.** Direct attention, connect spaces, or establish a viewpoint through a threshold. An internal frame need not be symmetrical or rectangular.

**Example.** A nurse is visible through the small glazed panel of a door while the corridor remains in the foreground.

### Leading lines
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.leading-lines -->

**Definition.** Visible or implied lines that encourage the eye to move toward, away from, or between elements. Architectural edges, paths, shadows, and aligned objects can all create them.

**Use.** Organize spatial reading and connect points of interest. Lines can also divert attention or terminate in an intentionally empty area.

**Example.** Rows of overhead cables converge toward a lone maintenance platform at the end of a tunnel.

### Foreground layering
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.foreground-layering -->

**Definition.** Placement of nearer elements between the camera and principal action to establish overlapping planes. The foreground may be sharp, blurred, moving, or partially occluding the subject.

**Use.** Articulate depth, establish a viewing position, or let information appear through gaps. Too much obstruction may hide action that the audience needs to follow.

**Example.** Hanging laundry intermittently reveals a conversation occurring in the courtyard beyond.

## Frame shape and multiple images

### Aspect ratio
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.aspect-ratio -->

**Definition.** The proportional relationship of an image's width to its height, commonly expressed as 16:9 or 2.39:1. Delivery ratio, recorded image area, and displayed crop can differ.

**Use.** Plan composition and protect the intended image across versions. ARRI's frame-line tool explicitly distinguishes camera formats and target ratios. [ARRI, Frame Line & Lens Illumination Tool](https://www.arri.com/en/learn-help/learn-help-camera-system/tools/frame-line-lens-illumination-tool).

**Example.** A production records extra image area while composing a primary 1.85:1 presentation.

### Portrait frame
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.portrait-frame -->

**Definition.** A frame taller than it is wide, with a width-to-height ratio below one. Vertical frame is an ordinary alias; 9:16 is one possible ratio rather than the definition.

**Use.** Organize vertical relationships, full figures, or work intended for vertical display. Reconsider blocking and background information when adapting a wider composition.

**Example.** A lift shaft fills a vertical frame, relating a mechanic at its base to a stranded passenger above.

### Widescreen frame
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.widescreen-frame -->

**Definition.** A broad horizontal frame, conventionally wider than the older roughly 4:3 screen shape. The term covers multiple ratios, so “widescreen” alone is not a complete delivery specification.

**Use.** Explore lateral relationships, landscape, or simultaneous action. It does not dictate shot size, genre, lens type, or the amount of visible background.

**Example.** Two estranged friends occupy opposite ends of a wide image while a closed ticket window separates them.

### Split screen
<!-- term-id: shot-size-angle-and-composition.split-screen -->

**Definition.** Presentation of two or more distinct images within the same overall frame. Panels may show simultaneous events, different times, contrasting views, or a graphic arrangement unrelated to ordinary chronology.

**Use.** Compare actions and information while deciding how attention should move between panels. Synchronization needs to be specified rather than inferred from proximity.

**Example.** One panel shows a caller waiting silently while another shows the recipient deciding whether to answer.

## Practical distinctions

Size labels describe the subject's image scale; angle labels describe viewpoint; arrangement labels describe who shares the shot; function labels explain its contribution. Composition and aspect ratio describe the image's organization. Store these dimensions separately.

Changing focal length can change framing from a fixed camera position. Moving the camera changes viewpoint and spatial relationships. A close-up made nearby with a wider lens therefore differs from one made farther away with a longer lens, even if the face occupies the same area. [Nikon, choosing lenses: perspective](https://www.nikonimgsupport.com/na/NSG_article?articleNo=000047395&configured=1&lang=en_SG).

Avoid equating “high angle” with weakness or “symmetry” with calm. A shot's meaning develops through situation, performance, sound, neighboring images, and expectations established by the work.
