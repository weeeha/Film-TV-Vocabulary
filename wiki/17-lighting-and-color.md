# 17 · Lighting and color

[Wiki home](../README.md) · [Alphabetical index](../INDEX.md)

Lighting describes how illumination reaches people, objects, and spaces. Color describes relationships among their recorded and displayed appearances. Keep the scene's light, camera exposure and white balance, and final grade separately editable: changing one does not produce every effect of changing another.

Roles, quality, direction, approach, and controls are compatible dimensions. A soft side key can be motivated by a window, supplemented by negative fill, and captured for a restrained color grade. The examples below are hypothetical; warm light does not guarantee comfort, nor does darkness guarantee fear.

## Light roles

### Key light
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.key-light -->

**Definition.** The principal source or contribution shaping illumination on a selected subject. It establishes a dominant lighting relationship rather than necessarily being the brightest visible object or a particular fixture type. [ARRI lighting handbook, key light](https://www.arri.com/resource/blob/83996/409091c612f371b0c68b41d9dcb636db/arri-lighting-handbook-english-data.pdf).

**Use.** Identify the main direction and modeling of the subject while coordinating secondary sources.

**Example.** A broad window provides the key on a seated reader, although a small visible lamp is brighter within the image.

### Fill light
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.fill-light -->

**Definition.** Illumination that raises areas left relatively dark by the key or other dominant source. Fill can come from another fixture, reflected light, or the environment. [ARRI lighting handbook, fill light](https://www.arri.com/resource/blob/83996/409091c612f371b0c68b41d9dcb636db/arri-lighting-handbook-english-data.pdf).

**Use.** Control shadow visibility and the contrast relationship without assuming that every shadow must disappear.

**Example.** A white wall returns enough window light to keep the reader's shaded eye visible.

### Backlight
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.backlight -->

**Definition.** Light reaching a subject from behind relative to the camera's view. It may illuminate hair, shoulders, translucent material, or an edge, depending on source position and the subject's shape.

**Use.** Separate layers, reveal transmission through materials, or establish a source behind the action. A backlight is not the same as a light aimed at the background.

**Example.** An open doorway behind a cyclist lights the edge of a wet coat.

### Rim light
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.rim-light -->

**Definition.** Illumination producing a visible band or edge highlight along a subject's contour. Rim describes the resulting appearance; back or rear-side placement often produces it, but the entire back need not be lit.

**Use.** Make a boundary readable against a similar-toned background. The width and strength of the rim depend on geometry, source size, and material.

**Example.** A narrow highlight outlines a dark instrument case against the backstage curtains.

### Background light
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.background-light -->

**Definition.** Illumination directed primarily at the environment behind the main subject. It describes the target of the light, not the source's location behind the subject.

**Use.** Reveal setting, create separation, or organize attention through patches and gradients. Background lighting may coordinate with the apparent direction of a window or practical fixture.

**Example.** A soft pool of light on a noticeboard makes its presence readable while the speaker remains separately lit.

## Light quality and delivery

### Hard light
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.hard-light -->

**Definition.** Light that produces relatively distinct shadow edges, commonly from a source with small apparent size as seen by the subject. Hardness is not the same property as brightness or contrast. [ARRI lighting handbook, light quality](https://www.arri.com/resource/blob/83996/409091c612f371b0c68b41d9dcb636db/arri-lighting-handbook-english-data.pdf).

**Use.** Make shape, cast shadows, or surface relief clear when appropriate to the scene.

**Example.** A small directional source projects the pattern of a railing sharply across the floor.

### Soft light
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.soft-light -->

**Definition.** Light producing broader, more gradual shadow transitions, commonly from a source with larger apparent size as seen by the subject. A physically large source becomes relatively smaller when moved farther away. [ARRI lighting handbook, light quality](https://www.arri.com/resource/blob/83996/409091c612f371b0c68b41d9dcb636db/arri-lighting-handbook-english-data.pdf).

**Use.** Control shadow transitions without assuming that soft light must be flat or low-contrast.

**Example.** A large nearby luminous surface creates a gradual transition across a sculptor's face.

### Diffused light
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.diffused-light -->

**Definition.** Light transmitted through a scattering material that alters its distribution. When the illuminated material becomes a sufficiently large apparent source, shadows soften; a tiny diffuser does not automatically create very soft light.

**Use.** Change an existing source's delivery while managing its effective size, position, light loss, and spill. “Diffused” names a method, not a guarantee of identical results.

**Example.** A curtain spreads incoming window light into a larger luminous area.

### Bounced light
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.bounced-light -->

**Definition.** Light reflected from a surface before reaching the subject. The bounce surface's size, color, texture, and reflectivity help determine the illumination's quality and color.

**Use.** Redirect light or turn a surface into an effective source. Bounced light is often soft, but a mirror or small reflective area can preserve a hard character.

**Example.** A lamp aimed into a pale ceiling creates a broad return of light over a kitchen table.

## Direction relative to the subject and camera

### Frontal
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.frontal -->

**Definition.** Illumination arriving from roughly the camera-facing side of a subject. Frontal light is a direction, not a statement about hardness, intensity, or whether shadows are completely absent.

**Use.** Preserve visibility on surfaces facing the lens or establish a source near the viewer's position. Specify elevation when facial modeling matters.

**Example.** A ticket examiner's handheld lamp illuminates a passenger from near the camera's line of sight.

### Side
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.side -->

**Definition.** Illumination arriving predominantly from one side of a subject relative to the camera. Side light can range from a modest off-axis position to a nearly perpendicular direction.

**Use.** Describe volume, surface relief, or distinct illuminated and shaded regions. Its effect depends on the subject's orientation and accompanying fill.

**Example.** Light from a corridor opening crosses a repair worker's hands and exposes the raised lettering on a metal plate.

### Back
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.back -->

**Definition.** A directional relationship in which light travels toward the camera-facing scene from behind the subject. This names source direction; “backlight” identifies a light or contribution used in that position.

**Use.** Specify a geometry that may create rim highlights, translucency, flare, or silhouette depending on exposure and other illumination.

**Example.** The low sun behind hanging fabric makes its thinner and thicker sections visibly different.

### Top
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.top -->

**Definition.** Illumination arriving predominantly from above a subject. Top light may be close to vertical or include a forward or sideways component, so height and aim remain useful details.

**Use.** Relate lighting to overhead fixtures, open sky, or a chosen pattern of shadow. It need not imply interrogation, danger, or harshness.

**Example.** An overhead work lamp lights a map while leaving the seated surveyors' lower faces less illuminated.

### Underlighting
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.underlighting -->

**Definition.** Illumination arriving from below a subject's usual eye or object level. Uplighting is a related name; the source may be visible, concealed, or reflected upward from another surface.

**Use.** Establish plausible low sources or deliberately change familiar shadow patterns. Its meaning depends on the scene and need not be threatening.

**Example.** Reflected pool light moves across the underside of a lifeguard's face during a quiet inspection.

## Overall approaches

### High-key lighting
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.high-key-lighting -->

**Definition.** An approach generally characterized by a bright overall tonal range and relatively limited deep shadow, often with substantial fill. It is not simply overexposure or the use of a powerful key light.

**Use.** Keep multiple parts of the image readily visible while controlling important contrast. High-key images can support discomfort or severity as readily as cheerfulness.

**Example.** A nearly white examination room leaves a patient few visually sheltered areas within the frame.

### Low-key lighting
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.low-key-lighting -->

**Definition.** An approach with substantial dark areas and selective illumination, often producing pronounced light-dark relationships. Low-key lighting does not require accidental underexposure or the loss of every shadow detail.

**Use.** Limit what is visible and concentrate emphasis while preserving the information intended for the grade. A dark image can remain carefully exposed.

**Example.** Only the workbench and a narrow doorway receive light in an otherwise dim instrument workshop.

### Silhouette
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.silhouette -->

**Definition.** A subject rendered relatively dark against a brighter surrounding region so its outer contour dominates and internal detail is reduced. The result depends on lighting relationships and exposure together.

**Use.** Prioritize recognizable outline, movement, or anonymity. This lighting use differs from discussing a costume's silhouette under ordinary illumination.

**Example.** Two people greeting each other inside a station become dark shapes against the bright platform beyond.

### Motivated lighting
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.motivated-lighting -->

**Definition.** Lighting designed to appear plausibly caused by an identifiable or implied source within the story's environment. The actual production fixtures can be concealed, supplemented, or positioned differently to achieve that appearance.

**Use.** Connect illumination to the fictional world without requiring the visible source to supply all usable light.

**Example.** A desk lamp motivates a warm pool on an actor's face while a concealed fixture supplies most of that illumination.

### Available light
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.available-light -->

**Definition.** Illumination already present at the location, such as daylight, street lighting, or existing interior fixtures. It is not limited to sunlight and does not guarantee that the crew leaves it unmodified.

**Use.** Work with the location's existing conditions, possibly shaping them through scheduling, blocking, reflectors, or subtraction. Record added sources separately.

**Example.** A conversation is staged beside an existing shop window while a dark cloth reduces unwanted reflection from one side.

### Practical lighting
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.practical-lighting -->

**Definition.** Use of functioning light sources belonging visibly to the set or story environment, such as table lamps, signs, or lanterns. Practicals may illuminate subjects, motivate additional light, or mainly appear as luminous objects.

**Use.** Coordinate art direction, actor interaction, and lighting control. A practical is not automatically the dominant source.

**Example.** A character switches on a bedside lamp, and the room's lighting changes in coordination with it.

## Control and measurement

### Intensity
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.intensity -->

**Definition.** In production conversation, the amount or strength of light. Precise records distinguish fixture output settings, illuminance reaching a surface, and luminance leaving a visible surface; these are different measurements.

**Use.** State the quantity and units when numbers matter. A dimmer percentage does not directly describe exposure or permit comparison between unrelated fixtures.

**Example.** A lighting note records 200 lux at the desk rather than calling every lamp setting “50 percent brightness.”

### Contrast
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.contrast -->

**Definition.** Difference between image regions or lighting contributions. Lighting contrast concerns illumination relationships; image contrast concerns displayed tonal or color differences after capture and processing. The same word needs its domain specified.

**Use.** Describe what is being compared and how. A stated ratio needs a measurement convention; it is not interchangeable with a software contrast-slider value.

**Example.** The crew reduces facial lighting contrast, while the colorist separately adjusts tonal contrast in the displayed image.

### Falloff
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.falloff -->

**Definition.** The reduction of illumination with distance or position within a light's coverage. For an ideal point source at constant incidence, illuminance follows an inverse-square relationship; extended sources, optics, and surroundings complicate that approximation.

**Use.** Predict how actors and backgrounds change brightness as distances change. Confirm real fixtures through measurements or tests.

**Example.** A nearby lamp lights a reader strongly but contributes much less to the wall several meters behind.

### Negative fill
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.negative-fill -->

**Definition.** The removal or absorption of ambient or reflected light to deepen selected shadow areas. It is often achieved with dark surfaces positioned to prevent existing light from returning to the subject.

**Use.** Increase modeling when the environment supplies more fill than desired. Negative fill changes a light relationship by subtraction rather than projecting darkness.

**Example.** Black fabric beside a pale-walled room reduces reflected light on one side of the performer's face.

### Flags
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.flags -->

**Definition.** Opaque light-control pieces used to block illumination from selected areas or prevent unwanted light from reaching the lens. A flag's position, size, and relationship to the source determine its effect.

**Use.** Control spill, shape a lit region, or reduce flare. Flags are not the same as translucent diffusion or partial-transmission nets.

**Example.** A flag prevents a window-motivated fixture from washing out the dark noticeboard behind an actor.

### Diffusion
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.diffusion -->

**Definition.** In lighting control, scattering material or an arrangement used to change the distribution and effective emitting area of a source. This differs from diffusion filters placed in the camera's optical path.

**Use.** Select material, illuminated area, and distance together. A material label alone does not specify softness, transmission, or how evenly the surface is filled.

**Example.** A large illuminated diffusion frame supplies a broad source for two people moving around a table.

### Color temperature
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.color-temperature -->

**Definition.** A description of light color related to a thermal radiator, expressed in kelvin (K); many nonthermal sources use correlated color temperature, or CCT. Lower values generally appear warmer and higher values cooler. [ETC, color temperature and CCT](https://blog.etcconnect.com/a-quick-definition-of-color-temperature-and-cct).

**Use.** Describe white-light balance while recognizing that CCT alone does not specify spectrum or color rendering.

**Example.** A 3200 K source appears warmer than a 5600 K source under the same camera white balance.

### Tint
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.tint -->

**Definition.** In lighting and white-balance controls, adjustment commonly associated with the green-magenta direction, distinct from the warm-cool temperature control. The numerical scale and exact behavior depend on the fixture, camera, or software.

**Use.** Match or deliberately separate sources whose CCT values alone appear similar. Record the control system with any numeric setting.

**Example.** A slightly green office fixture is balanced against a cleaner-looking nearby lamp before filming a neutral reference.

## Color treatment

### Palette
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.palette -->

**Definition.** The selected colors and their relative presence across a shot, scene, or work. The final palette results from designed surfaces, illumination, capture, and grading rather than belonging to one department alone.

**Use.** Coordinate recurring colors and meaningful exceptions. Separate an intended palette from a sampled frame that also contains incidental colors.

**Example.** A blue-gray station interior allows a small red scarf to remain a recurring point of recognition.

### Saturation
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.saturation -->

**Definition.** A description of color's strength relative to a neutral appearance, with exact numerical definitions depending on the color model. Saturation is distinct from hue and brightness, though image-processing controls can interact.

**Use.** Compare muted and vivid treatments without assuming a universal scale. Check important materials and skin under the actual output transform.

**Example.** A grade reduces the intensity of background advertisements while preserving the traveler's yellow raincoat.

### Hue
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.hue -->

**Definition.** The color-family attribute through which a color is described as red, orange, green, blue, and so on. Hue differs from saturation and lightness; numeric angles require a specified color model.

**Use.** Describe the direction of a color change precisely. A hue adjustment can target a particular region without requiring all colors to change equally.

**Example.** A slightly cyan painted door is adjusted toward blue to match adjacent shots.

### Color separation
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.color-separation -->

**Definition.** Distinction between subjects or image regions achieved through differences in color. Here it means visual differentiation, not the separate color records used in printing or older photographic processes.

**Use.** Improve the readability of overlapping forms through hue, saturation, and tonal relationships. Color separation can be subtle and need not use complementary colors.

**Example.** A desaturated green coat remains distinguishable from a warmer gray wall despite similar brightness.

### Color grade
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.color-grade -->

**Definition.** The coordinated treatment of image color and tone to achieve continuity and a chosen appearance. Grading can involve overall and selective adjustments; “color correction” often overlaps rather than naming a universally separate stage.

**Use.** Shape the final image while respecting capture limits and display transforms. Grade decisions cannot reconstruct detail that was never recorded.

**Example.** Exterior shots filmed under changing clouds are balanced before a shared cool-shadow treatment is applied.

### Monochrome
<!-- term-id: lighting-and-color.monochrome -->

**Definition.** An image organized around one color family and its tonal variations, including neutral black-and-white imagery. Monochrome is not the same as simply reducing saturation a little.

**Use.** Emphasize luminance structure, texture, or a constrained color approach. Conversion choices determine how originally different colors separate as tones.

**Example.** In a black-and-white sequence, the grade distinguishes a red sign from green foliage through their resulting gray values.

## Practical distinctions

Lighting roles answer what a contribution does. Direction answers where it comes from. Quality describes shadow transition and related source behavior. A side light can be key or fill; a backlight may produce a rim or pass through translucent fabric. These are different axes of description.

A practical source belongs visibly to the scene. Motivated lighting looks as though it comes from a plausible scene source. Available light already exists at the location. One lamp can meet all three descriptions, but none implies the others. Aputure's practitioner account describes visible practicals and additional window light as separately controlled contributions. [Aputure, *Bad Influence* lighting case study](https://aputure.com/en-US/case-studies/lighting-bad-influence).

Color temperature and tint are separate control dimensions in lighting systems; software scales should be stored with their context. [ETC, color-control documentation](https://www.etcconnect.com/webdocs/Controls/EchoAccessApp3.3.0/Content/IntroChapter/ColorControl.htm). Grading similarly offers distinct hue, saturation, temperature, tint, and contrast controls, but their effects interact within an image-processing pipeline. [Blackmagic Design, DaVinci Resolve color](https://www.blackmagicdesign.com/products/davinciresolve/color).

Hard light can coexist with low contrast if fill is strong. Soft light can coexist with deep shadows if its spill and ambient return are controlled. High-key and low-key describe an overall treatment; neither is a substitute for exposure measurements or a guarantee of mood.

## Visual interaction and structured fields

These are proposed interfaces, not functioning lighting controls or calibrated simulations. A swatch is not a complete physical spectrum; a schematic lighting preview should say so.

| Decision | Visual interaction | Proposed data fields |
|---|---|---|
| Role and direction | Light map linked to a frame preview | `light_id`, `role`, `target_id`, `position_m`, `aim_target`, `coordinate_space` |
| Source quality | Shadow-edge comparison with size and distance annotations | `emitting_width_m`, `emitting_height_m`, `subject_distance_m`, `diffusion_material`, `quality_note` |
| Motivation and practicals | Visible-source markers linked to actual contributions | `motivating_source_id`, `practical_fixture_id`, `available_light`, `supplementary_light_ids` |
| Light level | Measurement points and optional meter readings | `illuminance_lux`, `measurement_position_m`, `fixture_dimmer_pct`, `fixture_model`, `measurement_status` |
| Contrast | Paired image regions with stated measurement convention | `contrast_domain`, `comparison_regions`, `ratio_convention`, `contrast_ratio`, `contrast_note` |
| Subtraction and spill | Occlusion and bounce overlays | `flag_ids`, `negative_fill_surfaces`, `bounce_surface`, `spill_exclusion_regions` |
| Color of light | Swatches with source and camera context | `cct_k`, `tint_value`, `tint_scale`, `camera_white_balance_k`, `spectrum_reference_id` |
| Grade and palette | Before/after comparison under a named display transform | `palette_swatches`, `color_space`, `grade_reference_id`, `hue_note`, `saturation_note`, `output_transform` |
