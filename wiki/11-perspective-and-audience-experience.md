# 11 · Perspective and audience experience

[Wiki home](../README.md) · [Alphabetical index](../INDEX.md)

Perspective concerns access: whose experiences shape the presentation, what information is available, and when it becomes available. Audience experience concerns intended or observed responses. These are connected, but choosing a technique cannot guarantee a feeling.

Keep three questions separate: who speaks, whose experience is represented, and what the audience knows. Narratology offers several ways to describe these relationships; the distinctions are debated rather than a single universally accepted classification. See Burkhard Niederhoff's [Focalization](https://www-archiv.fdm.uni-hamburg.de/lhn/node/18.html) for a scholarly account.

## Range and depth of access

### Restricted narration
<!-- term-id: perspective-and-audience-experience.restricted-narration -->

**Definition.** Presentation that limits the audience largely to information available to a particular character or a small set of characters. Restriction concerns knowledge, not a requirement to use literal point-of-view camera shots.

**Use.** Let uncertainty unfold alongside the character's investigation or experience.

**Example.** We remain with a stranded passenger and learn why the train stopped only when she reaches its driver.

### Unrestricted narration
<!-- term-id: perspective-and-audience-experience.unrestricted-narration -->

**Definition.** Presentation that gives the audience information beyond any one character's immediate access, often by moving among people and locations. It need not disclose every fact or explain every mind.

**Use.** Build relationships among events that individual participants cannot yet understand.

**Example.** We see both the stranded train and the workers debating whether to restore power to its route.

### Subjective narration
<!-- term-id: perspective-and-audience-experience.subjective-narration -->

**Definition.** Presentation shaped by a character's perception or mental experience, potentially including altered sound, dreams, memories, attention, or distorted imagery. Subjectivity can occur without a narrator speaking.

**Use.** Make the difference between an event and someone's experience of it perceptible.

**Example.** During a crowded announcement, the passenger hears only fragments while her attention fixes on a familiar name.

### Objective presentation
<!-- term-id: perspective-and-audience-experience.objective-presentation -->

**Definition.** Here, presentation limited mainly to externally observable behavior and events, without direct access to a character's thoughts. “Objective” describes a presentation strategy, not neutrality, completeness, or freedom from editorial choice.

**Use.** Invite inference from actions, expressions, space, and timing.

**Example.** The passenger repeatedly checks an unopened envelope, but the film neither shows its contents nor supplies her thoughts.

### Unreliable narration
<!-- term-id: perspective-and-audience-experience.unreliable-narration -->

**Definition.** Presentation in which the audience has reason to question the accuracy, completeness, or interpretation of an account. Unreliability can arise from deception, limited knowledge, memory, or conflicting perspectives.

**Use.** Make interpretation part of the story while providing grounds for doubt or revision.

**Example.** A witness's account claims an empty platform, but a later view includes someone whose presence he had denied.

## Intended audience responses

### Suspense
<!-- term-id: perspective-and-audience-experience.suspense -->

**Definition.** Sustained concern about an uncertain outcome that matters to the audience. It can involve anticipated danger, conflicting possibilities, or delayed resolution; a viewer may experience suspense even when revisiting a familiar story.

**Use.** Make the relevant outcome and pressure understandable before stretching anticipation.

**Example.** We know a station gate will close soon and watch the injured passenger approach it slowly.

### Surprise
<!-- term-id: perspective-and-audience-experience.surprise -->

**Definition.** A response to an event or fact that departs from the audience's expectation. Surprise can be brief or can force a substantial reinterpretation of earlier events.

**Use.** Disrupt an established expectation with a meaningful change rather than an arbitrary interruption.

**Example.** The supposedly abandoned ticket booth opens and the missing conductor calmly asks to see a pass.

### Curiosity
<!-- term-id: perspective-and-audience-experience.curiosity -->

**Definition.** A desire to resolve a gap in understanding, such as what happened, why someone acted, or how a system works. Curiosity often addresses missing causes while suspense often emphasizes possible outcomes.

**Use.** Give incomplete information a clear enough shape that the audience can form questions.

**Example.** Every passenger carries the same stamped envelope, although they claim never to have met.

### Anticipation
<!-- term-id: perspective-and-audience-experience.anticipation -->

**Definition.** Attention directed toward an expected event or development. Anticipation can be pleasant, fearful, comic, or emotionally mixed; it does not require a threat.

**Use.** Establish an upcoming encounter, deadline, promise, or pattern and let preparation matter.

**Example.** A character carefully rehearses what she will say when her brother finally arrives on the evening train.

### Empathy
<!-- term-id: perspective-and-audience-experience.empathy -->

**Definition.** An audience member's engagement with another person's experience or feelings. Understanding a character's emotional situation does not require approving that character's decisions.

**Use.** Supply context, behavior, and vulnerability that make an experience accessible without dictating one response.

**Example.** We watch a dishonest clerk struggle to read a letter from his child before he returns to deceiving customers.

### Dread
<!-- term-id: perspective-and-audience-experience.dread -->

**Definition.** An apprehensive sense that something harmful or intolerable is approaching. Dread may develop through uncertainty and atmosphere rather than a precisely identified immediate threat.

**Use.** Accumulate meaningful warning signs and control how long relief remains unavailable.

**Example.** Each station on the route is quieter than the last, yet every clock continues to run at exactly the same speed.

### Relief
<!-- term-id: perspective-and-audience-experience.relief -->

**Definition.** A reduction or release of previously sustained pressure, fear, uncertainty, or effort. Its force depends on what the audience has been carrying and how securely the situation seems to change.

**Use.** Give tension a rhythm and allow consequences to register after urgent action.

**Example.** A trapped passenger hears the latch release, then simply sits down instead of immediately celebrating.

### Catharsis
<!-- term-id: perspective-and-audience-experience.catharsis -->

**Definition.** A term often used in contemporary creative discussion for an intense emotional release or clarification through dramatic experience. Its historical theoretical meaning is contested; it is not synonymous with a happy ending.

**Use.** Describe an intended culmination while naming the emotions and unresolved elements more specifically.

**Example.** Two estranged siblings finally acknowledge their shared grief without solving the practical dispute that separated them.

## Alignment and information

### Identification
<!-- term-id: perspective-and-audience-experience.identification -->

**Definition.** A viewer's sense of connection or alignment with a character's situation, goals, perspective, or imagined position. It can shift during a story and differs from literal identity or unqualified approval.

**Use.** Consider which experiences invite the viewer to accompany a character and which create friction.

**Example.** We share an outsider's difficulty understanding station rules even while noticing that she treats other passengers unfairly.

### Emotional distance
<!-- term-id: perspective-and-audience-experience.emotional-distance -->

**Definition.** The degree to which presentation encourages detached observation, reflection, or evaluation instead of immediate emotional closeness. It is an interpretive quality, not something determined solely by shot size.

**Use.** Give viewers room to consider patterns, contradictions, or institutions beyond one person's reaction.

**Example.** An uninterrupted wide shot lets us observe several passengers' competing needs during an argument rather than privileging one face.

### Withheld information
<!-- term-id: perspective-and-audience-experience.withheld-information -->

**Definition.** Relevant information that presentation leaves unavailable for a period. What is withheld may be obvious to the audience as a question or may become apparent only after disclosure.

**Use.** Specify the information gap and its duration, keeping it consistent with the established presentation.

**Example.** We hear one side of a telephone call and learn the other speaker's identity only when the call ends.

### Dramatic irony
<!-- term-id: perspective-and-audience-experience.dramatic-irony -->

**Definition.** A knowledge difference that lets the audience interpret a character's words or actions beyond the character's own understanding. This is a relationship between perspectives rather than a tone in itself.

**Use.** Make a familiar action carry an additional implication for the viewer.

**Example.** We have seen the canceled departure notice while a traveler reassures her family that she will arrive tonight.

## Camera and observation

### Point-of-view shot
<!-- term-id: perspective-and-audience-experience.point-of-view-shot -->

**Definition.** A shot presented as approximately what a particular character sees, commonly established by surrounding shots or staging. It need not reproduce human vision with optical precision.

**Use.** Tie a visible detail to a character's attention while distinguishing seeing from understanding.

**Example.** After the passenger looks down the track, a view along it reveals a stationary lantern in the distance.

### Reaction shot
<!-- term-id: perspective-and-audience-experience.reaction-shot -->

**Definition.** A shot emphasizing a response to an event, statement, or perceived object. A response can be a slight change, deliberate restraint, or apparent absence of reaction.

**Use.** Give the audience access to consequences and interpretation through behavior.

**Example.** After an announcement names a destination, the camera stays with the one passenger who stops packing.

### Observational perspective
<!-- term-id: perspective-and-audience-experience.observational-perspective -->

**Definition.** A descriptive approach that lets behavior and situations unfold with an emphasis on watching and listening rather than overt explanation. It is not automatically the documentary mode called observational cinema.

**Use.** Make time, routine, and relationships available for the audience's own inference.

**Example.** We spend a passage watching a cleaner prepare the closed station, learning its rhythms before any problem is announced.

## Practical distinctions

Range and depth are independent. A story can move among many characters while showing none of their thoughts, or remain with one person while representing their memories. Literal POV is one shot convention within a much broader design of access.

Suspense, curiosity, and surprise often combine. David Bordwell's discussion of [Hitchcock, Lessing, and suspense](https://www.davidbordwell.net/blog/2013/11/29/hitchcock-lessing-and-the-bomb-under-the-table/) examines how information supplied to an audience can shape anticipation. It should not be reduced to a rule that suspense always requires viewers to know more than every character.
