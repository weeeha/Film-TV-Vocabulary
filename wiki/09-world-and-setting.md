# 09 · World and setting

[Wiki home](../README.md) · [Alphabetical index](../INDEX.md)

Worldbuilding describes the conditions under which a story operates. Setting specifies the place and time of an event. Design enough of the world to explain choices, limits, and visible details; a complete fictional encyclopedia is not a prerequisite for a compelling scene.

The entries here are working creative definitions. Geographic and social terms retain wider meanings outside filmmaking. They are prompts for inquiry, not a formula for classifying real communities.

## Physical world

### Era
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.era -->

**Definition.** The historical or imagined period in which events occur, with its available technologies, institutions, and cultural assumptions. An era may be precisely dated or deliberately approximate.

**Use.** Establish what characters consider ordinary and what would be impossible, novel, or controversial.

**Example.** A port drama set before reliable radio makes a missing ship an information problem as well as a rescue problem.

### Geography
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.geography -->

**Definition.** The arrangement of terrain, waterways, distances, borders, and routes that gives the story world its spatial logic. Geography includes relationships between places, not just attractive scenery.

**Use.** Make travel, trade, isolation, defense, and encounters understandable.

**Example.** A river separates the hospital from the power station; the only bridge becomes a recurring point of conflict during blackouts.

### Climate
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.climate -->

**Definition.** The usual patterns of temperature, precipitation, and other atmospheric conditions over extended periods. Climate shapes everyday adaptations; weather describes a particular short-term condition.

**Use.** Connect architecture, clothing, work schedules, and food production to their environment.

**Example.** A settlement accustomed to prolonged heat has shaded streets and nighttime markets even when the current scene happens on a mild day.

### Ecology
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.ecology -->

**Definition.** The relationships among organisms and their environment, including food, water, waste, competition, and regeneration. A fictional ecosystem becomes more convincing when its parts affect one another.

**Use.** Give survival problems and environmental changes concrete consequences.

**Example.** Polluted canals reduce insect life, then fish stocks, forcing the community to abandon a familiar source of food.

### Settlement
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.settlement -->

**Definition.** A place where a population lives, together with its distribution and organization: village, town, city, camp, dispersed homesteads, or another arrangement.

**Use.** Establish density, access, anonymity, dependence, and likely social encounters.

**Example.** In a compact hillside village, everyone sees a newcomer arrive; in an enormous transit city, the same arrival can remain unnoticed.

### Architecture
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.architecture -->

**Definition.** The design and construction of buildings and built spaces, including layout, structure, circulation, materials, and their cultural meaning.

**Use.** Translate the world's priorities into spaces that characters must physically navigate.

**Example.** An administration building places public waiting rooms far below private offices, making hierarchy visible through the visitor's journey rather than a speech.

### Infrastructure
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.infrastructure -->

**Definition.** The systems that enable everyday activity, such as water, energy, transport, communications, sanitation, and distribution. Their maintenance and ownership are part of the world.

**Use.** Identify dependencies and reveal who keeps a society functioning.

**Example.** A neighborhood's evening routine follows the two hours when its shared generator can power pumps and cooking equipment.

## Social world

### Institutions
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.institutions -->

**Definition.** Durable organizations, rules, and practices that organize collective life: courts, schools, guilds, religious bodies, councils, and similar structures.

**Use.** Give authority and opposition specific procedures rather than an abstract label such as “the system.”

**Example.** A repair guild controls certification; an ingenious unlicensed mechanic can fix the machine but cannot legally return it to service.

### Class
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.class -->

**Definition.** A structure of social positions shaped by resources, work, ownership, status, and power. The relevant divisions depend on the world and are not always reducible to wealth.

**Use.** Show unequal access, conflicting interests, and different expectations of the same event.

**Example.** Water engineers receive secure housing while wealthy traders still queue for permits, complicating a simple rich-versus-poor hierarchy.

### Economy
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.economy -->

**Definition.** The ways a society produces, distributes, exchanges, and consumes goods and services. Money is only one possible coordinating mechanism.

**Use.** Explain scarcity, incentives, obligations, and the practical cost of choices.

**Example.** A harbor community allocates fuel through work credits, so a rescue mission requires volunteers to surrender part of their winter allowance.

### Customs
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.customs -->

**Definition.** Familiar practices and expectations learned through participation in a community. Customs may be informal, contested, or different across generations.

**Use.** Communicate belonging and friction through ordinary behavior rather than exposition.

**Example.** Visitors leave their tools at the communal doorway; a stranger keeping a knife on their belt inadvertently signals distrust.

### Rituals
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.rituals -->

**Definition.** Repeated, patterned actions carrying shared significance, often marking a transition, obligation, belief, or membership. Rituals can be religious or secular.

**Use.** Give a community's values an observable form and create opportunities for variation or refusal.

**Example.** Before launching a repaired boat, every worker touches its nameplate; one person's refusal makes an unresolved dispute visible.

### Language
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.language -->

**Definition.** The systems of expression people use, including speech, signing, writing, specialized vocabularies, and conventions of address. Language also changes across situations and communities.

**Use.** Establish access, expertise, intimacy, and misunderstanding without making accent a substitute for character.

**Example.** Two rivals switch from formal titles to an old workshop shorthand when an emergency forces them to cooperate.

### Belief systems
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.belief-systems -->

**Definition.** Connected ideas about reality, value, authority, and obligation that inform how people interpret events. Members of a community need not hold identical beliefs.

**Use.** Make decisions understandable from inside a character's worldview.

**Example.** Some residents regard abandoned machines as shared inheritance; others believe reopening them violates a duty to the dead.

## Possibility and limitation

### Technology
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.technology -->

**Definition.** Tools, techniques, and systems through which people accomplish tasks. A world's technology includes maintenance, skills, supply chains, and access as well as devices.

**Use.** Establish reliable capabilities and meaningful limits before they become plot solutions.

**Example.** Portable batteries are powerful but their connectors are scarce, so a courier guards an adapter more carefully than the battery itself.

### Magic
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.magic -->

**Definition.** Supernatural or extraordinary capabilities treated as part of a fictional world's possibilities. Magic may have explicit rules or remain mysterious within the chosen storytelling approach.

**Use.** Decide what characters and audiences can reasonably expect, especially when magic resolves conflict.

**Example.** A healer can transfer pain but cannot remove it, making every successful treatment a difficult interpersonal choice.

### World rules
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.world-rules -->

**Definition.** Statements of what consistently can, cannot, or tends to happen in a fictional world. They can describe physical, social, supernatural, or institutional conditions.

**Use.** Support coherent consequences while separating objective rules from a character's mistaken beliefs.

**Example.** Machines cannot operate beyond the city's signal towers; a later exception requires an explanation rather than silent rule replacement.

### Limitations
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.limitations -->

**Definition.** Boundaries on an ability, resource, system, or institution. A limitation describes what a capability cannot accomplish or the conditions in which it fails.

**Use.** Keep powerful resources from dissolving every obstacle and generate alternative tactics.

**Example.** An aerial camera can map rooftops but cannot distinguish people inside buildings, leaving the investigator dependent on local testimony.

### Costs
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.costs -->

**Definition.** The expenditure, loss, risk, or obligation incurred by an action or capability. A cost may be immediate, delayed, personal, or borne by someone else.

**Use.** Turn otherwise obvious solutions into choices with consequences.

**Example.** Heating the clinic tonight consumes fuel reserved for tomorrow's water deliveries, so the decision redistributes harm rather than eliminating it.

### Taboos
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.taboos -->

**Definition.** Strong social prohibitions whose violation carries moral, spiritual, or communal significance. A taboo is not necessarily identical to a written law.

**Use.** Explain why a technically possible action remains difficult to take openly.

**Example.** Entering abandoned family homes is forbidden even when useful materials are visible through broken windows, forcing scavengers to negotiate exceptions.

## Kinds of place

### Public space
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.public-space -->

**Definition.** A space used by a wider community, with access governed by local norms or rules. Public use does not necessarily imply public ownership or equal freedom to remain there.

**Use.** Stage visibility, accidental encounters, collective activity, and contested belonging.

**Example.** A market square allows a private argument to acquire witnesses, allies, and social consequences.

### Private space
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.private-space -->

**Definition.** A space whose access is restricted or understood to belong to particular people or groups. Privacy may be physical, social, or temporary.

**Use.** Establish boundaries, trust, intrusion, and differences between public and private behavior.

**Example.** A guarded storeroom becomes emotionally significant when its owner gives another character a key without explanation.

### Domestic space
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.domestic-space -->

**Definition.** A space associated with living, care, rest, household work, and personal routine. Domestic space can be shared, improvised, institutional, or mobile.

**Use.** Reveal relationships through habits, possessions, and the distribution of labor.

**Example.** Two estranged siblings still prepare breakfast around each other's movements without speaking, showing a shared history through spatial familiarity.

### Workplace
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.workplace -->

**Definition.** A setting organized around labor, tasks, responsibilities, and working relationships. It may overlap with a home, vehicle, street, or institution.

**Use.** Generate recurring encounters and show competence, hierarchy, solidarity, or exploitation in action.

**Example.** A night-shift control room keeps rivals together long enough for a technical disagreement to expose a deeper conflict.

### Wilderness
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.wilderness -->

**Definition.** A setting framed as relatively outside the story's dominant built or managed environment. This is a perspective-dependent designation, not proof that a place is uninhabited or without history.

**Use.** Explore unfamiliarity, exposure, navigation, and competing relationships to land.

**Example.** Outsiders call a forest empty while their guide recognizes cultivated paths and seasonal gathering sites.

### Frontier
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.frontier -->

**Definition.** A boundary zone between jurisdictions, systems, settlements, or imagined possibilities. Who describes it as a frontier matters, especially when expansion obscures existing inhabitants.

**Use.** Stage uncertain authority and encounters between incompatible assumptions.

**Example.** A repair station sits where two cities' communication networks end, making it a bargaining point rather than an uncontested outpost.

### Transit space
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.transit-space -->

**Definition.** A place organized around movement between destinations, such as a station, ferry, corridor, checkpoint, or airport. Waiting is often as important as travel.

**Use.** Put otherwise separate characters together and attach decisions to departure or arrival.

**Example.** A delayed ferry creates a temporary community whose members must decide whether to share a limited shelter.

## Scene conditions

### Interior
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.interior -->

**Definition.** A scene staged within an enclosed location, commonly marked INT. in screenplay headings. The designation describes the represented space; an interior can be filmed on a constructed set.

**Use.** Specify spatial enclosure and practical conditions for staging, light, and sound.

**Example.** A nighttime kitchen scene is interior even when the kitchen set occupies an outdoor studio backlot.

### Exterior
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.exterior -->

**Definition.** A scene staged in an outdoor location, commonly marked EXT. in screenplay headings. It describes the fictional location, not simply whether the camera is outdoors.

**Use.** Clarify exposure to weather, visible surroundings, and movement through open space.

**Example.** A conversation on a covered station platform remains an exterior setting despite partial shelter.

### Day
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.day -->

**Definition.** A broad scene-time designation indicating daylight hours in the story. It does not specify a particular sun angle, weather condition, or exposure treatment.

**Use.** Establish time continuity, then add more specific lighting intentions where needed.

**Example.** Two daytime scenes can have very different appearances: an overcast morning street and a bright late-afternoon courtyard.

### Night
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.night -->

**Definition.** A scene-time designation indicating nighttime in the story. Visibility may come from moonlight, practical fixtures, artificial sources, or stylized treatment; night does not mean an unreadable image.

**Use.** Coordinate story time, daily routines, and the intended sources of illumination.

**Example.** A busy night market is brightly illuminated while the adjacent residential alley remains mostly dark.

### Dawn
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.dawn -->

**Definition.** The transition toward daylight around the beginning of the day. Its visible duration and character vary with geography, season, and weather.

**Use.** Connect a scene to changing activity and light without assuming one universal emotional meaning.

**Example.** Workers finish repairing a pump as the first customers arrive, making dawn a deadline rather than merely a hopeful image.

### Dusk
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.dusk -->

**Definition.** The transition from daylight toward darkness at the end of the day. It is a changing interval rather than a single fixed color or exposure.

**Use.** Stage closing routines, reduced visibility, and a shift in how spaces are used.

**Example.** A public courtyard empties as vendors pack away, leaving two characters increasingly exposed to one another.

### Weather
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.weather -->

**Definition.** The atmospheric conditions at a particular time and place: rain, wind, snow, fog, heat, and related conditions. Weather affects action as well as appearance.

**Use.** Connect atmosphere to practical consequences and continuity across shots.

**Example.** Wind prevents a character from hearing an approaching bicycle; wet clothes persist when the scene moves indoors.

### Season
<!-- term-id: world-and-setting.season -->

**Definition.** A recurring portion of the year associated with particular environmental, agricultural, or social patterns. Some worlds organize seasons around rainfall or other cycles rather than four temperate divisions.

**Use.** Establish resources, daylight patterns, clothing, labor, and recurring events.

**Example.** A flood-season ferry service becomes the only connection between districts that are walkable during dry months.

## Practical distinctions

World facts, character beliefs, and production choices need separate records. A character may believe a mountain is impassable; the geography record can leave that unresolved. A blue color grade is a visual treatment, not evidence that the fictional climate is cold.

The INT./EXT., location, and day/night heading convention is illustrated in the [BBC television screenplay format guide](https://downloads.bbc.co.uk/writersroom/scripts/screenplaytv.pdf). More specific setting details belong in action, production notes, or structured fields as appropriate; a particular format guide is not universal across every production.

## Visual interaction and structured fields

| Decision | Suggested interaction | Proposed data fields |
|---|---|---|
| Geography | Map with routes and barriers | `locations`, `routes`, `distance_km`, `access_conditions` |
| World rule | Rule card with exceptions | `statement`, `scope`, `exceptions`, `decision_status` |
| Social system | Institution and dependency diagram | `institutions`, `roles`, `resources`, `relationships` |
| Scene setting | Place card and time selector | `location_id`, `interior_exterior`, `time_of_day`, `weather` |
| Knowledge | Compare fact and belief layers | `world_fact`, `believed_by`, `certainty`, `evidence` |

These are interaction proposals. Geographic coordinates, distances, and dates remain unknown unless the project has actually established them.
