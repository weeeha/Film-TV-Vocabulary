# 18 · Editing, transitions, and pace

[Wiki home](../README.md) · [Alphabetical index](../INDEX.md)

Editing organizes what viewers see and hear, when they receive information, and how separate moments appear related. A transition describes the join between images; an editing pattern describes relationships across several shots; pace describes the developing experience. These are connected decisions, but none can substitute for the others.

All examples below are hypothetical. Read durations as illustrative choices, not recipes for a particular emotion. A long shot containing difficult, rapidly changing action can feel more urgent than a string of quiet close-ups.

## Transitions between images

### Cut
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.cut -->

**Definition.** An immediate change from one shot to another, with no visible transitional overlap. The sound may change at the same moment or follow a separate edit point.

**Use.** Choose the cut point around a movement, look, idea, or change in attention. A cut can feel unobtrusive or emphatic depending on the relationship between the images.

**Example.** A mechanic notices a vibration; the film cuts directly to the loose bolt causing it.

### Dissolve
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.dissolve -->

**Definition.** A transition in which the outgoing image gradually becomes transparent while the incoming image becomes visible, temporarily placing both images together. “Cross dissolve” is a common name for this overlap.

**Use.** Consider the combined image as well as the endpoints. Duration, alignment, and contrast can suggest passage, association, or lingering memory without automatically specifying elapsed time.

**Example.** A lit apartment dissolves into the same room after sunrise, with the window remaining aligned.

### Fade
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.fade -->

**Definition.** A gradual transition between an image and a uniform field, often black. A fade-out removes the image; a fade-in introduces it. Unlike a dissolve, two scene images need not overlap.

**Use.** Mark a boundary, an interruption, or a change in attention. Specify the field color and whether sound continues, since those choices alter the perceived closure.

**Example.** The workshop fades to black while its final machine hum continues for another second.

### Wipe
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.wipe -->

**Definition.** A transition in which a moving boundary progressively replaces one image with another. The boundary may be straight, shaped, soft, or motivated by an object crossing the frame.

**Use.** Specify direction, speed, edge treatment, and the relationship to movement inside the shots. A wipe can announce a chapter or connect coordinated movement.

**Example.** A passing bus fills the frame, and its trailing edge reveals the destination street.

### Match cut
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.match-cut -->

**Definition.** A cut that connects shots through a salient similarity, such as shape, position, movement, or conceptual association. It need not preserve continuous action or connect adjacent places.

**Use.** Name the feature being matched and the meaning created by the change. Do not use the label for every smooth edit; match on action is a more specific continuity technique.

**Example.** A circular ventilation fan cuts to an overhead view of a spinning pottery wheel.

### Jump cut
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.jump-cut -->

**Definition.** A cut that creates a noticeable discontinuity within a similar view of the same subject, often by removing part of an action or interval. The subject appears to jump in position or time.

**Use.** Compress repetition, expose editing, or produce deliberate restlessness. Check whether the jump communicates the intended omission rather than accidentally confusing the action.

**Example.** Three cuts within a fixed view show a painter repeatedly changing the color of one wall.

### Smash cut
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.smash-cut -->

**Definition.** An abrupt cut emphasizing a strong contrast between adjacent moments, often in sound, scale, action, or emotional tone. It describes the force of the juxtaposition rather than a separate transition mechanism.

**Use.** Build a clear expectation before disrupting it. Loudness alone does not make a contrast meaningful, and a quiet incoming shot can be equally forceful.

**Example.** A crowd celebrates a promised launch; the next image is a deserted, silent factory floor.

## Editing systems and relationships

### Continuity editing
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.continuity-editing -->

**Definition.** An approach that helps viewers understand a coherent flow of action, space, and time across shots. It uses cues such as consistent direction, matching actions, and intelligible looks, while often omitting unimportant intervals.

**Use.** Decide which relationships viewers must understand, then preserve the cues supporting them. Continuity is an organized impression, not a requirement to show every movement.

**Example.** A worker enters a room, approaches a desk, and opens a drawer across three compatible angles.

### Discontinuity editing
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.discontinuity-editing -->

**Definition.** An approach that foregrounds interruptions or inconsistencies in spatial, temporal, graphic, or narrative flow. It can deliberately prevent the seamless connections associated with continuity editing.

**Use.** Identify what the interruption should make viewers notice or question. A deliberate discontinuity still benefits from a clear intention and enough context to support interpretation.

**Example.** A discussion repeatedly returns to an empty chair from incompatible angles, interrupting the otherwise stable room geography.

### Cross-cutting
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.cross-cutting -->

**Definition.** Alternation between distinct lines of action, often in different locations and often understood as simultaneous. Its relationship to “parallel editing” varies among writers and production teams.

**Use.** State explicitly whether the actions share a time, cause one another, or only resemble one another. The switching pattern can imply a connection that the story later confirms or complicates.

**Example.** A technician restores power while passengers wait in the stalled elevator served by that circuit.

### Parallel editing
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.parallel-editing -->

**Definition.** Alternating or juxtaposing lines of action to establish a relationship between them. It is often used interchangeably with cross-cutting; some traditions distinguish the terms by simultaneity or thematic comparison.

**Use.** Describe the actual temporal and thematic relationship instead of depending on a disputed boundary. This wiki keeps both labels searchable without treating one definition as universal.

**Example.** Preparations for a public banquet alternate with a family dividing a small evening meal.

### Montage
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.montage -->

**Definition.** The construction of meaning through the arrangement and juxtaposition of shots. In common production shorthand, a “montage sequence” often means a condensed series showing a process or passage of time; theoretical uses are broader.

**Use.** Specify whether the intention is compression, comparison, accumulation, or collision between ideas. A music track is optional.

**Example.** Repeated images of measuring, cutting, and repairing compress several weeks of building a small boat.

## Spatial and action continuity

### Shot/reverse shot
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.shot-reverse-shot -->

**Definition.** An alternating pattern of views toward opposing sides of an interaction, commonly two people speaking or looking at one another. The shots need not have identical framing or show literal opposites.

**Use.** Control whose experience dominates through duration, framing, and when reactions replace speech. Maintain readable relationships unless disorientation serves a specific purpose.

**Example.** A tenant explains a problem in medium close-up; the reverse view holds on the landlord's delayed response.

### Eyeline match
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.eyeline-match -->

**Definition.** An edit linking a person's look with a following image understood as what they are looking toward. Direction and angle help establish the spatial relationship, even when the shots were filmed separately.

**Use.** Check gaze height, screen position, and the implied distance to the object. A following view can suggest attention without necessarily becoming a literal optical point-of-view shot.

**Example.** A child looks upward and right; the next image shows a kite caught above the doorway.

### Match on action
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.match-on-action -->

**Definition.** A cut between views that carries an action across the edit with compatible movement and timing. The viewer follows the action rather than being distracted by the camera change.

**Use.** Compare body position, object state, movement direction, and the precise phase of the gesture. A tiny timing adjustment may feel smoother than mechanically identical frames.

**Example.** A hand begins opening a case in a wide shot and completes the movement in close-up.

### Screen direction
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.screen-direction -->

**Definition.** The direction a person, object, or look appears to travel or face within the image, such as left to right. It is a screen relationship, not necessarily a fixed compass direction.

**Use.** Preserve direction when it helps viewers follow a route, pursuit, or exchange. Establish a visible turn or new orientation when direction changes.

**Example.** A courier continues moving left to right across three street views until a clear turn reverses the route.

### 180-degree rule
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.180-degree-rule -->

**Definition.** A continuity guideline that keeps camera positions on one side of an established axis of action, helping preserve relative screen positions and directions. The axis may connect people or follow movement.

**Use.** Draw the axis before choosing coverage. Crossing it can work when movement, a new establishing view, or deliberate disorientation makes the change understandable.

**Example.** Two negotiators remain on opposite screen sides until a visible camera move circles behind one of them.

## Sound overlap and supporting shots

### J-cut
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.j-cut -->

**Definition.** A split edit in which incoming audio starts before the corresponding incoming image. The next shot or scene is therefore heard while the previous image is still visible.

**Use.** Lead attention toward a new speaker, place, or event. Decide how much the early sound reveals and whether it should first be interpreted as belonging to the outgoing image.

**Example.** Train wheels become audible over a sleeping passenger's home before the picture changes to the station.

### L-cut
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.l-cut -->

**Definition.** A split edit in which outgoing audio continues after the image changes to the next shot. The earlier sound remains present while viewers receive new visual information.

**Use.** Hold a thought across a reaction or scene change. Check whether the overlapping voice is continuing live dialogue, remembered speech, or an editorial connection.

**Example.** An engineer keeps explaining a fault while the image cuts from her face to the damaged assembly.

### Sound bridge
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.sound-bridge -->

**Definition.** Sound that connects adjacent shots or scenes across a visual boundary, through continuation, anticipation, or an associated sonic element. J-cuts and L-cuts are specific ways to create such a connection.

**Use.** Decide whether the bridge preserves a shared space or connects different times and places. Document where the sound belongs within the story.

**Example.** Rain continues across a cut from a courtyard to the room overlooking it, maintaining the same storm.

### Insert
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.insert -->

**Definition.** A shot emphasizing a detail within the principal action or scene, commonly a hand, object, display, or written information. Its function is to make a relevant element available to attention.

**Use.** Establish enough context for the detail to be understood and allow enough time for any required reading. An insert need not always be an extreme close-up.

**Example.** During a repair, an insert reveals that the replacement component carries the wrong serial number.

### Cutaway
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.cutaway -->

**Definition.** A shot that temporarily leaves the main action for a related subject, reaction, or surrounding detail. It may supply context, redirect attention, or provide room to omit part of the principal action.

**Use.** Check what viewers infer about simultaneity and location. The distinction from an insert is functional and can vary with how a scene is organized.

**Example.** A heated meeting cuts away to an assistant waiting outside the closed door.

## Duration and pacing

### Shot duration
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.shot-duration -->

**Definition.** The time a shot occupies in the finished sequence between its beginning and end. Duration can be measured precisely, but its experienced speed depends on activity, information, sound, and expectation.

**Use.** Record actual duration alongside the beat it must communicate. Avoid assuming that a numerical target guarantees tension, calm, or clarity.

**Example.** A six-second shot gives viewers time to notice a missing photograph before a character reacts to it.

### Cutting rate
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.cutting-rate -->

**Definition.** The frequency of cuts across a specified interval, sometimes described as cuts per minute. It summarizes one aspect of editing pace while concealing variation within that interval.

**Use.** Define the measurement window and examine the distribution of shot lengths. Compare performance tempo and event density separately rather than treating a high rate as automatic excitement.

**Example.** The final minute doubles the number of cuts while the characters themselves become increasingly motionless.

### Hold
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.hold -->

**Definition.** A deliberate continuation of a shot, pose, or moment beyond a point where a change might be expected. In editing discussion, “hold” usually means retaining the image rather than cutting away.

**Use.** Specify what remains active during the hold: a look, background movement, sound, or the viewer's uncertainty. Distinguish it from a literal freeze frame.

**Example.** After a visitor leaves, the shot holds on the host rearranging the untouched second cup.

### Pause
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.pause -->

**Definition.** A temporary reduction or suspension of action, speech, or narrative progression. A pause may occur within a moving shot and can retain substantial environmental sound or visual activity.

**Use.** Locate the interruption in a particular layer and decide what viewers should process during it. Length alone does not establish whether the pause feels reflective or uncomfortable.

**Example.** A witness stops speaking while traffic continues behind her, leaving the unfinished sentence unresolved.

### Ellipsis
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.ellipsis -->

**Definition.** The omission of part of an event or time interval from the presentation, allowing viewers to infer what occurred between the shown moments. It can be brief or span years.

**Use.** Preserve the information needed to understand the change while deciding what remains uncertain. A cut, dissolve, title, or sound transition may carry the omission.

**Example.** A baker starts kneading dough; the next shot shows the cooled loaves being packed for delivery.

### Repetition
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.repetition -->

**Definition.** The return of an image, action, sound, or structural unit within a sequence. Repetition can recreate the same material or show a recurring event with meaningful changes.

**Use.** Identify what stays stable and what changes at each recurrence. The audience learns a pattern, so even a small variation can become significant.

**Example.** Three mornings begin with the same doorway composition; on the fourth, the expected person never enters.

### Rhythmic pattern
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.rhythmic-pattern -->

**Definition.** An organized relationship among durations, accents, movements, and intervals across time. Editing rhythm can align with speech or music, work independently, or repeatedly shift between them.

**Use.** Describe the pattern in concrete terms, such as short–short–long or alternating impact and recovery. Test it with and without music to locate its actual source.

**Example.** Two brief preparation shots precede a longer attempt, and that three-part pattern repeats with increasing strain.

### Long take
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.long-take -->

**Definition.** A shot sustained for an unusually extended duration relative to its context, without an apparent cut. There is no universal number of seconds that makes a take “long.”

**Use.** Plan how attention develops inside the image through performance, blocking, movement, or changing information. A long take can contain rapid action and need not produce a slow experience.

**Example.** One uninterrupted view follows a cook managing several simultaneous orders during a crowded service.

### Sequence shot
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.sequence-shot -->

**Definition.** A shot that carries the dramatic work of a whole scene or substantial sequence within a continuous presentation. It is often a long take, but its defining emphasis is structural completeness.

**Use.** Identify the beginning, developments, and resolution that must occur inside the shot. Specify if the intended continuous appearance is assembled with concealed cuts.

**Example.** One moving shot follows an inspection from arrival through discovery of the fault to the decision to close the building.

### Accelerated montage
<!-- term-id: editing-transitions-and-pace.accelerated-montage -->

**Definition.** A montage whose succession of images becomes progressively faster, commonly through decreasing shot durations. “Accelerated” here describes the editing pattern rather than necessarily speeding up the footage itself.

**Use.** Set a perceivable baseline before increasing frequency, and retain enough information for the actions to register. Sound accents can support or resist the acceleration.

**Example.** Packing shots shorten as a departure approaches, ending on one sustained view of an item left behind.

## Practical distinctions

| Decision | Distinguish it from | Useful question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Match cut | Match on action | Are two images linked by resemblance, or is one continuous action being carried across views? |
| Insert | Cutaway | Is the detail part of the central action, or does the shot temporarily redirect attention elsewhere? |
| Long take | Sequence shot | Is the claim about relative duration, or about a whole dramatic unit occurring in one shot? |
| Cutting rate | Perceived pace | What changes in performance, motion, event density, and sound while cut frequency stays fixed? |
| Cross-cutting | Parallel editing | What timing relationship does this team intend, given the overlapping terminology? |

For split edits, the reliable distinction is the direction of sound overlap: incoming audio leads the picture in a J-cut; outgoing audio trails it in an L-cut. [Adobe's split-edit documentation](https://helpx.adobe.com/premiere/desktop/edit-projects/trim-clips/perform-j-cuts-and-l-cuts.html)

## Visual interaction and structured fields

These are proposed learning and authoring controls, not an existing editing application.

| Decision | Visual interaction | Proposed fields |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Transition choice | Two-image preview with draggable boundary | Transition type; duration; outgoing shot; incoming shot; intended relationship |
| Sound overlap | Separate image and audio strips with an audible loop | Picture cut time; audio start/end; sound source; J/L classification |
| Spatial continuity | Overhead axis diagram linked to shot thumbnails | Axis endpoints; camera side; gaze direction; movement direction; intentional crossing |
| Pace development | Timeline of shot lengths beside beat markers | Shot duration; event density note; performance tempo; sound rhythm; measurement window |
| Parallel actions | Reorderable lanes with shared or independent clocks | Action line; depicted time; sequence order; simultaneity claim; connecting idea |
| Ellipsis | Before/after comparison and omitted-time label | Shown endpoints; inferred interval; required information; intentional uncertainty |
