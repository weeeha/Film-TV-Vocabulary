# Creative Vocabulary — Film & TV Atlas

A living wiki of the choices that shape a film or television show: what it is about, who inhabits it, how the story unfolds, what the audience sees and hears, and how references guide the work.

**Status:** draft information architecture and starter vocabulary. This is a map for building the wiki, not an exhaustive dictionary or an implemented application. The groupings below are an editorial proposal, not a universal industry taxonomy. Terms can belong to several groups; definitions and terminology can vary across traditions and productions.

## What the wiki should let you do

1. **Learn:** understand a term through a plain-language definition and an example.
2. **Discover:** browse neighboring concepts, alternatives, and combinations.
3. **Direct:** select vocabulary to describe an actual character, scene, sequence, or shot.
4. **Collaborate with an agent:** edit the same creative decisions through structured fields, with changes visible to the person directing the work.

The recommended foundation is a connected wiki. An alphabetical glossary can be one view; visual controls can be another. Both should use the same underlying entries.

## Vocabulary map

The lists below are seed terms for future linked pages. They deliberately separate different dimensions instead of putting every kind of choice into one menu.

### 1. Form, format, and genre

- **Form:** fiction, documentary, experimental film, hybrid documentary-fiction.
- **Medium:** live action, animation, mixed media.
- **Release format:** feature, short, television episode, limited series, continuing series, anthology series.
- **Genre:** drama, comedy, thriller, horror, science fiction, fantasy, romance, mystery, crime, action, adventure, western, musical, historical film.
- **Genre combinations:** romantic comedy, science-fiction horror, crime thriller, tragicomedy.
- **Tone:** sincere, ironic, satirical, tragic, playful, contemplative, absurd, ominous, hopeful, melancholic.

Genre, tone, medium, and release format remain separate properties.

### 2. Premise, theme, and meaning

- Premise, logline, central dramatic question, theme, thematic argument, counterargument.
- Motif, symbol, allegory, metaphor, irony, ambiguity.
- Belonging, identity, power, responsibility, grief, memory, survival, justice, freedom, sacrifice.

The last list supplies possible themes, not a closed set of universal story types.

### 3. Story patterns and dramatic engines

- Quest, pursuit, escape, rescue, investigation, revenge, survival, transformation, coming of age, rise and fall, homecoming.
- Internal conflict, interpersonal conflict, institutional conflict, environmental conflict.
- Goal, obstacle, stakes, deadline, dilemma, escalation, reversal, consequence.

A story can combine several patterns. Its dramatic engine describes what keeps generating problems and choices.

### 4. Narrative structure and time

- Three-act structure, five-act structure, episodic structure, circular structure, braided narrative, frame narrative.
- Linear chronology, nonlinear chronology, parallel timelines, reverse chronology, real time.
- Setup, inciting incident, turning point, midpoint, crisis, climax, resolution, denouement.
- Beat, scene, sequence, act, subplot.
- Flashback, flashforward, ellipsis, repetition, time loop.

Named structure models should identify their source or tradition. They are tools for interpretation and construction, not mandatory formulas.

### 5. Television storytelling

- Episodic storytelling, serialized storytelling, hybrid episodic-serialized storytelling.
- Series engine, season arc, episode arc, recurring character arc.
- A-story, B-story, C-story, runner.
- Pilot, cold open, teaser, act break, cliffhanger, tag, finale.
- Bottle episode, standalone episode, two-part episode, anthology episode.
- Procedural reset, accumulating consequences, recurring conflict.

TV structure needs several scales at once: an episode can resolve its immediate problem while advancing a season-long relationship.

### 6. Character roles

- Protagonist, antagonist, deuteragonist, ensemble lead, supporting character.
- Foil, confidant, ally, rival, love interest, narrator, audience surrogate.
- Catalyst, gatekeeper, witness.

Role describes a character's function relative to a particular story. It does not determine personality or morality.

### 7. Character archetypes and construction

- **Archetypal patterns:** mentor, trickster, guardian, rebel, caregiver, explorer, ruler, outsider, innocent, sage.
- **Other character patterns:** antihero, tragic hero, reluctant hero, unreliable narrator.
- **Inner construction:** want, need, fear, belief, value, contradiction, flaw, strength, secret, formative experience.
- **Agency:** initiative, choice, resistance, complicity, responsibility.

Archetype systems disagree and overlap. Store the framework when relevant, and permit multiple labels and exceptions.

### 8. Character arcs and relationships

- Growth arc, decline arc, steadfast arc, redemption, corruption, disillusionment, reconciliation.
- Mentor–student, rivals, uneasy allies, adversaries, family, found family, partners.
- Trust, dependency, loyalty, jealousy, intimacy, alienation, betrayal, power imbalance.
- Relationship formation, rupture, repair, reversal, separation.

Relationship dynamics can change by scene. A character should not be permanently reduced to a single archetype or emotional score.

### 9. World and setting

- Era, geography, climate, ecology, settlement, architecture, infrastructure.
- Institutions, class, economy, customs, rituals, language, belief systems.
- Technology, magic, world rules, limitations, costs, taboos.
- Public space, private space, domestic space, workplace, wilderness, frontier, transit space.
- Interior, exterior, day, night, dawn, dusk, weather, season.

Setting describes where and when something happens; worldbuilding describes the wider conditions that make it possible.

### 10. Scenes and sequences

- **Dramatic function:** introduction, exposition, discovery, confrontation, negotiation, confession, decision, reversal, payoff, aftermath.
- **Activity:** chase, interrogation, meal, journey, battle, escape, reunion, farewell, ritual.
- **Information:** reveal, concealment, misdirection, dramatic irony, recognition.
- **Internal movement:** objective, obstacle, tactic, turning point, changed situation.
- **Sequence patterns:** preparation, heist, pursuit, investigation, training, rescue, siege.

One scene may be an interrogation by activity, a revelation by function, and a reversal in the larger plot. Do not force those into mutually exclusive types.

### 11. Perspective and audience experience

- Restricted narration, unrestricted narration, subjective narration, objective presentation, unreliable narration.
- Suspense, surprise, curiosity, anticipation, empathy, dread, relief, catharsis.
- Identification, emotional distance, withheld information, dramatic irony.
- Point-of-view shot, reaction shot, observational perspective.

Intended audience effects should be recorded as intentions or interpretations. A technique cannot guarantee a particular response.

### 12. Dialogue, performance, and blocking

- Subtext, exposition, banter, monologue, interruption, overlap, silence, understatement.
- Naturalistic performance, stylized performance, restrained performance, heightened performance, deadpan.
- Vocal rhythm, pause, breath, volume, gesture, posture, eyeline.
- Blocking, entrance, exit, crossing, proximity, separation, stillness, group arrangement.
- Objective, tactic, listening, reaction, status shift.

### 13. Production design and character appearance

- Set design, location dressing, props, hero prop, set dressing, practical fixture.
- Costume, makeup, hair, prosthetics, silhouette, material, texture, patina, wear.
- Palette, shape language, scale, density, repetition, visual hierarchy.
- Period authenticity, stylization, lived-in detail, visual continuity.

These entries connect story facts to visible evidence: occupation through tools, scarcity through repairs, authority through spatial arrangement.

### 14. Shot size, angle, and composition

- **Size:** extreme wide shot, wide shot, full shot, medium long shot, medium shot, medium close-up, close-up, extreme close-up.
- **Angle:** eye level, high angle, low angle, overhead, canted angle.
- **Subject arrangement:** single, two-shot, group shot, over-the-shoulder shot.
- **Function:** establishing shot, master shot, insert, cutaway, reaction shot, point-of-view shot.
- **Composition:** symmetry, asymmetry, central framing, rule of thirds, negative space, headroom, looking room, frame within a frame, leading lines, foreground layering.
- **Frame shape:** aspect ratio, portrait frame, widescreen frame, split screen.

A shot can be a low-angle medium close-up, framed asymmetrically, functioning as a reaction shot. These are compatible attributes, not competing choices.

### 15. Camera movement and support

- Pan, tilt, roll, dolly in, dolly out, lateral tracking, pedestal up, pedestal down, crane movement, orbit, arc, follow, lead.
- Locked-off camera, handheld camera, shoulder rig, stabilized camera, gimbal, Steadicam, vehicle mount, aerial camera.
- Motivated movement, unmotivated movement, reveal, reframing, whip pan.
- Path, direction, travel distance, duration, speed profile, acceleration, subject tracking.

Movement and support are distinct: a tracking movement can use several support systems. A zoom changes focal length; it is not camera translation. A dolly zoom combines both.

### 16. Lens, focus, and image capture

- Focal length, field of view, wide-angle lens, normal lens, telephoto lens, prime lens, zoom lens, anamorphic lens.
- Aperture, depth of field, shallow focus, deep focus, rack focus, focus distance.
- Exposure, frame rate, shutter angle, motion blur, slow motion, time-lapse.
- Sensor or film format, grain, digital noise, lens distortion, diffusion, flare.

Technical values need units and context. Focal length alone does not determine framing; camera position, format, and subject distance also matter.

### 17. Lighting and color

- **Role:** key light, fill light, backlight, rim light, background light.
- **Quality:** hard light, soft light, diffused light, bounced light.
- **Direction:** frontal, side, back, top, underlighting.
- **Approach:** high-key lighting, low-key lighting, silhouette, motivated lighting, available light, practical lighting.
- **Control:** intensity, contrast, falloff, negative fill, flags, diffusion, color temperature, tint.
- **Color treatment:** palette, saturation, hue, contrast, color separation, color grade, monochrome.

Keep scene lighting, camera exposure, and the final grade related but separately editable.

### 18. Editing, transitions, and pace

- Cut, dissolve, fade, wipe, match cut, jump cut, smash cut.
- Continuity editing, discontinuity editing, cross-cutting, parallel editing, montage.
- Shot/reverse shot, eyeline match, match on action, screen direction, 180-degree rule.
- J-cut, L-cut, sound bridge, insert, cutaway.
- Shot duration, cutting rate, hold, pause, ellipsis, repetition, rhythmic pattern.
- Long take, sequence shot, accelerated montage.

Pace is multidimensional: cutting frequency, performance tempo, camera motion, event density, and sound rhythm can move independently. Long takes can contain rapid action.

### 19. Sound, voice, and music

- Dialogue, production sound, ADR, voiceover, narration, Foley, sound effects, ambience, room tone.
- Diegetic sound, nondiegetic sound, on-screen sound, off-screen sound, subjective sound.
- Sound perspective, dynamic range, layering, silence, reverberation, distortion, sound bridge.
- Score, source music, cue, theme, leitmotif, drone, ostinato, sting.
- Tempo, rhythm, timbre, instrumentation, dissonance, consonance, musical counterpoint.

Audio entries need listening examples and timeline controls as well as visual diagrams.

### 20. Animation, effects, and mixed techniques

- 2D animation, 3D animation, stop motion, rotoscoping, motion capture.
- Key pose, timing, spacing, anticipation, follow-through, squash and stretch.
- Practical effects, miniatures, animatronics, compositing, matte painting, digital environment, motion tracking.
- Stylization, integration, continuity, interaction, simulation.

### 21. Documentary and nonfiction approaches

- Observational, participatory, expository, reflexive, poetic, performative approaches.
- Interview, direct address, archival footage, observational scene, reconstruction, reenactment, actuality.
- Narrator position, subject participation, evidence, attribution, uncertainty, editorial framing.

These approaches can overlap. Sources and the distinction between observed, reconstructed, and inferred material belong on the page.

### 22. References and creative constraints

- Reference work, specific scene, shot, timestamp, frame, sound cue, excerpt.
- Referenced dimension: composition, movement, lighting, palette, material, performance, rhythm, sound, narrative function.
- Reference intent, adaptation note, similarity target, exclusions, priority.
- Project decision state: candidate, working, established, open question.
- Constraint, preference, approved exception, continuity requirement.

Record what a reference contributes. “Use this scene's camera restraint and industrial ambience” is more actionable than “make it like this film.” Reference strength should be specific to a dimension and treated as a proposed application control, not an established filmmaking measurement.

## Connections matter as much as categories

Use relationships such as:

- **is a kind of:** close-up → shot size.
- **is often confused with:** dolly in ↔ zoom in.
- **can combine with:** close-up ↔ dolly in ↔ shallow focus.
- **can serve:** reaction shot → reveal a response.
- **contrasts with:** restricted narration ↔ unrestricted narration.
- **is illustrated by:** term → an attributed example and relevant timestamp.
- **is applied in:** vocabulary entry → a particular project decision.

Browse by category, alphabetical term, creative intention, or production scale. “Create unease” can lead to several techniques, but each link should explain context and tradeoffs rather than promise an emotional outcome.

## Keep these distinctions visible

| Object | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Vocabulary entry | A reusable concept or technique | Dolly in |
| Editable property | One dimension of a creative choice | Movement duration |
| Recipe | An editable combination of decisions | A slow approach during a discovery |
| Project instance | A particular use in a particular work | Shot 12 in Scene 3 |
| Reference | Evidence or inspiration attached to a dimension | A clip illustrating movement |

A definition belongs to the shared wiki. Changing one film's shot should change its instance, not the shared meaning of “dolly in.”

## Reusable entry template

Each term page should contain:

1. **Stable ID, name, aliases, category, and applicable scale.**
2. **Plain definition:** what makes this concept distinct.
3. **Example:** diagram, short clip, audio sample, or annotated story excerpt, with provenance.
4. **Creative use:** what it can contribute and the contexts in which it helps.
5. **Limitations and common confusions.**
6. **Related terms, alternatives, and compatible combinations.**
7. **Visual interaction:** the appropriate way to inspect or manipulate it.
8. **Structured representation:** fields, allowed values, units, and optional free text.
9. **Sources and editorial status:** including disagreements between definitions.

Not every entry needs a slider. An archetype benefits from comparison cards and relationship diagrams; a movement benefits from a path preview; sound benefits from playback. Some concepts are descriptive tags rather than controllable quantities.

## Example entry: Dolly in

**ID:** `camera.movement.dolly_in`<br>
**Kind:** camera movement<br>
**Scale:** shot

**Definition:** the camera moves physically toward the subject or a chosen point in the scene.

**Possible creative uses:** progressively emphasize a detail, approach a character, or alter the audience's spatial relationship to an event. Meaning depends on performance, context, framing, sound, and editing.

**Common confusion:** zooming in changes focal length while the camera may stay in place. Moving the camera changes viewpoint; the resulting perspective and parallax differ from a stationary zoom.

**Visual interaction:** a top-down camera path paired with start/end frame previews and a duration control. Where no scene geometry exists, the preview should be labeled schematic.

**Editable properties:** target, path, travel distance, duration, speed profile, start/end framing, and whether orientation tracks the target.

**Related:** dolly out, tracking shot, zoom in, dolly zoom, reframing.

## Visual and structured forms of the same decision

The following is an illustrative content model, not an implemented renderer or a provider-specific generation API:

```json
{
  "schema_version": "0.1-draft",
  "id": "shot_012",
  "scene_id": "scene_003",
  "decision_status": "candidate",
  "narrative": {
    "scene_functions": ["discovery"],
    "intended_effect": "growing unease"
  },
  "framing": {
    "start_size": "medium_shot",
    "end_size": "medium_close_up",
    "angle": "eye_level"
  },
  "camera": {
    "movement_term_id": "camera.movement.dolly_in",
    "target_id": "character_01",
    "duration_seconds": 8,
    "speed_profile": "constant",
    "travel_distance_meters": null
  },
  "lighting": {
    "key_quality": "soft",
    "key_direction": "side",
    "fill_intent": "minimal"
  },
  "editing": {
    "cut_within_shot": false
  },
  "references": [
    {
      "reference_id": "reference_01",
      "dimensions": ["camera_movement"],
      "influence": "moderate",
      "adaptation_note": "Use the restrained approach; choose framing for this scene."
    }
  ]
}
```

`reference_01` and `character_01` are illustrative placeholders. `null` means the travel distance has not been decided; it should not silently become a default. The sample specifies intended framing, not a physically solved camera setup.

| Choice | Possible interaction | Structured form |
|---|---|---|
| Archetype | Compare cards and select several | List of term IDs plus notes |
| Character arc | Arrange labeled turning points | Ordered beats and relationships |
| Scene function | Select tags on a scene card | List of function IDs |
| Camera movement | Edit a path and scrub a preview | Path, target, duration, units |
| Lighting | Move lights in a diagram | Direction, quality, intensity, color |
| Pace | Adjust several tracks on a timeline | Shot durations, pauses, event density |
| Framing | Compare or manipulate frame diagrams | Size, angle, placement, aspect ratio |
| Reference influence | Choose influence per dimension | Reference ID, dimension, labeled strength |

When an agent changes a value, the visual view should reflect the same saved field. Free-text direction remains available for ideas the vocabulary does not yet express. Creative intentions must remain distinct from achieved or verified results.

## Suggested first content pass

Start by giving full pages to characters, scene functions, story structure, shot language, lighting, editing, sound, and references. Use the remaining categories to preserve coverage while expanding the dictionary. Add named film examples only after checking that they demonstrate the claimed technique.

The useful measure of progress is the number of well-defined, connected, illustrated entries—not the length of an unreviewed list. A comprehensive wiki grows through explicit coverage and revision; it should never claim that these labels exhaust all possible cinema.

## Sources and research starting points

- [Yale Film Analysis](https://filmanalysis.yale.edu/) demonstrates a searchable, cross-linked film vocabulary with illustrative clips. It is a reference for the wiki approach, not the source of this entire proposed taxonomy.
- [BFI Replay: Inventing Film Language](https://replay.bfi.org.uk/collection/419) provides historical examples of techniques including close-ups, camera movement, editing, and continuity.
- [BBC: Screenplay Format for TV Shows](https://downloads.bbc.co.uk/writersroom/scripts/screenplaytv.pdf) is a source for television screenplay presentation conventions; screenplay formatting is distinct from the creative taxonomy above.

The taxonomy, page model, and control mappings in this draft are proposals. The individual glossary pages still require source checks, illustrated examples, and editorial review.
