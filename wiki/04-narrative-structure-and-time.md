# 04 · Narrative structure and time

[Wiki home](../README.md) · [Alphabetical index](../INDEX.md)

Structure describes relationships among parts: how events develop, how information is arranged, where attention shifts, and what changes as a result. Time adds a second distinction: when events occur in the story world versus when and for how long the work presents them.

Named models are tools, not requirements. Aristotle's beginning, middle, and end should not be treated as proof that every screenplay must use modern three-act proportions. His discussion belongs to a particular theory of dramatic action. [Aristotle: Poetics, section VII](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1974/1974-h/1974-h.htm)

## Large-scale organization

### Three-act structure
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.three-act-structure -->

**Definition.** An organizing model that groups a story into three broad movements, often setup, confrontation, and resolution in Syd Field's screenwriting tradition. It describes changes of dramatic situation rather than requiring three visible title cards or equal durations. [Syd Field's paradigm](https://sydfield.com/syd_resources/the-paradigm-worksheet/)

**Use.** Test how the initial conditions develop into sustained struggle and a concluding movement. Treat proposed timing as an adjustable model, not a universal law.

**Example.** A clerk discovers forged permits, attempts to expose them, then must decide what evidence she will publicly sacrifice her career to defend.

### Five-act structure
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.five-act-structure -->

**Definition.** An arrangement dividing a dramatic work into five major sections. Five theatrical acts, a five-act television script, and Freytag's five-part dramatic progression are related historical usages, not interchangeable systems; Freytag distinguishes introduction, rise, high point, fall, and catastrophe. [Freytag: Die Technik des Dramas](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/50616/50616-h/50616-h.htm)

**Use.** State the chosen tradition and each division's function before applying labels or timing.

**Example.** A production divides a succession dispute into five performed acts, with each interval following a change in who can claim the throne.

### Episodic structure
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.episodic-structure -->

**Definition.** An arrangement built from relatively distinct incidents or units, often linked by a traveler, setting, concern, or recurring situation rather than one tightly continuous causal chain. It can organize a feature or novel as well as television; it does not automatically imply disconnected meaning.

**Use.** Let variation, comparison, and accumulation connect locally shaped experiences.

**Example.** A film follows a repair worker through five unrelated homes, each revealing a different attitude toward keeping broken things.

### Circular structure
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.circular-structure -->

**Definition.** An arrangement that returns to an opening situation, location, image, or pattern in a way that invites comparison. The return can reveal change, entrapment, renewal, or uncertainty; it does not require events literally to repeat in the story world.

**Use.** Let the ending alter the meaning of something viewers first encountered at the beginning.

**Example.** A film opens and closes with a woman waiting at the same station, but the second wait is voluntary.

### Braided narrative
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.braided-narrative -->

**Definition.** An arrangement that interweaves two or more sustained story strands, allowing their alternation to create contrast, connection, or delayed convergence. The strands can share a time period or span different eras; occasional cutaways alone do not necessarily form a developed braid.

**Use.** Let one strand change the significance of another without collapsing their differences.

**Example.** A dam engineer's inspection alternates with a displaced farmer's appeal and a child's exploration of the dry riverbed.

### Frame narrative
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.frame-narrative -->

**Definition.** A narrative situation that contains or introduces another telling, such as a testimony, remembered account, manuscript, or story told to a listener. The outer frame can affect how the embedded story is interpreted, including who controls its presentation and what remains uncertain.

**Use.** Make storytelling itself part of the dramatic situation.

**Example.** During a hearing, a retired navigator recounts a lost voyage while the person questioning her gradually reveals knowledge of events she omitted.

## Chronological arrangements

### Linear chronology
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.linear-chronology -->

**Definition.** Presentation that proceeds broadly in the order events occur in the story world. Linear storytelling can still omit intervals, change viewpoint, or compress years; it means ordered progression rather than continuous coverage of every moment.

**Use.** Keep attention on developing causes, decisions, and consequences without major temporal rearrangement.

**Example.** A film follows a shopkeeper from the morning she receives an eviction notice through the evening she decides to contest it.

### Nonlinear chronology
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.nonlinear-chronology -->

**Definition.** Presentation that departs substantially from the chronological order of story events. It can withhold causes, juxtapose life stages, revisit earlier situations, or arrange material by association; complexity of presentation should not be confused with complexity of the underlying events.

**Use.** Control when viewers can reinterpret motives and consequences.

**Example.** A farewell is shown before the friendship begins, making ordinary early gestures carry the knowledge that the relationship will end.

### Parallel timelines
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.parallel-timelines -->

**Definition.** Two or more temporal tracks developed alongside one another in the telling. They may follow separate periods, simultaneous events, or alternative versions of events; the project should specify which meaning applies rather than relying on the label alone.

**Use.** Compare developments and reveal connections across temporal contexts.

**Example.** A present-day bridge inspection alternates with its construction forty years earlier, progressively explaining why one support was never properly documented.

### Reverse chronology
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.reverse-chronology -->

**Definition.** An arrangement presenting successive narrative units in an order that moves backward through story time. Individual scenes may still unfold forward internally; reverse chronology differs from a film that merely opens near the ending and then resumes a normal sequence.

**Use.** Make earlier causes revise the meaning of consequences already witnessed.

**Example.** Each scene occurs one day before the last, gradually transforming an apparent act of generosity into evidence of a calculated betrayal.

### Real time
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.real-time -->

**Definition.** Presentation in which elapsed screen time broadly corresponds to elapsed time within the represented action. Real-time storytelling can use cuts and multiple locations; it does not require a single take, and interruptions or omissions should be identified when comparing durations.

**Use.** Make waiting, urgency, and limited opportunity tangible through shared duration.

**Example.** A twenty-minute episode follows the final twenty minutes before a ferry departs, cutting among passengers without skipping time.

## Dramatic developments

### Setup
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.setup -->

**Definition.** Material establishing conditions, relationships, rules, information, or expectations that later developments depend on. Setup can occur throughout a work and may serve an immediate scene as well as a later payoff; it is not limited to introductory exposition.

**Use.** Make later actions understandable while giving early details present dramatic value.

**Example.** A mechanic struggles with a door that opens only when lifted, establishing the solution to a later trapped-room problem.

### Inciting incident
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.inciting-incident -->

**Definition.** An event that significantly disturbs the initial situation and initiates the story's principal developing problem or opportunity. Different analysts may locate it differently, especially when disturbance, discovery, and commitment occur separately; its function matters more than an imposed page number.

**Use.** Identify what makes the current story begin rather than continue as ordinary routine.

**Example.** A clerk opens a routine return envelope and finds the identity card of a person officially recorded as never having existed.

### Turning point
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.turning-point -->

**Definition.** A development that changes the direction, conditions, understanding, or strategy of an ongoing action. Turning points can occur at many scales; a major act transition is one use, but a small scene can also turn on a decisive response.

**Use.** Mark where characters must reconsider what they are doing or what their situation means.

**Example.** The clerk's supervisor admits the missing record was removed on purpose, turning an administrative inquiry into a question of complicity.

### Midpoint
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.midpoint -->

**Definition.** The approximate middle of a work or a significant development placed there in some structural models. A midpoint may reframe the goal, increase commitment, or reveal information, but not every narrative needs a special reversal at precisely half its runtime.

**Use.** Examine whether the middle changes the terms of the ongoing action.

**Example.** Halfway through a search, the protagonist finds the missing person, who insists that returning home would endanger everyone involved.

### Crisis
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.crisis -->

**Definition.** A point of acute pressure where existing approaches fail or a consequential choice becomes unavoidable. Some frameworks reserve crisis for the decision before the climax; others use it more broadly, so the intended scale and tradition should be stated.

**Use.** Bring incompatible commitments into a situation that requires action.

**Example.** The clerk must choose between releasing unverified evidence immediately and allowing a scheduled purge to destroy the only people who could verify it.

### Climax
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.climax -->

**Definition.** A decisive concentration of action or confrontation through which a principal conflict reaches its determining outcome. It may be quiet or spectacular, and some narratives distribute several climaxes across strands; it is not necessarily the loudest or most expensive sequence.

**Use.** Make accumulated choices and pressures produce a consequential result.

**Example.** At the hearing, the clerk publicly identifies herself as the official who approved the first erasure, making the evidence impossible to dismiss anonymously.

### Resolution
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.resolution -->

**Definition.** The settling, transformation, or deliberate leaving open of the central situation after its decisive developments. Resolution can answer a practical question without resolving every emotional or thematic uncertainty; a bleak or ambiguous outcome can still establish a new state.

**Use.** Show what the main action has changed and which uncertainties remain intentional.

**Example.** The erased names are restored, but the clerk loses her position and must rebuild trust with those she helped endanger.

### Denouement
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.denouement -->

**Definition.** The concluding unfolding of consequences, explanations, and relationships after the principal climax. Often overlapping with resolution, denouement emphasizes the experience of aftermath rather than simply the answer to the main dramatic question; some works make it very brief or omit it.

**Use.** Let viewers understand and feel the changed situation.

**Example.** Months after the hearing, the former clerk helps a restored resident fill out an ordinary library form under her recovered name.

## Units of organization

### Beat
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.beat -->

**Definition.** A small unit of dramatic action, information, or change within a larger passage. In performance and script notation, beat can also mean a pause; a project should distinguish a story beat from a timed silence to avoid ambiguity.

**Use.** Track shifts in intention, tactic, knowledge, or response within a scene.

**Example.** A daughter asks for money, notices her father's packed suitcase, and changes her request to an invitation to stay.

### Scene
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.scene -->

**Definition.** A bounded unit of presented action, commonly organized around a particular time and place, whose participants, activity, or situation can be considered together. Dramatic scenes and production scene headings do not always align exactly, especially during intercut action or extended sequences.

**Use.** Give a local encounter a clear purpose and developing condition.

**Example.** In a closed ticket office, a passenger bargains with the night clerk until both realize the missing ticket belongs to the clerk's son.

### Sequence
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.sequence -->

**Definition.** A larger unit made of connected scenes or passages that pursue a shared action, situation, or developmental phase. Sequence boundaries are analytical or creative decisions rather than universal fixed lengths; a sequence can span several locations and shifts in time.

**Use.** Plan an extended action with preparation, changing conditions, and a local outcome.

**Example.** An evacuation sequence moves from the warning broadcast through door-to-door searches to the final departure from the flooded street.

### Act
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.act -->

**Definition.** A major division of a dramatic work or a structural interpretation of its progression. Acts can be marked in a script, separated by performance intervals or broadcast breaks, or inferred during analysis; these uses need not correspond to identical story functions.

**Use.** Organize substantial movements while specifying the project's formatting and structural convention.

**Example.** A television script ends its second labeled act when the rescue team discovers that the distress signal originated inside its own vehicle.

### Subplot
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.subplot -->

**Definition.** A secondary but developed line of action related to a larger narrative through characters, causality, contrast, or theme. A subplot has its own progression; a passing detail or isolated supporting scene does not necessarily constitute one.

**Use.** Complicate the main action or examine a related question through different stakes.

**Example.** While the clerk investigates erased records, her apprentice secretly prepares to leave the profession, testing a different response to the institution's failures.

## Temporal devices

### Flashback
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.flashback -->

**Definition.** A presented passage depicting events earlier than the narrative's current temporal reference point. A flashback can be a memory, testimony, or impersonal presentation; its temporal position alone does not establish that it is subjective, reliable, or complete.

**Use.** Supply or revise knowledge of a past event when it matters to current interpretation.

**Example.** After a witness denies knowing the station, an earlier scene shows her hiding a suitcase beneath its bench.

### Flashforward
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.flashforward -->

**Definition.** A presented passage depicting events later than the narrative's current temporal reference point. It can show an actual later event, a conditional possibility, or a character's imagined future; the work should make its status clear or make the uncertainty intentional.

**Use.** Introduce anticipation or make present choices carry knowledge of a possible consequence.

**Example.** Before the bridge opens, the film briefly shows its empty control room covered in flood silt several months later.

### Ellipsis
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.ellipsis -->

**Definition.** The omission of a span of story time from the presented action. An ellipsis can be indicated clearly or left for viewers to infer, and its duration may be seconds or years; omitted time differs from merely presenting events more quickly.

**Use.** Remove routine transitions, create gaps in knowledge, or emphasize changed conditions.

**Example.** A cut moves from an apprentice signing a contract to the same hand, visibly older, tearing up its renewal.

### Repetition
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.repetition -->

**Definition.** The renewed presentation of an event, image, action, or narrative unit. Repetition can revisit the same occurrence or show different occurrences of a similar pattern; those possibilities should be distinguished because they create different relationships between story and presentation.

**Use.** Invite comparison through altered context, viewpoint, duration, or missing information.

**Example.** The same farewell appears three times, each revealing a different person listening from behind the station wall.

### Time loop
<!-- term-id: narrative-structure-and-time.time-loop -->

**Definition.** A story-world condition in which a period or pattern of events recurs, often with some participants retaining knowledge across iterations. Rules about memory, reset triggers, and exceptions vary; a circular ending or repeated edit alone does not establish a literal loop.

**Use.** Make learning, experimentation, and changing choices visible against recurring circumstances.

**Example.** Every failed evacuation returns the engineer to breakfast, but each reset leaves one additional crack in the kitchen window.

## Practical distinctions

Keep two timelines when chronology matters: the order events occur and the order audiences encounter them. Add a third duration track if the work compresses, omits, repeats, or stretches time. A flashback changes presentation order; an ellipsis omits coverage; a time loop changes what happens within the fictional world.

A turning point describes a change of direction. A climax describes decisive culmination. A crisis often names the pressured choice leading into that culmination, but usage varies. Resolution and denouement can overlap, so use them as functional notes rather than forcing every ending into two separate compartments.

No act model, midpoint convention, or hero's-journey outline is a requirement. Record the framework, the reason for using it, and where the particular work departs from it.

## Visual interaction and structured fields

These are proposals for a future story editor, not implemented controls or automatic quality judgments.

| Decision | Visual interaction | Proposed data fields |
|---|---|---|
| Compare event and presentation order | Two synchronized timelines connected by event IDs | `event_id`, `story_order`, `presentation_order`, `story_time`, `screen_duration_seconds` |

| Describe loop rules | Iteration lanes showing retained state and reset conditions | `iteration_id`, `reset_trigger`, `retained_state`, `loop_exceptions` |
