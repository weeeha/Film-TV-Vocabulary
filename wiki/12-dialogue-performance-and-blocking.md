# 12 · Dialogue, performance, and blocking

[Wiki home](../README.md) · [Alphabetical index](../INDEX.md)

Performance turns written possibilities into observable behavior. This chapter separates what is said, how it is delivered, where bodies move, and what a character is trying to accomplish. None of these labels is a score for acting quality. A restrained performance can be intensely active; a silent person can control a scene.

The examples are original, hypothetical teaching situations. Descriptions such as “naturalistic” depend on a production's conventions and its audience, rather than defining a single correct way for people to behave.

## Dialogue and implication

### Subtext
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.subtext -->

**Definition.** Meaning, desire, or tension communicated beneath the literal words. Subtext may emerge through context, delivery, avoidance, or contradictory behavior; it does not require a consciously hidden message in every line.

**Use.** Give a conversation more than one level while preserving playable intentions. The National Theatre's rehearsal discussion connects subtext to what speakers seek from one another. [National Theatre, *Three Sisters* rehearsal pack](https://images.nationaltheatre.org.uk/uploads/2022/10/BPA-three-sisters-pack.pdf).

**Example.** “You kept the receipt?” sounds practical, but the speaker is testing whether the gift was chosen reluctantly.

### Exposition
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.exposition -->

**Definition.** Information that helps the audience understand circumstances, relationships, history, or rules. In dialogue, exposition is spoken information; it can also be carried by action and design elsewhere in the film.

**Use.** Supply what the scene needs without making characters explain facts solely for the viewer. Give the information a present purpose, such as persuasion or correction.

**Example.** A tenant refuses the landlord's offer: “You promised repairs before last winter, too.”

### Banter
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.banter -->

**Definition.** A conversational exchange built around playful challenge, teasing, or quick replies. Its surface lightness can coexist with affection, competitiveness, resentment, or unequal permission to joke.

**Use.** Establish relational familiarity and verbal rhythm. Distinguish mutual play from a situation in which one person laughs only to remain accepted.

**Example.** Two mechanics trade jokes about each other's repairs until one mentions a failed inspection and the other stops smiling.

### Monologue
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.monologue -->

**Definition.** An extended stretch of speech by one person. Other characters may be present and listening; a monologue is not necessarily a soliloquy, direct address, or narration.

**Use.** Sustain a particular viewpoint while allowing changes in tactic, thought, and listener response to provide movement within the speech.

**Example.** A retiring conductor recounts a railway accident, first entertaining the room, then gradually admitting his own responsibility.

### Interruption
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.interruption -->

**Definition.** An event that cuts into an ongoing utterance or action. Another speaker, a sound, or the original speaker's self-correction can interrupt; sustained simultaneous speech is not required.

**Use.** Change conversational control, prevent information from landing, or show urgent recognition. Specify the trigger so the timing can be repeated across takes.

**Example.** Before a witness names the visitor, the lawyer cuts in with a question about the weather.

### Overlap
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.overlap -->

**Definition.** Two or more voices sounding at the same time. Overlap may result from interruption, agreement, a shared routine, or competing conversations; it is an audible relationship, not automatically a conflict.

**Use.** Build group texture or urgency while deciding which words must remain intelligible. Sound recording and editorial flexibility affect how it is staged.

**Example.** Siblings finish different versions of the same family story over one another.

### Silence
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.silence -->

**Definition.** The absence of speech during a meaningful interval. Performance silence may still contain breathing, movement, environmental sound, music, and active thought; it does not imply an empty soundtrack.

**Use.** Let a question remain unanswered, allow attention to shift, or make nonverbal behavior carry a choice. Its meaning depends on preceding and subsequent action.

**Example.** Offered an apology, a shopkeeper quietly turns the sign from open to closed.

### Understatement
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.understatement -->

**Definition.** Expression that deliberately gives less verbal or emotional emphasis than the circumstances might conventionally invite. It differs from simply lacking clarity or failing to respond.

**Use.** Suggest control, social convention, humor, or an attempt to contain feeling. The surrounding evidence must let the audience perceive what has been reduced.

**Example.** Surveying a flooded kitchen, the homeowner says, “We may need another towel,” while lifting a floating chair.

## Performance approaches

### Naturalistic performance
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.naturalistic-performance -->

**Definition.** Acting organized to resemble plausible everyday behavior within the work's social and aesthetic conventions. Hesitation, interruption, and subtle response may contribute, but no checklist makes a performance naturalistic.

**Use.** Encourage attention to believable circumstances and relationships. Naturalism is constructed and rehearsable; it does not mean the actor simply behaves as themselves.

**Example.** A cashier continues sorting coins while trying to conceal concern about an unexpected visitor.

### Stylized performance
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.stylized-performance -->

**Definition.** Acting that uses an evident formal pattern beyond ordinary behavioral imitation, such as choreographed gestures, patterned speech, or deliberately artificial timing.

**Use.** Establish a coherent performance language for satire, ritual, fantasy, or other forms. Consistency among performers can matter more than resemblance to daily conversation.

**Example.** Every official in a fictional ministry repeats the same precise hand movement before delivering a refusal.

### Restrained performance
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.restrained-performance -->

**Definition.** Acting with limited outward display relative to the character's situation or the production's baseline. Emotion and intention remain active even when vocal and physical changes are small.

**Use.** Make minor departures from habitual behavior consequential. Camera distance, sound, and editing need to preserve the details on which the performance depends.

**Example.** A normally meticulous archivist misaligns one folder after learning that her colleague has disappeared.

### Heightened performance
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.heightened-performance -->

**Definition.** Acting whose vocal, physical, or emotional expression is amplified beyond a production's ordinary register. It can still follow psychologically coherent intentions and need not become caricature.

**Use.** Meet the expressive demands of melodrama, farce, musical scenes, or moments of rupture. Define the baseline so intensity has a recognizable relationship to the surrounding work.

**Example.** A mayor's ceremonial confidence swells into theatrical outrage when the audience begins laughing.

### Deadpan
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.deadpan -->

**Definition.** Delivery that maintains a notably matter-of-fact or unexpressive surface despite surprising, absurd, or emotionally charged content. Deadpan is a specific contrast between presentation and circumstances.

**Use.** Let the audience perceive incongruity without an actor visibly announcing the joke. It can also suggest habit, concealment, or institutional indifference.

**Example.** A hotel clerk calmly adds “minor ceiling collapse” to a guest's list of room preferences.

## Voice and bodily expression

### Vocal rhythm
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.vocal-rhythm -->

**Definition.** The pattern of speech timing, stress, duration, and phrasing across an utterance. Rhythm includes variation and rests; it is more specific than speaking quickly or slowly.

**Use.** Differentiate thought processes, relationships, or changes of tactic. A rhythmic shift may matter more than the overall pace.

**Example.** A negotiator speaks in clipped units while reading terms, then lengthens the final phrase when asking for trust.

### Pause
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.pause -->

**Definition.** A temporary break within speech or action. A pause has a position and duration relative to something continuing; silence is the broader condition of not speaking.

**Use.** Mark reconsideration, a withheld response, or time needed to register information. Give the performer a reason for the pause rather than only a prescribed delay.

**Example.** A sister pauses before saying “our house,” deciding whether to include her estranged brother.

### Breath
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.breath -->

**Definition.** The audible or visible pattern of inhalation, exhalation, and breath suspension within a performance. Breath supports voice and can reveal effort, anticipation, regulation, or a change of thought.

**Use.** Connect speech to physical action and let transitions occur before words arrive. Avoid treating any single breathing pattern as proof of a particular emotion.

**Example.** A runner steadies her breathing before recording a message that claims everything is ordinary.

### Volume
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.volume -->

**Definition.** The relative loudness of a performer's vocal delivery. Performance volume and the final soundtrack level are related but distinct: a whispered line can be mixed prominently.

**Use.** Establish who must hear, who might overhear, and how effort changes during a scene. Discuss vocal behavior separately from microphone placement or playback loudness.

**Example.** A parent lowers their voice as an argument reaches the doorway of a sleeping child's room.

### Gesture
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.gesture -->

**Definition.** A meaningful or expressive bodily movement, often involving hands, arms, face, or head. A gesture can be deliberate communication, habitual action, or behavior whose significance the character does not recognize.

**Use.** Externalize a tactic or reveal contradiction without making every movement symbolic. Consider cultural context and continuity across coverage.

**Example.** A candidate verbally welcomes questions while repeatedly guiding the interviewer toward the exit.

### Posture
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.posture -->

**Definition.** The body's sustained alignment or attitude in space, including how weight, spine, shoulders, and head are organized. Posture is a condition that may change, whereas a gesture is a movement.

**Use.** Express occupation, fatigue, habit, or a relationship to the room without reducing personality to a body type.

**Example.** A retired soldier initially stands through a meeting, then finally accepts the chair offered by his daughter.

### Eyeline
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.eyeline -->

**Definition.** The direction and apparent target of a character's gaze. In production, an eyeline also identifies where a performer should look so shots describe a coherent spatial relationship.

**Use.** Establish attention, avoidance, or an unseen presence. Match target height and screen direction when coverage requires continuity; looking at the lens creates a different relationship.

**Example.** A witness answers the detective but watches the reflection of someone behind him.

## Blocking and spatial relationships

### Blocking
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.blocking -->

**Definition.** The planned positions, movements, and physical interactions of performers through a scene. It includes relationships to furniture, props, entrances, and camera coverage, rather than merely marks on the floor.

**Use.** Turn dramatic changes into spatial events and coordinate repeatable action. Rehearsal can discover blocking as well as execute a predetermined plan.

**Example.** Each failed request moves a daughter farther from the dinner table until her mother must stand to continue speaking.

### Entrance
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.entrance -->

**Definition.** A character's arrival into a scene, playing area, or visible frame. Entering the room and entering the camera's frame may occur at different moments.

**Use.** Control when presence becomes known and how it alters an existing arrangement. An entrance can be announced by sound before anyone becomes visible.

**Example.** Work boots appear beneath the office door before the employee hears that the building owner has arrived.

### Exit
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.exit -->

**Definition.** A character's departure from the scene's space or from the visible frame. An off-screen character may remain nearby and continue influencing the action.

**Use.** End participation, transfer attention, or leave an unresolved relationship behind. Specify whether the departure is geographical, conversational, or simply outside the shot.

**Example.** A manager leaves the frame, but her keys remain on the table and her voice continues from the corridor.

### Crossing
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.crossing -->

**Definition.** A performer's movement from one position to another across the playing space or frame. A cross can alter sightlines and relationships even when dialogue continues uninterrupted.

**Use.** Make a tactic physical, reveal or conceal another subject, and create opportunities for reframing. Mark start, destination, and dramatic trigger.

**Example.** While denying interest in the letter, a guest crosses from the fireplace to the desk where it lies.

### Proximity
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.proximity -->

**Definition.** The closeness of characters to one another or to a significant object. Its meaning depends on personal, cultural, spatial, and situational expectations rather than a universal distance scale.

**Use.** Explore access, trust, intimidation, or practical cooperation. Record actual distance only when measurement helps reproduce the staging.

**Example.** Two rivals must lean toward the same failing radio, briefly sharing a space they have otherwise avoided.

### Separation
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.separation -->

**Definition.** Spatial distance or an intervening boundary between participants. Separation can exist despite physical closeness when furniture, orientation, or another body prevents contact and shared attention.

**Use.** Make a relationship legible through space while leaving its interpretation open to context. A divided arrangement can precede reconciliation or protect a character's privacy.

**Example.** A glass partition lets a mother and son see each other but prevents their attempted embrace.

### Stillness
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.stillness -->

**Definition.** A sustained reduction of visible bodily movement. Stillness can contain intense attention, preparation, resistance, or exhaustion; it is not equivalent to inactivity or emotional emptiness.

**Use.** Create contrast with surrounding motion or make a small change readable. Distinguish intentional stillness from freezing because a performer has no playable action.

**Example.** As everyone rushes to pack, one resident remains seated with the house key resting in her palm.

### Group arrangement
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.group-arrangement -->

**Definition.** The spatial organization of several people, including clusters, rows, gaps, orientation, and shared or competing centers of attention. It describes collective blocking rather than only individual positions.

**Use.** Show affiliation and access without explanatory dialogue. Track how the arrangement changes when someone enters, defects, or becomes the focus.

**Example.** Workers begin in separate lunch groups and gradually form one semicircle around a dismissed colleague.

## Playable action and response

### Objective
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.objective -->

**Definition.** What a character wants to achieve in a particular stretch of action. A useful performance objective concerns an outcome, often involving another person, rather than an instruction to display an emotion.

**Use.** Give speech and movement direction while permitting multiple tactics. Scene objectives can conflict with a character's wider goals or stated intentions.

**Example.** During a farewell, the visitor's immediate objective is to make the host ask them to stay.

### Tactic
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.tactic -->

**Definition.** A specific approach a character uses to pursue an objective, such as persuading, flattering, challenging, distracting, or reassuring. Tactics can change while the desired outcome remains constant.

**Use.** Replace vague emotional instructions with actions aimed at a partner. The National Theatre describes such work through what speakers try to elicit from each other. [*Three Sisters* rehearsal pack](https://images.nationaltheatre.org.uk/uploads/2022/10/BPA-three-sisters-pack.pdf).

**Example.** To borrow the van, a brother first jokes, then bargains, then admits why he needs it.

### Listening
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.listening -->

**Definition.** Active reception and interpretation of another person's words and behavior within a performance. Listening can change the listener's next action before any spoken response occurs.

**Use.** Keep exchanges responsive rather than making each actor wait for a cue. The National Theatre's rehearsal account emphasizes attention to the acting partner. [*Three Sisters* rehearsal pack](https://images.nationaltheatre.org.uk/uploads/2022/10/BPA-three-sisters-pack.pdf).

**Example.** A prepared objection disappears when the speaker hears an unexpectedly personal reason for the decision.

### Reaction
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.reaction -->

**Definition.** A response to perceived information, action, or sensation. It can be vocal, physical, delayed, suppressed, or displaced into another task; it is not restricted to a facial expression.

**Use.** Show that an event has changed someone's internal or practical situation. Distinguish the performed response from a reaction shot, which is a framing or editorial function.

**Example.** Hearing a familiar name, a cook turns off the burner before asking who called.

### Status shift
<!-- term-id: dialogue-performance-and-blocking.status-shift -->

**Definition.** A change in the relative authority, influence, or social position enacted between participants. Immediate interactional status can differ from formal rank and can reverse within a single exchange.

**Use.** Identify the moment behavior reorganizes around a new source of power. Look for altered permission, space, interruption patterns, or who seeks whose approval.

**Example.** The inspector stops asking questions when the cleaner reveals she has the only copy of the report.

## Practical distinctions

“Get him to forgive you” supplies an objective; “make him laugh” can be a tactic; “speak more quietly” is a delivery adjustment. These instructions operate at different levels and can be combined. A useful directing note connects the visible adjustment to the character's immediate circumstances.

Blocking is the arrangement of bodies in scene space. Composition is the image made from a chosen viewpoint. An actor may remain still while a moving camera transforms the composition, or walk extensively while a following camera holds roughly the same framing.

Silence, pause, and stillness should remain separate: a person may pause speaking while continuing to move, stay still while speaking rapidly, or listen silently without creating a dead interval.

## Visual interaction and structured fields

These are proposals for a future interface, not functioning controls. Preserve interpretation as text; do not invent numerical emotion or status scores.

| Decision | Visual interaction | Proposed data fields |
|---|---|---|
| Speech and implication | Script annotations beside an optional performance clip | `line_id`, `literal_text`, `subtext_note`, `speaker_id`, `intended_listener_ids` |
| Objective and tactic | Beat cards showing changes and their triggers | `beat_id`, `objective_text`, `tactic_text`, `change_trigger`, `interpretation_note` |
| Vocal timing | Annotated waveform or time ruler | `start_time_s`, `duration_s`, `overlap_with_line_id`, `pause_reason`; timings may be unknown |
| Eyeline | Target marker in a floor plan and frame preview | `actor_id`, `eyeline_target_id`, `target_height_m`, `coordinate_space` |
| Blocking | Scrubbable floor plan with start and end positions | `actor_id`, `path_points_m`, `movement_trigger`, `coordinate_space`, `position_known` |
| Group relationships | Position map linked to scene beats | `participant_ids`, `group_ids`, `relationship_note`, `status_shift_note` |
